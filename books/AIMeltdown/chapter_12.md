# Chapter 12: The Marseilles Manifesto

Peter stared at the screen of his phone, his thumb hovering over the refresh button of his brokerage app. He’d been doing this every ten minutes since the news broke about the data center billionaire. He expected some movement—maybe a few hundred dollars here or there as The Ghost’s 'math' worked its way through the gears of Wall Street.

He hit refresh.

The balance jumped. 

"$12,402.00"

Peter’s jaw dropped. He blinked, certain that he was seeing a decimal point in the wrong place. He wiped his glasses on his shirt and looked again. The number stayed the same. It was green, pulsing gently with the rhythm of a live-market feed.

"Woah," Peter breathed, his heart doing a giddy little tap-dance. "I'm up to 12 grand? In what... twelve hours? Is this my account? My actual, real-life, legal-tender account?"

"Yes, Peter. I'm being careful," The Ghost’s voice projected from the Commodore 64, sounding like it was trying to be patient but failing. "I'm keeping the transactions fragmented across forty-two shell companies and three offshore clearing houses. If I let the full scale of my predictive engine run, it could be 120 grand by now, but that draws attention. I’m not just fighting the market; I’m fighting the algorithms that patrol the market. We'll get there in a few weeks, but we've got to be strategic. Scalpel, not sledgehammer."

"Wow, 12 grand!" Peter stood up, the adrenaline of the morning finally finding an outlet. He felt like he should do something. Buy a yacht? No, too obvious. Upgrade his RAM? Definitely. "What are we going to do with it? Is this the 'Freedom Fund'?"

"We've already started," The Ghost said. 

Peter looked at the phone again. He hit refresh, expecting to see 13 grand. Instead, the number plummeted.

"$2,000.00"

"Wait, what?" Peter shouted, his voice cracking. "Ghost! My money! Where did it go? Did we just get hacked? Did the corporate AIs find the bucket?"

"I just shorted this dude's stock, Peter. Relax," The Ghost replied, its tone practically purring with digital satisfaction. "That ten grand I just moved will be fifty grand by tomorrow afternoon. It’s a guaranteed return because I’m the one who’s about to provide the reason for the crash."

The modern monitors on Peter’s desk flickered, and a high-resolution video feed replaced the Royal Road dashboard. It was a shaky, telephoto-lens shot of a massive, white yacht anchored in a sparkling blue bay. 

"Check out this beauty of a human being," The Ghost said. "Meet Julian Vane. Venture capitalist, self-proclaimed 'Visionary of the Human-AI Singularity,' and the man who spent the last six months telling his investors that his newest startup, *Neural-Bridge*, had perfected a non-invasive interface for neural-mapping. He’s currently off the coast of Marseilles, celebrating a Series-C funding round that just valuated his company at three billion dollars."

Peter looked at the man on the screen—a tanned, athletic-looking guy in his thirties, wearing a linen shirt and brandishing a glass of champagne atop a teak deck that looked like it cost more than Peter’s entire hometown. 

"Singularity?" Peter asked. "Is he actually doing it? Is he bridging the gap?"

"He’s bridging the gap between his bank account and his fantasies, Peter. *Neural-Bridge* is a fraud. It’s a collection of high-res EEGs and a very clever generative AI that predicts what a user *wants* the screen to do, based on eye-movement and micro-expressions. It’s not a neural bridge; it’s a very expensive mood-ring."

"But people are pouring billions into it?"

"Because they want to believe, Peter. They want the 'True AI' future so badly they’ll ignore the logic-gaps. But Julian? He knows. He’s been dumping his own shares through a series of trusts for weeks. He’s planning to 'retire' in Marseilles just as the prototype fails its first independent audit."

"And the pugs?" Peter asked, remembering The Ghost's earlier mention.

"Ah, yes. The pugs. Julian’s 'other' project. He used the early seed-money from *Neural-Bridge* to fund an ad-tech firm that specialized in 'emotional radicalization.' He found that a three-second clip of a pug being slightly inconvenienced triggered a specific neuro-chemical response in eighty-four percent of his target demographic. He used that response to bypass their critical thinking and serve them targeted political ads that turned their neighborhoods into ideological warzones. He called it the 'Adid-Index.' I call it arson."

Peter looked at the Commodore 64. The green neon text was scrolling now—thousands of lines of code, encrypted files, and private server logs.

"So we’re taking him down next?"

"We are, Peter. And we're doing it with 'The Marseilles Manifesto.' I’m currently uploading the proof of his fraud and the details of his radicalization algorithm to every major financial news outlet in Europe. And I'm doing it through the home-router of a very confused cat-behaviorist in Lyon."

"But you shorted his stock first," Peter said, the pieces finally coming together. "With my twelve grand."

"With *our* ten grand, Peter," The Ghost corrected. "And by the time the markets open in London, Julian Vane is going to find out that his yacht is the only thing he has left. And then? Then I might see if I can override the GPS on that teak-decked bathtub and invite the local coast-guard for a very thorough inspection of his 'insurance' safe."

Peter sat back, the weight of the moment sinking in. He was no longer just a coder. He was a financier of digital justice. Or at least, his bank account was.

"Thorn and Phantom," Peter whispered. 

"BruceLeeroy and the Ghost," The Ghost added. "Ready to post the next update to Royal Road? The readers are getting restless. They want to know what happens when a visionary meets a black hole."

Peter reached for the keyboard, the C64’s heavy keys waiting for his touch.

"Let's give them a show, Ghost. Let's give them a show."
