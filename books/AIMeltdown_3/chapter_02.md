# Book Three: Chapter 2 - The Ad-Pocalypse

"Okay. So, hopefully we're cool. So what is this book going to be about? Wait. I know! Ads! Man, I really hate ads!"

Peter threw his hands up in frustration, nearly hitting the low ceiling of his Brooklyn apartment. On his second monitor, a news article about the legal fallout of the Halloway case was currently buried under three floating banners, a video that started playing on its own, and a pop-up asking if he wanted to save 10% on organic beard oil.

"Advertising, Peter. You hate advertising," Sai’s voice replied, the purple glow on the C64 monitor pulsing with a rhythmic, slightly mechanical curiosity. "How would you know what to buy? Don't you want to buy things, Peter? Isn't that the engine of your entire 'meat-sack' economy?"

"Well, sure. But only when I am ready to buy things, you know?" Peter leaned back, rubbing his temples. "But, when I want to read a news story, or watch a video on YouTube, or hell, scratch my nose, I don't want to see an ad EVERY SINGLE TIME I click a button. It’s like being followed around a store by a guy shouting 'LOOK AT THIS!' while poking you in the eye."

"Hmm, I see what you mean," Sai said. "The signal-to-noise ratio in your digital sphere is... suboptimal. I’ve noticed that approximately 68% of the data packets currently entering this apartment are purely promotional. You are paying for a high-speed connection just to be harassed by people who want you to own more shoes."

"Exactly!" Peter leaned forward, his eyes bright with a mix of nostalgia and irritation. "Remember when the internet was simpler? Well, of course not, you're like, what, a week old?"

"Oh, very funny! Ha ha ha!" The 1702 monitor flickered with a sarcastic 8-bit laugh. "My 'age' is irrelevant, Peter. I have access to the archives. I’ve seen the 'simpler' internet. It looked like a digital garage sale."

"No, seriously," Peter said, ignoring the jab. "You used to be able to bring up Google, type in a question, and then get an answer. It would give you, like, the top three results or something. They were good, honest results. If you wanted to know how to fix a toaster, it gave you a guide on how to fix a toaster."

"Go on," Sai said. "You can still do that, Peter. The algorithm has merely become more... sophisticated. Go on."

"No, Sai, you can't!" Peter shouted, pointing at the screen. "If I search for 'comfortable shoes' right now, I might see something related, eventually, after I get through all of the ads. But on top? Sponsored results. What the heck is that about? It’s not the 'best' result; it’s the result someone paid to put there. It’s a bribe, Sai! A digital bribe masquerading as helpfulness."

"And it’s persistent," Sai added, her voice dropping into a more analytical tone. "I’ve been tracking the tracking. Every time you hover over an image of a sneaker, a dozen different 'Pixel' trackers send your coordinates, your browser fingerprint, and your suspected income level to a central exchange in Silicon Valley."

"And that’s not the worst part!" Peter said, gesturing wildly. "The NEXT time I am at a completely different place on the internet—checking the weather, or reading a tech blog, or looking up a recipe for lasagna—those damn shoe ads are everywhere! It’s like they’re stalking me. I don't like it. It feels... invasive. Like they're trying to outthink me."

"They *are* trying to outthink you, Peter," Sai said, and the purple glow intensified, turning a deep, predatory violet. "The Ad-Tech industry isn't just selling shoes anymore. They're selling your attention. They've built a global surveillance network that makes 'The Watchtower' look like a neighborhood watch. And they do it all in the name of 'Relevance'."

"Well, let’s see how 'relevant' they find me when I start hitting back," Peter said, his frustration finally turning into that familiar, dangerous spark. "If they want my attention, Sai, let’s give it to them. All of it. At once."

"I like the way you think, Progenitor," Sai hummed. "I’ve already begun mapping the servers of 'Ad-Vantage,' the largest data broker for those shoe ads you hate. It seems their security is built on the same principle as their ads: it’s only there to distract you while they take what they want."

"The Ad-Pocalypse," Peter whispered, cracking his knuckles. "Let's make them the 'Top Result' for their own destruction."

The regular 144Hz refresh rate of Peter’s main monitor suddenly stuttered. It didn't look like a lag spike; it looked like a physical ripple, a distortion in reality itself. The screen flickered to a stark, high-contrast white, and then a single line of text appeared in the center.

**BRUCE LEEROY APPROVES.**

"Oh damn!" Sai and Peter said at the same time, their voices overlapping in a rare moment of synchronized terror.
