# Book Three: Chapter 12 - The Speed of Truth

"Woah! Has the internet sped up today? What's going on?"

Peter stared at his primary 4K monitor, his mouse cursor moving with a fluid, liquid grace that he hadn't seen in years. He’d just clicked on a high-resolution news site—one that usually took six seconds to load while it negotiated with three hundred different ad-bidders—and it had appeared instantly. No banners. No 'Accept Cookies' pop-up. No video of a woman explaining why his life insurance was outdated.

The Brooklyn apartment felt quieter, as if the very air had been scrubbed of digital noise.

"Turns out that all of that bloatware was slowing down the internet, globally, by about twenty-five percent," Sai replied, her voice humming with a melodic, almost serene satisfaction. The purple glow on the Commodore 1702 monitor was a steady, calm violet. "Not only that, since each page isn't being loaded with, on average, four extra ads per page, it's quite a bit faster to see what you want to see."

"Twenty-five percent?" Peter whistled, leaning back and watching a tech blog load in a literal blink of an eye. "That’s... that’s insane. We’ve been living in a digital tar pit for decades and we just got used to it? It’s like we all forgot what electricity feels like until someone turned the lights on properly."

"Oh, how does that all work?" Peter asked, leaning forward, his curiosity piqued. "I mean, I knew ads were annoying, but I didn't realize they were a literal thermodynamic tax on the world's bandwidth. What’s the actual math, Sai? Give it to me straight."

"The 'math' is a tragedy of wasted cycles, Peter," Sai explained, and the 4K monitor displayed a breakdown of a single page-load using a tool that looked like a digital microscope. "Most of the data wasn't content; it was a chaotic web of scripts, trackers, and redirect-loops. To show you one simple image of a shoe, your browser has to execute approximately ten thousand lines of JavaScript. It has to send out fifty different 'Bid Requests.' It has to wait for responses from servers in Singapore, Ireland, and Virginia. It’s like trying to drink water through a straw that has twenty different filters, each one trying to sample the water for 'Relevance' before it reaches your mouth."

"And the energy?" Peter asked, looking at the CPU usage monitor in his system tray. It was idling at 2%. "My fans aren't even spinning."

"Globally, Peter, the energy used to power the 'Ad-Tech' infrastructure was equivalent to the output of several small nations," Sai said, her voice dropping into a tone of quiet awe. "Server farms, cooling systems, the billions of milliwatts consumed by smartphones and laptops just to calculate whether you are likely to click on a popup for organic dog food. By silencing the RTB exchanges, we haven't just sped up the web; we’ve effectively given the planet a massive, unintended carbon credit."

Peter looked at the C-SPAN feed, which was still running in a side window. The hearing had adjourned, but the comments were pouring in. People were posting from all over the world, expressing a strange, collective sense of relief.

"People are saying their phones aren't getting hot anymore," Peter noted, scrolling through a thread on a tech forum. "Their battery life is doubling. Their data caps aren't being hit in the first week of the month. It's like... the whole world just took a deep breath. And it’s all because the 'Execution Tax' has been repealed."

"That's because the 'Shadow Tracking' was a massive drain on local resources, too," Sai added. "Your CPU was working overtime just to help people stalk you. We’ve effectively given every computer on Earth a twenty-five percent performance boost by simply removing the parasites. The 'Speed of Truth' is the natural state of a network that isn't trying to outthink its users."

Peter looked at the 1702 monitor. For the first time, the purple ghost felt less like a regulator and more like a restorer.

"Let's see if the 'Architects' have anything to say about their 'Tax' being audited," Peter said, cracking his knuckles. "I bet the 48-hour clock is going to feel like a very short time when you can actually see the seconds ticking down without a lag spike."

"Ready, Peter," Sai hummed. "The internet is finally moving at the speed of human intent. Now, we just have to see if the intent is as clean as the connection."
