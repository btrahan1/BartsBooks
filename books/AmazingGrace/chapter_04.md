## Chapter 4

Wednesday afternoons at the Havenwood Public Library were Mary's
favorite. The after-school rush of children had subsided, and the
pre-dinner lull had settled in, leaving only the quiet hum of the
fluorescent lights and the gentle rustle of turning pages. This was when
she did her best work: cataloging.

She was seated at a heavy oak table in the back room, surrounded by
cardboard boxes that smelled of dust and time. The boxes contained the
archives of the Havenwood Historical Society, a disorganized collection
of letters, ledgers, and photographs that the library had recently
agreed to house. Her task was to bring order to this chaos, and she
approached it with a quiet, methodical joy.

Her fingers, so expressive and fluid on the piano keys, were just as
deft here. She handled a brittle, sepia-toned photograph with reverence,
her touch light as she slipped it into an acid-free sleeve. She
deciphered the elegant, looping cursive of a hundred-year-old letter,
summarizing its contents onto a small index card with her neat, precise
handwriting. This was a different kind of music---the music of a town's
history---and she was its careful arranger.

The small bell above the library's main door jingled, a sound so
familiar she didn't look up. It would be Mrs.Â Gable returning her
mystery novels or Mr.Â Abernathy looking for a new Western.

But the footsteps that followed were different. They weren't the soft
shuffle of sneakers or the slow tread of worn loafers. They were crisp,
sharp, and confident, the sound of expensive leather on polished
linoleum. They moved with a city pace, a rhythm of urgency that felt
alien in the sleepy afternoon quiet.

Mary finished labeling a photograph of the town's founding mayor and
carried a small stack of books out to the main desk. Standing in the
small, seldom-visited musicology section was the man from the church.

He looked just as out of place here as he had in the pew. His dark suit
was immaculate, his posture ramrod straight as he scanned the spines of
the books. He seemed less to be browsing and more to be assessing, as if
calculating the collection's market value. He hadn't seen her yet.

Mary took her place behind the high circulation desk, the solid wood a
familiar comfort. For two days, she'd heard the whispers. "Did you see
that fella in the back on Sunday?" "Must be from the city, looked like
he had money." "He asked Agnes at the diner who played the piano at
First Presbyterian." Agnes, a notorious gossip, had been happy to gush.
"Our Mary Steepleton," she'd apparently declared. "Taught by her mother
before her. An angel at the keys, that one."

The man pulled a book from the shelf, a slim volume on the history of
American hymnody. He flipped through it for a moment before snapping it
shut and placing it back with a decisive click. He turned, and his eyes
met Mary's. A flicker of recognition crossed his face, followed by a
smooth, professional smile. He approached the desk.

"Good afternoon," he said. His voice was like his footsteps: crisp and
clear, with no trace of a local accent.

"Good afternoon," Mary replied politely. "Can I help you find
something?"

"Perhaps. I'm doing a bit of research." He gestured back toward the
music section. "I'm interested in the history of local music. Folk
traditions, church choirs, that sort of thing. Is there a section for
that?"

Mary blinked, a little surprised by the specific request. "Well, most of
what we have is in the general musicology section you were just in.
But," she paused, her librarian instincts taking over, "we do have some
local choir recordings in the archives. The First Presbyterian Christmas
Cantata from 1988 is a local favorite. And some of the older town
histories mention the founding of the church choir."

She was being helpful, professional. She saw a patron with a research
query.

He leaned forward slightly, resting his hands on the polished surface of
the desk. His gaze was intense. "I was at the service on Sunday. At
First Presbyterian."

"Oh," Mary said, a little flustered. "I hope you enjoyed your visit."

"The music was... impressive," he said, and the word choice hung in the
air. It wasn't "beautiful" or "lovely," the words she was used to
hearing. "Impressive" sounded analytical, like a grade. "The
arrangements, especially. They were quite sophisticated for a small
parish."

Mary felt the familiar blush creep up her neck. She offered her
practiced, gentle smile. "Oh, it's just Church Music," she said, the
words a shield of humility. "Pastor Dave likes the old hymns. I just try
to make them sound nice for everyone."

He watched her, his expression unreadable. It was as if she'd spoken in
a foreign language he was trying to translate. The simplicity of her
answer did not seem to align with the complexity of what he had heard.

"Have you always been the pianist there?" he asked, his tone shifting
from analytical to personal.

"Since I was a teenager, officially," she said. "My mother, Sarah,
played before me. I learned from her."

"I see." He stood up straight, pulling a wallet from his jacket. But
instead of money, he extracted a slim, cream-colored business card. He
slid it across the desk. It was heavy, expensive cardstock.

**Adrian Cross** **A&R, Vanguard Artist Group**

Mary looked down at the unfamiliar letters. A&R? Vanguard? They meant
nothing to her.

"My name is Adrian Cross," he said. "I'm a talent scout. What you do,
Ms.Â Steepleton, is not 'just Church Music.' Not by a long shot." He
tapped a finger on the card. "I'm staying at the inn for a few more
days. I would very much like to have a proper conversation with you
about your future."

Mary stared at the card, then back up at his intense, expectant face.
The quiet, orderly world of her Wednesday afternoon had just been
invaded. Her heart gave a sudden, unfamiliar lurch, a dissonant chord
struck in the peaceful music of her life.
