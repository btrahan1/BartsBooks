Chapter 6

Adrian Cross returned to the front pew and sat down. The smile on his
face was entirely different from the one he had arrived with. The
salesman's slick, practiced grin was gone, replaced by something
genuine, energized, and bordering on awe. He hadn't felt the thrill of
pure, unadulterated discovery like this in a very long time, if ever.
This wasn't about finding a marketable commodity anymore; it was about
witnessing something real.

A moment later, Pastor Dave emerged from his office, his expression
serene. He walked to the edge of the chancel, leaned over the low
railing, and whispered to Mary, his voice too low for Adrian to hear. He
gave her a reassuring nod and a kind smile, then turned and walked
quietly out a side door, leaving them alone again. The gesture was
clear: *I have done my part. The rest is in your hands. I trust you.*

A soft chuckle escaped Adrian. "My parents were not subtle people."

"Pastor Dave just spoke very highly of you," she continued, her eyes on
her hands. The music she was playing was gentle, a simple foundation for
her words. "He said you were a man of surprising character. That is not
something to ignore, so I won't." She let a final chord hang in the air.
"I assume you have some sheet music with you?"

Adrian, feeling as if he were part of a ritual he didn't fully
understand but was desperate to see through, reached into his briefcase.
He hadn't expected this. He had brought the sheet music as a
contingency, a technical test in case her improvisational skills were
her only trick. It wasn't a hymn. It wasn't a pop song arrangement. It
was a dense, contemporary classical piece by a notoriously difficult
avant-garde composer, a thing of jagged rhythms and dissonant, complex
harmonies titled "Reflections in a Shard of Glass." It was a piece
designed to showcase technical brilliance, and he had seen Juilliard
graduates sweat through it. It was, in his professional opinion,
musically brilliant but emotionally sterile.

He walked to the piano and handed the sheaf of papers to her.

Mary took it without a word. She didn't place it on the piano's music
stand. She held it in her lap, her head bowed. The church fell into a
profound silence, broken only by the distant tick of a clock in the
narthex.

Adrian returned to his pew and watched. She did not make a sound for the
first five minutes, her stillness absolute. She was simply reading it,
absorbing it. He saw her eyes moving back and forth, not just note by
note, but phrase by phrase, system by system, absorbing the structure,
the architecture of the piece.

Then, she began to hum. It was barely audible, just a soft, low
vibration in her chest. She wasn't humming the melody---there was barely
one to be found---but rather the feeling of the chords, testing their
dissonant relationships in the air. Her right index finger rose and
traced the shape of a particularly difficult arpeggio in the air above
the page, a ghost of a performance. Adrian felt a shiver go down his
spine. She wasn't just sight-reading; she was internalizing it,
translating the cold, black-and-white ink into her own native language
of sound and emotion.

The fifteen minutes felt both like an eternity and an instant. Finally,
she took a slow, deep breath and looked up. Her eyes, clear and direct,
met his across the empty space.

"Would you like me to play it as is," she asked, her voice even and
calm, "or as I hear it?"

Adrian Cross, a man who had negotiated multi-million dollar contracts
with rock stars and soothed the fragile egos of opera divas, a man who
always, *always* had the right words, was rendered utterly speechless.

The question was the most profound artistic statement he had ever heard.
"As is" was the test he had brought for her---a demonstration of pure
technical skill, a soulless reproduction of the page. "As I hear it" was
an offer of something else entirely. It was an offer of interpretation,
of artistry, of her soul. It was the difference between a photograph and
a painting.

He had come to Havenwood to find a talent. He had just been offered a
masterpiece.

After a long moment, he finally found his voice, and it was little more
than a whisper.
