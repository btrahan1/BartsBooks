Chapter 14

Julian sighed, a long, weary sound that was the polar opposite of
Adrian's impassioned zeal. He swiveled in his chair, turning to face the
panoramic view of the city as if the answer lay somewhere in the grid of
streets below.

"Well, Adrian, it's not up to me," he said, his voice returning to its
familiar, pragmatic tone. "Even if I believed you---and your little
outburst has me leaning that way---I don't have the power to just hand
you the keys to Carnegie Hall. She will need to audition. She will need
to convince 'them' that she can play."

He said the word 'them' with a kind of grim reverence. 'Them' was the
Carnegie selection committee. THE committee. A shadowy cabal of old
money, musical aristocrats, and legendary performers who were the
absolute gatekeepers of the classical world. They were notoriously
insular, famously unimpressed by hype, and ruthlessly protective of the
stage's legacy. Even getting to play in front of them, in a formal
audition, was an incredible privilege, a feat in and of itself.

Julian swiveled back around, a new, calculating glint in his eye. The
CEO was back in control. "But... I have a plan," he said, and laid it
out.

His plan was a delicate piece of corporate espionage, disguised as a
casual lunch. One of the key members of the committee was a man named
Arthur Pendleton. They weren't exactly friends, but they were respectful
colleagues who moved in the same circles. Julian would arrange for
Adrian to have lunch with him the following week at The Galleria, a
notoriously high-end mall.

"In the atrium of that mall," Julian explained, "there is a Fazioli
grand piano. The store puts it there for publicity. Anyone can play it."

The pieces clicked into place in Adrian's mind, a plan of beautiful,
terrifying simplicity.

"You'll invite him to lunch at the cafe right next to it," Julian
continued. "You'll talk about portfolios, market trends, boring things.
While you're having a friendly, unsuspecting lunch, your pianist will
walk up to that piano and begin to play."

It was low risk. There was no formal request to deny, no reputation on
the line. And it was the ultimate test of Adrian's word. Julian's final
instruction was the master stroke.

"You won't even tell him. You don't say a word. You act as surprised as
he is. We just let the chips fall where they may."

The following Tuesday, Adrian felt like he was walking to his own
execution. The Galleria's atrium was a cathedral of commerce, a
three-story marble and glass temple to consumerism. The air hummed with
the noise of a thousand conversations, the clatter of the food court,
and the tinny pop music piped in from unseen speakers. In the center of
it all, gleaming under a massive skylight, sat the Fazioli grand piano.
It looked magnificent and entirely out of place.

Mary was already there, sitting on a bench a hundred feet away, trying
to look inconspicuous. She looked like a librarian from Havenwood who
had taken a wrong turn on a bus tour. She was clutching her canvas tote
bag to her chest like a life raft, her eyes wide as she took in the
chaotic scene. Adrian had explained the plan to her, and she had hated
every part of it that involved subterfuge. What had convinced her was
one simple sentence: "Mary, this is the only way to get a seat at the
table to talk about the roof."

Arthur Pendleton arrived, a tall, impeccably dressed man in his late
sixties with an air of bored authority. They sat at a small table, and
Adrian ordered coffee, his hand shaking so badly he nearly spilled it.

"So, Adrian," Arthur began, his tone condescendingly cordial. "Julian
tells me you have a new project you're excited about."

"Yes, I..." Adrian started, his throat suddenly dry. His eyes kept
darting toward Mary.

And then, he saw her stand up. She walked with a quiet determination
toward the piano. A security guard started to move toward her, then saw
the unassuming look on her face and seemed to decide she wasn't a
threat.

"Is everything all right, Adrian? You seem distracted," Arthur said, a
hint of annoyance in his voice.

Mary sat down at the piano. She rested her hands on the keys for a
moment, took a deep breath, and closed her eyes, shutting out the mall,
the noise, the entire world.

And then she began to play.

It was "How Great Thou Art."

The first notes were simple, clear, and pure. They cut through the
atrium's din like a beam of light. A few shoppers slowed their pace.

Arthur Pendleton paused, his fork halfway to his mouth. "Well, that's a
bit more pleasant than the usual rubbish they play in here," he
muttered.

By the second verse, Mary's left hand was weaving a complex, gorgeous
counter-melody. The sound from the Fazioli was breathtaking, richer and
more resonant than the old Baldwin, and she used its full power, her
playing swelling to fill the enormous space. The clatter from the food
court began to subside. Teenagers stopped scrolling on their phones and
looked up. A mother with a crying toddler found him suddenly silent, his
little head cocked, listening.

Adrian said nothing. He just watched Arthur's face. The initial
annoyance had melted into professional curiosity.

By the third verse, the music was a tidal wave of glorious, soaring
sound. Mary was no longer just playing a hymn; she was testifying. She
was pouring every ounce of her faith---in her music, in her God, in her
purpose---into the keys.

The atrium had fallen almost completely silent. A crowd was forming, a
circle of mesmerized shoppers and store clerks standing twenty feet from
the piano, their faces filled with wonder.

Arthur Pendleton had forgotten his lunch entirely. He was leaning
forward, his elbows on the table, his gaze locked on the unassuming
woman at the piano. His jaw was slack. The mask of bored aristocracy was
gone. He was just a man, listening to music that was fundamentally
changing the air he was breathing.

The final verse was a whisper, a gentle, humble return to the simple
melody, before the final, triumphant chord echoed through the marble
hall.

For a beat, there was absolute silence.

Then, the atrium erupted. It wasn't polite applause; it was a
spontaneous, heartfelt ovation from a hundred strangers who had just
shared a moment of unexpected grace.

Mary looked up, startled by the noise, a blush rising on her cheeks. She
gave a small, shy nod, and quickly slipped away from the piano,
disappearing back into the crowd.

Adrian finally let out the breath he'd been holding. He looked at
Arthur.

Arthur Pendleton slowly, deliberately, placed his napkin on the table.
He turned to Adrian, his eyes narrowed, not with anger, but with the
sharp, assessing gaze of a connoisseur who knows he has just been
played, but has witnessed something priceless in the process.

He leaned in close, his voice a low, dangerous whisper.

"Don't you ever, *ever* insult my intelligence like this again, Adrian."

Adrian's heart plummeted.
