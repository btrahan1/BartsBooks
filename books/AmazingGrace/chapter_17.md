## Chapter 17

The sound of Arthur Pendleton's solitary, deliberate clapping echoed in
the room. It was not the thunderous applause of a crowd but something
far more potent: a single expert's acknowledgment of another. The other
four committee members remained motionless, their expressions a mixture
of shock, confusion, and something akin to intellectual outrage. They
had come to judge a technical performance and had instead been subjected
to a deeply personal emotional experience.

The clapping stopped. Arthur rested his hands on his lap, a small,
satisfied smile on his face, content to let his colleagues wrestle with
what they had just heard.

Finally, a woman with severe silver hair and a string of pearls spoke.
Her name was Eleanor Vance, a renowned musicologist and a notorious
purist. She had not been clapping.

"Why did you play it like that?" she asked. The question was sharp,
analytical, stripped of all emotion. It wasn't a request for an artistic
explanation; it was a demand for a justification. She was asking why
Mary had dared to deviate from the sacred text of the composer's score.

Mary, still seated at the piano, blinked. The question genuinely puzzled
her. She wasn't being coy or defiant. In her world, music wasn't a set
of instructions to be followed; it was a language to be spoken. One
didn't speak a language "wrong," one simply spoke it with one's own
voice.

She thought for a few seconds, trying to find the words to explain a
process that was as natural to her as breathing. She looked from Eleanor
Vance's severe face to the warm, supportive faces of her friends and
neighbors pressed against the glass of the observation booth. She knew,
with absolute certainty, for whom she had been playing.

"It's... just how I hear it," she answered, her voice soft but clear,
carrying easily in the hall's perfect acoustics. She looked at the
committee with genuine, guileless curiosity. "Did I play it wrong?"

The question, so simple and sincere, completely disarmed them. It was a
checkmate they had never anticipated. They were prepared to deconstruct
her technique, to critique her interpretation, to challenge her artistic
choices. They were not prepared for a performer who didn't seem to
believe that artistic choices were choices at all, but rather an
inevitability.

Another committee member, a stooped man with a wispy goatee, cleared his
throat. "Ms.Â Steepleton, the composer's notations are quite specific.
The opening is marked *aggressivo e forte*. Your interpretation was...

"I know," Mary said with a simple nod. "It sounded so angry on the page.
I didn't think it needed to be. The notes are already sad enough on
their own. They don't need to shout."

This was too much for Eleanor Vance. "Music is not about what you
*think* it needs, young woman! It is about honoring the composer's
intent!"

Before Mary could respond, Arthur Pendleton spoke, his voice calm and
authoritative. "Eleanor, let's be honest. The composer, in this case, is
a pretentious nihilist who writes music designed to be admired, not

An assistant walked onto the stage from the wings, carrying a single,
bound folio of sheet music. He placed it on the piano's stand with the
gravity of a man delivering a death sentence.

Mary looked at it. It was exactly as Adrian had predicted: dense,
modern, and fiendishly complex. It was a musical obstacle course, a
thing of ink and paper designed to trip up and expose any flaw in a
performer's technique.

She looked at the first few bars, her brow furrowed in concentration.
She hummed a small, questioning phrase. Then she looked up at the
committee, her expression open and honest.

"I'm going to need a minute with this one."

And for the next fifteen minutes, in the most important audition of her
life, in a multi-million-dollar concert hall in front of the most
powerful people in her field, Mary Steepleton sat in complete quiet and
simply read the music, her head bowed, just as she had done in the
sanctuary of her home church. The committee could do nothing but wait.
