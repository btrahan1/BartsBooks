## Chapter 2

The final, resonant "Amen" from Pastor Dave hung in the air for a moment
before dissolving into the comfortable bustle of a congregation
released. The sounds of shuffling feet on the heart pine floors,
whispered greetings, and the low rumble of men clearing their throats
filled the sanctuary. Mary gathered her well-worn music books---more out
of habit than necessity---and placed them neatly in her canvas tote bag.
By the time she stood up, Pastor Dave was already at the main doors, his
broad, kind face ready for the weekly receiving line.

Mary took her customary place, a half-step behind his right shoulder. It
was a position she had occupied for nearly twenty years, ever since her
mother's hands had grown too stiff with arthritis. She was a fixture, as
much a part of the church's architecture as the worn velvet cushions on
the pews.

The first few families filed past with polite nods. "Good word, Pastor."
"See you at the potluck, Mary." She offered a quiet, genuine smile to
each. She had played for most of their weddings, their baptisms, and,
for some, their parents' funerals. Her music was the soundtrack to their
lives, a fact she never consciously considered.

Then came Eleanor Williams, a woman whose floral hats were as much a
Sunday institution as the sermon itself. She clasped Pastor Dave's hand
with both of hers.

"Thank you, Pastor, a beautiful sermon. Truly, the spirit was moving
today."

"The spirit does the moving, Eleanor, I just try to keep up," he
chuckled.

Eleanor then turned her full, radiant attention to Mary. Her eyes,
magnified by her spectacles, were bright with emotion. "And you," she
said, taking Mary's hands in her own dry, warm ones. "Mary, dear. That
was so beautiful! That last verse of 'Amazing Grace'... it gave me
goosebumps right up my arms. You have real talent, girl."

A faint blush rose on Mary's cheeks. She gave a small, almost apologetic
smile and a gentle squeeze of Eleanor's hands. "Oh, it's just Church
Music, Ms.Â Williams," she said, her voice soft. "But I am so glad you
enjoyed it."

It was her standard reply, delivered with an honesty that was utterly
disarming. In her mind, there was "Music"---the complex, intimidating,
brilliant stuff performed by professionals in concert halls---and then
there was "Church Music," which was simply a utility. It was a tool,
like the furnace that warmed the building or the coffee maker that
brewed for fellowship. It served a purpose, and she was the one who knew
how to operate it. The idea that her "operation" of it was a form of
artistry was a concept that simply didn't compute.

Ms.Â Williams patted her hand one last time, beaming. "Well, whatever it
is, it's a gift. Don't you let anyone tell you different." She bustled
off toward the fellowship hall, no doubt making a beeline for the pecan
sandies.

As the line of departing worshippers began to thin, Pastor Dave turned
his head slightly, just enough to catch Mary's eye. He had witnessed
that exact exchange, or a variation of it, hundreds of times. He saw the
truth that Mary herself was blind to. He was a decent preacher, he knew
that. He crafted his sermons with care, prayed on his words, and spoke
from the heart. But he also knew, with a certainty that humbled him
every single Sunday, that his words were just ink on a page until Mary's
music gave them color and flight. She didn't just accompany him; she
elevated him. Her instinctive swells and fades, the way she could
conjure a mood of contemplative stillness or soaring hope from that old
Baldwin, turned his rather mundane oratory into something that felt, as
Ms.Â Williams had said, like the spirit moving.

He waited for the last family to pass through the doors, a young couple
with a squirming toddler. Then, in the momentary quiet of the empty
narthex, he leaned in close to Mary.

"God Bless You, Mary," he whispered, the words thick with a gratitude he
could never fully express.

Mary looked up at him, her smile simple and serene. "You too, Pastor,"
she said, believing he was just thanking her for doing her job. She gave
a final glance around the empty sanctuary, then looked past him, out the
open doors to the sunlit street. The stranger in the nice suit from the
back pew was nowhere to be seen. Odd, she thought for a fleet moment.
Most visitors at least stayed for a cup of coffee.
