## Chapter 3

The drive from the church to the small house she'd inherited from her
mother was less than five minutes, but it was a journey Mary cherished.
The potluck had been its usual warm and chaotic affair, a symphony of
casserole dishes clanking, children shrieking with laughter, and the low
hum of a community catching up with itself. Mary had eaten a slice of
ham and two different kinds of potato salad before excusing herself,
feeling the familiar need for the quiet that always followed the joyful
noise of Sunday.

She had never married. There was no tragic story of a lost love or a
broken heart. Men had expressed interest over the years, certainly. She
was pleasant to look at, with kind eyes and a smile that reached them,
but she carried an air of serene self-containment that many found
beautiful but impenetrable. The truth was simpler: her life felt full
already. Between her part-time job at the town library, tending her
mother's garden, and the music that was the bedrock of her existence,
she never felt a void that needed filling.

She let herself into the silent house, the air smelling faintly of lemon
polish and old books. The centerpiece of her small living room, even
more so than the comfortable floral sofa, was the piano. It was a
generation older than the church's Baldwin, a dark mahogany spinet with
ivory keys worn smooth and concave by her mother's hands, and then by
her own.

She ran a hand over its closed lid, the wood cool beneath her palm.
Ms.Â Williams' words echoed in her mind. *"You have real talent, girl."*
It was meant as the highest compliment, but to Mary, it always felt like
a misunderstanding, like someone praising a hammer for being a good
screwdriver.

The memory rose in her as it often did when someone used the word
"talent." It was vivid, painted in the bright, oversaturated colors of
early childhood.

She was four, and the world was measured from the height of her mother's
lap. Her mother, Sarah Steepleton, would be practicing for Sunday
service, and Mary would be nestled in the warm space between her arms.
The piano was a magical creature then, a wooden giant with a hundred
smiling teeth that sang when you touched it. Her mother's hands were
mesmerizing, dancing over the keys while her low, steady voice hummed
the melodies of "What a Friend We Have in Jesus" and "The Old Rugged
Cross." Mary's own chubby fingers would reach out, tapping the keys,
making clumsy, happy sounds that her mother would effortlessly
incorporate into the harmony. For Mary, this wasn't learning; it was
love made audible.

By the time she was six, her small hands seemed to know the keyboard as
well as her mother's. A visiting piano teacher, Mrs.Â Albright, heard her
playing through an open window one afternoon and insisted she be entered
into the county-wide youth recital.

Her mother had been hesitant. "It's just our music, Helen," she'd said.

"Sarah, it's a gift from the Lord," Mrs.Â Albright had insisted. "It's
not right to hide it."

So Mary went. She sat on the vast stage, her little feet dangling high
above the pedals, and waited while other children played stilted,
carefully memorized renditions of "FÃ¼r Elise" and "Minuet in G." When
her name was called, she walked to the gleaming black grand piano, a
terrifying monster compared to her familiar spinet. She played "Jesus
Loves Me." She didn't just play the simple melody; she played it the way
her mother did, with little runs and grace notes, making the chords
richer and fuller, her eyes closed just as she always did on her
mother's lap. She won the blue ribbon. People clapped and called her a
prodigy. She just felt confused. She had just played a song she loved.
What was there to win?

The next year, there was another recital, and another blue ribbon. This
time for a rendition of "How Great Thou Art" that had left several of
the judges dabbing at the corners of their eyes with their
handkerchiefs. Mrs.Â Albright was beaming from the front row, but Mary,
from her perch on the piano bench, saw something else.

She saw Penelope Vance, the girl who had played right before her.
Penelope had performed a frantic, complicated piece full of fast scales
that sounded to Mary like a mouse scurrying across the keys. All the
adults had whispered that Penelope was technically brilliant and took
lessons in the city. Now, Penelope was sitting next to her mother, her
face blotchy and red, her lower lip trembling as she stared daggers at
Mary.

When the head judge announced Mary's name for the blue ribbon again, the
applause felt different. It was loud, but it didn't feel happy. It felt
heavy. As Mary walked forward to accept the ribbon, she had to pass
Penelope's row. She heard the girl's mother hiss, "It's favoritism, is
what it is. That hymn nonsense always gets the old biddies crying."

The ribbon didn't feel special this time. It felt cold and sharp in her
small hand.

The next year, when Mary was seven, Mrs.Â Albright signed her up for the
regional competition. The stakes were higher, the children were more
serious, and the air in the recital hall was thick with nervous
ambition. Mary was slated to play last. Her piece was "It Is Well with
My Soul," a hymn her mother had taught her after her own grandfather had
passed. For Mary, the song was the feeling of her mother's arms around
her, a promise that even when things were sad, there was peace to be
found.

She sat in the audience, waiting her turn, listening to the other
children. They were incredible. Their fingers flew across the keys,
playing music that was complex and dazzling. But Mary noticed that none
of them closed their eyes. They stared intently at their sheet music or
their hands, their faces tight with concentration. They were trying
very, very hard to win.

Then it was her turn. She walked onto the stage, adjusted the bench, and
looked out at the dark, faceless audience. She took a deep breath, just
as her mother had taught her, and closed her eyes. She wasn't in a
recital hall anymore. She was in the little sanctuary of First
Presbyterian, with the sun coming through the windows. She began to
play. She played the story of the hymn---the sorrow of the opening
chords, the quiet strength of the verses, the soaring, resilient hope of
the chorus.

When she finished and the final note faded, there was a beat of absolute
stillness. Then, the applause erupted, louder than she had ever heard
it. When she opened her eyes, she saw that the head judge, a
stern-looking woman with a tight bun, was openly weeping.

Backstage, her mother hugged her tightly. "You were wonderful, darling."

But Mary had seen the other children's faces as she walked off stage.
The jealousy was no longer hidden. It was stark and ugly. They looked at
her as if she had cheated, as if her genuine emotion was a trick she had
used to beat them.

On the car ride home, the large, ornate trophy sitting between them on
the front seat, Mary was silent. The setting sun cast long shadows
across the fields.

"What's wrong, sweet pea?" her mother asked, her voice gentle. "You won.
You should be proud."

Mary traced a pattern on the dusty dashboard with her finger. "Mommy,"
she said, her voice small. "When you play at church, is it a
competition?"

Her mother looked surprised. "Gracious, no. Of course not. It's for
worship. It's for the congregation."

"To make them feel better?"

"Yes," her mother said. "To help them feel closer to God. Or just... to
give them a moment of peace."

Mary looked at the trophy gleaming in the fading light. "Today... I
don't think I made those other kids feel better. I think I made them
feel bad." She looked at her mother, her seven-year-old face etched with
a profound and sudden understanding. "It doesn't feel right. Competing
with Church Music. It's the only music I really know, and... I think
we're using it wrong."

Sarah Steepleton pulled the car over to the side of the quiet country
road. She turned and looked at her daughter, truly looked at her, and
saw a wisdom that was far older than her years. She saw a tiny piece of
her own soul reflected back at her.

"You know," Sarah said softly, reaching out to brush a stray hair from
Mary's forehead. "I think you might be the smartest person I've ever
met."

"I don't want to do it anymore," Mary said, the words a quiet
declaration. "The competitions. I just want to play at church. With
you."

Her mother smiled, a slow, radiant smile of pure love and relief. "Then
that's what we'll do," she promised.

And they did. Mary Steepleton never entered another piano competition.
The trophies were put in a box in the attic, and her music went back to
where she knew it belonged: not on a stage, but in the heart of a
small-town church, offered up freely, with no thought of a prize.
