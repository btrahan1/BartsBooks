## Chapter 13

The office of Julian Vance, CEO of Vanguard Artist Group, was a world
away from the Havenwood church. It was a glass-walled box on the 48th
floor, overlooking a sprawling, indifferent cityscape. The furniture was
minimalist and brutally expensive. The air was chilled to a precise 68
degrees. It was a room designed for commerce, not for miracles.

Adrian stood before the massive, polished granite desk, his heart still
thrumming with the quiet energy of the church. He had driven back to the
city in a daze, the sound of Mary's playing echoing in his mind, the
simple, profound fairness of their deal clutched in his hand like a
sacred text.

"Let me get this straight," Julian said, leaning back in his leather
throne. He was a man who measured the world in quarterly reports and
market penetration. He peered at Adrian over a pair of ridiculously
stylish reading glasses. "You found an unknown amateur pianist in a
church in... where is this place? Nowhere?"

"Havenwood," Adrian said, his voice tight.

"Havenwood," Julian repeated, the name tasting like sawdust in his
mouth. "And you want us, Vanguard Artist Group, to finance a
one-night-only debut concert for her at the most expensive, most
prestigious, most impossible-to-book venue in North America."

"Yes," Adrian said.

Julian let out a short, incredulous laugh. "Carnegie Hall! Are you
nuts!"

"Julian, if you could just hear her..."

"Hear her? Adrian, I don't need to hear her!" Julian said, his voice
rising. "We can't just 'book' Carnegie Hall for a concert! It takes
months, years! You need a hook, a story, a pre-existing fan base! We'd
be laughed out of the industry!" He slammed a folder down on his desk
for emphasis. "And for who? A Hick from the Sticks!?"

The air in the room seemed to crystallize. The casual, cruel phrase,
spoken without a second thought, landed like a physical blow.

Adrian slammed his fist on the granite desk. The sound was like a
gunshot in the sterile office. He stood up so fast his chair scraped
violently against the floor. He was shaking, a tremor of pure,
protective rage running through him.

He pointed a trembling finger at his boss. His voice was low, guttural,
and shaking with a fury Julian had never, in fifteen years, imagined the
man possessed.

"Don't. You. EVER. Call her that."

Julian flinched, his condescending smirk wiped clean from his face.

"EVER!" Adrian roared, his voice cracking with the strain. "She has more
integrity, more soul, more goddamn *music* in one finger than you have
in this entire building! You don't even DESERVE to hear her play!"

His chest was heaving. The silence that followed was absolute, broken
only by the faint hum of the city far below. Adrian stood there, panting
slightly, his finger still pointing, his eyes blazing with a righteous
fire his boss had never seen. Julian Vance, a man who had stared down
rock stars and crushed rival executives, was shocked into a state of
stunned, motionless silence. He looked at his best A&R man, his most
reliable, level-headed employee, and saw a complete stranger, a zealot,
a true believer.

A full minute passed. The tension in the room was a living thing.

Finally, Julian slowly took off his glasses and placed them on the desk.
All the theatrics were gone. He looked at Adrian like he was trying to
solve a very complicated math problem.

"You're serious," Julian said. It wasn't a question. "You're risking
your career. You're risking *my* reputation. For a church pianist."

"I am," Adrian said, not backing down an inch.

Julian stared at him for a long time. He picked up his phone, tapped a
few keys, and then set it back down face up.

"I just checked the calendar," he said, his voice terrifyingly neutral.
"There's a cancellation at the Hall in three weeks. A Russian orchestra
lost their funding."

He looked back up at Adrian. "If we book it, and she bombs... you're
done. You know that? You're out. No severance, no recommendation."

"Register it," Adrian said. "Charge the deposit to my personal card if
you have to."

Julian leaned back, a small, cold smile touching his lips. "I don't need
your money, Adrian. I need your judgment. And right now, I'm terrified
yours has been compromised by... sentiment." He stood up and walked to
the window. "But I've never known you to be suicidal."

He turned back. "Is she really that good?"

Adrian sank back into his chair, the adrenaline draining away, leaving
him exhausted.

"Good isn't the right word, Julian," he said quietly. "She's not 'good.'
She's... necessary."
