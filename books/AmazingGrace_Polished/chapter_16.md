## Chapter 16

The audition room was not a room at all; it was a small, acoustically
perfect concert hall nestled deep within the Carnegie building. It was a
space that radiated history and judgment. The air was heavy with the
ghosts of a hundred years of musical genius.

As Mary walked from the wings toward the magnificent Steinway grand
piano in the center of the stage, to say she was nervous was an
understatement. Every fiber of her being screamed that she did not
belong here. The stage lights were blinding, the polished wood floor
reflected her image like a dark mirror, and the quiet from the handful
of people in the theater seats felt like a physical pressure. This was
the recital stage of her childhood nightmares, magnified a thousand
times over. Her hands were cold, her stomach a tight knot of dread.

Then, she looked at 'the Committee.' They were seated in the center of
the third row. Four of them were exactly as she had pictured: stern,
older, their faces etched with the bored, unimpressed expressions of
people who had heard everything and been moved by nothing. They held
clipboards. But the fifth face, Arthur Pendleton's, was different. He
was not bored. He was leaning forward slightly, his expression one of
keen, focused anticipation. He was the one who had opened this door, and
he was waiting to see if he had been right.

Her eyes drifted past them, toward a glass-walled observation booth at
the back of the hall. She expected to see Adrian, maybe Julian.

She saw the entire church.

It wasn't literally every single member, but it felt like it. Pastor
Dave was there, his kind face pressed against the glass, a wide, proud
smile splitting his face. Martha Gable, the choir director, stood beside
him, clutching a tissue in her hand already. She saw Mr.Â Abernathy,
Ms.Â Williams in a truly spectacular floral hat, and the young couple
with the toddler, who was being bounced on his father's hip. They had
chartered a bus, a whole bus, and driven six hours through the night to
be here for her. They weren't an audience; they were an anchor. They
were home.

A slow, radiant smile spread across Mary's face. She lifted a hand and
gave a small, simple wave to the booth. A flurry of excited waves came
back through the glass. And in that instant, the fear vanished. It
didn't just recede; it evaporated completely, burned away by the warmth
of their presence.

This wasn't a recital hall anymore. It was her sanctuary. This wasn't an
audition. It was a service. And she knew exactly what to do.

She immediately relaxed. Her shoulders, which had been up around her
ears, dropped. The knot in her stomach untied itself. She walked the
rest of the way to the piano not as a terrified contestant, but as a
woman approaching her sacred work.

She reached the piano and turned. First, she gave a slight, respectful
bow to the small, scattered audience of industry insiders and assistants
Adrian's team had gathered. Then she turned and offered a separate,
gracious bow to Adrian and Julian, who were standing in the wings,
looking more nervous than she was. Finally, she turned to the five
people who held her future in their hands and gave them a deep,
respectful bow. It was not the bow of a supplicant, but of an artist
greeting her peers.

She sat down on the bench. There was no sheet music anywhere. She
adjusted the bench, took a slow, deep breath, and closed her eyes.

Then, she began to play "Reflections in a Shard of Glass."

She played it not as she had for Adrian in the church, and not as she
had imagined she would. She played it for the people in the booth. The
jarring, aggressive opening was transformed into the sound of a
community being shaken. The difficult, frantic passages became the
chaotic, fearful energy of uncertainty. The slow, contemplative section
became a prayer for guidance, a moment of quiet faith in the dark. And
the final, soaring crescendo was not just the sound of healing, but of a
town, a congregation, a family, coming together to build something new
and whole from the broken pieces.

When the final, peaceful chord faded into the rafters, four members of
the committee were still staring, their pens hovering uselessly over
their clipboards.

The fifth, Arthur Pendleton, was slowly and quietly clapping.
