## Chapter 15: The Festival of Revival

King Theron was not a stupid man. He had his own spies. He knew the Prince was not just "inspecting." He knew the boy had met with a mysterious, sharp-eyed young woman. He knew something was happening.

He had expected a prison break. An escape attempt. Perhaps a subtle assassination plot.

He had not expected a town crier.

The crier, a man hired by Ren with a pouch of Garrick's gold, stood in the center of the dreary town square, his voice magically amplified to echo off the black stone buildings.

**"Hear ye, hear ye!"**

People stopped. They looked up from the mud, their faces sullen and curious.

**"By order of the Kidnapped Prince, William of the Kingdom of Wonders,"** the crier declared, the phrase itself a masterpiece of political jujitsu, **"and as an honored guest of our own great King Theron, we announce the Festival of Revival!"**

A murmur went through the crowd. Festival? They hadn't had a festival in a generation.

**"One month from today, in this very square!"** the crier boomed. **"There will be music! There will be food! There will be a grand competition of craft, with prizes awarded from the Prince's own treasury! Let the smiths bring their finest steel! Let the weavers bring their brightest colors! Let the artisans of Asgar show their true soul!"**

He finished with a final, ringing proclamation.

**"Come one, come all! Let us show the world that Asgar is not a kingdom of iron and shadow, but of heart and hands!"**

He rang his bell and moved on to the next street, leaving the crowd in a state of stunned, buzzing excitement.

Up in the Iron Citadel, King Theron listened to the echo of the crier's voice. He looked at the five most respected (and dissident) craftsmen in his city, who had just been summoned to a private audience with him... at the Prince's "request."

He looked at the small, six-year-old boy who was sitting in the corner, calmly explaining the concept of "soulful joinery" to a bewildered royal guard.

The Asgarian captain, who had been assigned as William's permanent liaison, just stood by the door and groaned. He was no longer a kidnapper. He was now the head of festival security. He definitely needed a raise.

The King had been played. Again.

He couldn't stop the festival; to do so would be to admit the Prince had more influence in his own city than he did. It would be a public relations disaster. He was trapped. He had to endorse it.

He looked at the craftsmen. He saw a flicker of hope in their eyes he hadn't seen in years.

"Well," King Theron said, his voice a low, dangerous rumble. "It seems we are having a party."

William looked up from his lecture. "Excellent," he chirped. "I'll handle the catering."

This is a fantastic callback. William isn't just imitating Elian's methods; he's adapting them, using the story of his father's success as a powerful, inspirational tool. He's not just a commander; he's a evangelist for the "Wondrous" philosophy.

The way he delivers the "homework" assignment, now refined and imbued with his own authority, is a perfect demonstration of how much he has learned.
