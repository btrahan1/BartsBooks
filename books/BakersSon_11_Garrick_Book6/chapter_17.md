## Chapter 17: The First Spark

The next day, the guild hall was different. It had been cleaned. The dust was gone. Torches burned brightly in their sconces. The five masters stood before a long table, each with two cloth-wrapped objects before them. They were nervous, but it was the nervous energy of students about to present their final thesis, not of prisoners before a king.

Prince William entered, flanked by Finn and Galen. He did not go to the head of the room. He walked to the center and sat on a simple wooden stool, making himself part of their circle.

"Present," he said simply.

The masters looked at each other, hesitant.

William's eyes scanned the group and landed on a thin, wiry man with wood shavings in his beard.

"You there," William said. "Woodworker. You go first."

The man flinched.

"Make me proud," William added, his voice softening, but with a devious, clever glint in his eye, "and I may tell my father about you someday."

The effect was electric. The other masters—the smith, the potter—looked at the woodworker with a sudden, intense envy. To have one's work mentioned to the legendary Elian? That was a prize beyond gold.

The woodworker, a man named Jorn, swallowed hard and stepped forward. "Yes, Your Highness."

He unwrapped his first piece. It was a small, crudely carved bird. One wing was thicker than the other, and the beak was crooked. "My father was a fletcher," Jorn said, his voice rough. "He told me to carve a bird to understand the shape of a feather. I... I had no gift for it."

He then unwrapped his second piece.

The room fell silent.

It was another bird. A hawk, poised to strike, its wings half-unfurled. It was not a perfect, sterile carving. It was raw, dynamic, and bursting with a fierce, wild energy. The grain of the dark, gnarled wood had been used to form the feathers, giving it a look of rugged, natural power. It was not a beautiful piece. It was a *living* piece.

"Last night," Jorn said, his voice thick with an emotion he hadn't felt in years, "I did not try to carve a perfect bird. I tried to carve... the hunt. The feeling of the wind. The moment before the strike." He looked at William, a look of profound, dawning self-realization on his face. "I am not a fletcher. I am not a furniture maker. I am... a carver of wild things."

He had found his story.

William stood and walked to the table. He did not touch the hawk. He just looked at it. He saw the story. He saw the soul.

He turned to the woodworker.

"My father," William said with grave seriousness, "will be very interested to hear about your work."

He then turned to the Master Smith. "You're next. And your story had better have a point."

That's a powerful and poetic response from the Smith. He's not just presenting an object; he's baring his soul. He has completely bought into William's (and Elian's) philosophy. The "homework" has worked exactly as intended, breaking down his professional pride and forcing him to reconnect with his personal, emotional core.

It sets a beautiful, vulnerable tone for his presentation.
