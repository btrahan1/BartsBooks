## Chapter 19: The Memory of a Meal

The Master Smith, his face a mixture of relief and renewed purpose, returned to his place. The iron rose, a promise of a new future for the city, sat on the pedestal.

William turned his gaze to the next in line. "Next!"

It was the baker. A short, round woman with flour dusting her eyebrows and a perpetually worried expression. Her name was Anya.

"This should be interesting," Finn murmured to Galen. "In a city that prides itself on hardtack that can double as a paving stone, a true baker is a rebel."

Anya shuffled forward, her hands trembling slightly. "Your Highness."

She placed her two items on the table. The first was a small, dense, perfectly square biscuit. It was pale and looked utterly unappetizing.

"My first ration brick," she said, her voice barely a whisper. "To be accepted into the Baker's Guild, one must prove they can create a brick that will last a year without molding and can survive a ten-foot drop without chipping. It is... functional."

She then unwrapped her second item.

The air in the hall changed. A warm, sweet, and impossibly comforting aroma filled the room—the scent of cinnamon, baked apples, and memory.

It was a simple, rustic-looking loaf of bread, braided and glistening with a light honey glaze. It was not a grand, noble pastry. It was something far more powerful. It was a grandmother's love, given form.

"My mother..." Anya began, her voice thick with emotion. "She was not a guild baker. She baked for us. For her family. When I was a child, and the winter nights were cold, she would make this. A honey-apple braid loaf. We had no sugar, so she used the sweetest apples. We had no fine flour, so she ground the oats herself."

She looked at the loaf, but she was seeing a warm, fire-lit kitchen from a lifetime ago.

"Last night," she said, "I did not follow the Guild's recipe. I followed my mother's. I did not measure. I felt. I listened to the dough. It told me it did not want to be a brick. It told me it wanted to be... a hug."

She looked at William, her worried expression replaced by a quiet, defiant pride. "This is not the bread of a soldier. It is the bread of a family. And I had forgotten... I had forgotten how to make it."

William stood. He walked to the table. He did not speak. He simply broke off a small piece of the warm bread and ate it.

The flavor was... home.

He turned to the baker, his eyes serious.

"Master Anya," he said. "At the Festival of Revival, you will not be baking ration bricks."

He looked at the other masters. "You will all bring your stories. Your masterpieces. But Master Anya... she will bring the comfort."

He then looked at Finn, a challenge in his eyes. "And you, Uncle Finn. You will work with her. I want to see what happens when a healer and a baker combine their arts."

Finn's eyes lit up. Healing bread. The potential was staggering. "Yes, Your Highness," he said, a grin spreading across his face. "With pleasure."

The revolution in Asgar was not just about aesthetics. It was about to become delicious.

This is a great setup. The contrast between a brick—the most basic, functional unit of construction—and "something else entirely" promises a significant, dramatic reveal. For a mason, whose art is inherently structural and rigid, this will be a fascinating interpretation of Elian's "soulful craft" philosophy.
