## Chapter 21: The Glove and the Ghost

The hall was still buzzing with the impossible sight of the floating stone. The masters were looking at the quiet mason with a new, profound respect.

Finally, William turned to the last of the five. "Leatherworker. You are last. Let us see your story."

Last up was the leatherworker. His name was Bram, a man with a kind, weathered face and hands stained dark from a lifetime of working with tannins and dyes.

He stepped forward, a nervous energy about him. He placed his first, cloth-wrapped item on the table.

He unwrapped it. It was a military doublet, a piece of hardened leather armor. It was functional, ugly, and rigid. "My guild piece," Bram said, his voice soft. "It can stop a short-sword. It is what our city values. Strength. Defense." He looked at it with a clear lack of affection. "It has no... give."

He then reached into the satchel at his side and pulled out his second piece.

It was not a magnificent saddle. It was not a royal banner.

It was a child's 'stickball' glove.

It was tiny. It was beautiful.

Crafted from the softest, most supple doeskin, it was the color of warm honey. The stitching was a work of art, forming a delicate, spiraling pattern. It was not just a piece of sporting equipment; it was an heirloom, a perfect, miniature vessel of hope and love.

Bram held it in his large, calloused hands as if it were a fragile bird.

"I... I have a son," he began, his voice thick with an emotion he clearly wasn't used to showing. "He is four. He loves to play stickball in the street with the other children. But his hands... they are small. He is always getting bruised."

He looked at the tiny glove. "Last night, I did not think of my guild. I did not think of armor. I thought of him. I thought of the feeling of his small hand in mine. I thought of his laughter."

He placed the glove on the pedestal. It seemed to radiate a gentle, paternal warmth.

"This is not a tool of war," Bram said, looking at William. "It is not a masterpiece of high magic. It is... just a father's wish. A wish for his son to be able to play, and to not be hurt. A wish for him to have a childhood... better than my own."

The story was so simple, so pure, and so deeply relatable that it cut through all the epic grandeur of the previous presentations. It was a story not of a master's skill, but of a father's love.

William stood. He walked to the pedestal. He did not touch the floating stone, or the iron rose, or the healing bread.

He gently picked up the tiny glove. He slipped his own small hand inside. It was, of course, too big, but he could feel the softness, the care, the love that had been stitched into every seam.

He looked at Bram, and his eyes were not the eyes of a prince or a commander. They were the eyes of a six-year-old boy who missed his own father.

"It is the finest piece I have seen today," William said softly.

He then looked around the hall, at the five masters who had bared their souls.

"The festival will be a success," the Prince declared, his voice ringing with a new, quiet confidence. "The soul of Asgar is not dead. It is strong. It is beautiful."

He held up the small glove.

"And it is in very, very good hands."

This is the perfect scene to show the tangible results of William's "homework." The transformation of the city isn't being done by royal decree or paid labor; it's a grassroots movement, a city rediscovering its own soul and rebuilding itself from the ground up.

King Theron's bewildered perspective is the ideal lens through which to view this chaos. He's a king used to giving orders and seeing them followed. He's not prepared for a city that is rebuilding itself out of sheer, infectious inspiration.
