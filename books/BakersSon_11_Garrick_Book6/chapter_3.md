## Chapter 3: The Flaw in the Horseshoe

The Asgarian soldiers were preparing to mount, their movements efficient and brutal. But the procession was halted by a single, small figure.

Prince William had stopped in front of the captain's own warhorse, a massive, ill-tempered black stallion.

The horse was favoring its left foreleg, a slight, almost imperceptible limp. No one had noticed. No one except the boy with his father's eyes.

William frowned. He walked right up to the warhorse, a creature that had trampled men, and stood before it without a trace of fear. He pointed at the horse's hoof.

"This is unacceptable," he declared.

The captain, who was about to mount, stopped. "What is?"

"Your horse is lame," William said, his tone one of deep, professional disappointment. "The grain is off. The balance is compromised. It is an insult to the animal."

He then turned and pointed his small, imperious finger at the nearest soldier.

"You there. Bring me your knife."

The soldier blinked. "My... my knife, Your Highness?"

"For scraping," William clarified, as if speaking to a simpleton. "This horse has something lodged in its hoof. A stone, most likely. We cannot travel with a compromised transport. It is inefficient." He clapped his hands. "Do it. Do it now!"

The soldier, completely baffled, looked to his captain. The captain just stared, his mind trying to catch up to the sheer, surreal audacity of the situation. His prisoner was now commanding his men and critiquing his horse's podiatry.

The soldier, seeing no counter-command, hesitantly drew a wicked-looking dagger and offered it, hilt-first, to the small prince.

William took the knife. "Hold his leg," he ordered the soldier.

The soldier, now completely under the spell of the tiny commander, knelt and lifted the massive warhorse's leg. The horse, which normally would have kicked the man's teeth out, stood perfectly still, its intelligent eyes fixed on the small boy.

William, with the practiced, steady hand of a master craftsman, used the tip of the knife to pry a sharp, jagged piece of flint from the soft part of the hoof. A single drop of blood welled up.

"Hmm," William said. He then looked up at Finn. "Uncle Finn. A poultice. Now, if you please. The 'Green Leaf' paste. For inflammation."

Finn, who had been watching with a proud smile, stepped forward. He knelt, and with a touch of his hand and a smear of green paste from a vial, the small wound sealed and the inflammation vanished.

William patted the horse's leg. "Acceptable." He handed the dagger back to the soldier. "Clean your blade. It is unbalanced."

He then turned to the captain, his hands on his hips. "You should take better care of your tools," he admonished. "A flawed tool produces flawed work. This is basic craftsmanship."

He then turned and allowed Finn to lift him onto his own horse, leaving the captain and his entire cavalry column standing in stunned, silent disbelief.

They had captured a prince. But it was becoming increasingly, terrifyingly clear that he was the one in charge.

The Asgarians are not his captors; they are his staff. And they are, he has decided, deeply incompetent.
