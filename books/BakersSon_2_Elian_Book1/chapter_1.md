### Chapter 1: The Splintered Soul

Elian stared at the table.

It was a good table. By any normal measure, it was a masterpiece. The maple top was a single, flawless slab, its grain swirling like captured cream. The mortise and tenon joints were so precise they seemed to have grown that way.

But Elian wasn't looking at the joints. He was listening to the wood's soul.

And the wood was bored.

*"I could have been a violin,"* it whispered in his mind, a long, mournful hum. *"I held the song of a thousand birds in my branches. Now I hold gravy boats."*

"Life is full of disappointments," Elian muttered under his breath, wiping a non-existent speck of dust from the surface. "Now be quiet before I turn you into a toilet seat."

The wood went sullenly silent.

The shop door chimed. The bell—a gift from a client he despised—was made of cheap brass and sounded like a dying bird. Elian winced.

He tasted the air. Old parchment, lavender, and the faint, cloying sweetness of privilege. The Mayor's assistant.

A man named Loric, whose face was a perfect blend of ambition and incompetence, bustled in. "Elian! Just the man! The Mayor was absolutely delighted with the podium."

"Was he," Elian said, his voice flat. The cedar podium had screamed at him for three days about a murder it had witnessed as a sapling. Elian had silenced it with a thick coat of lacquer. He still felt guilty about it.

"He said the acoustics were remarkable!" Loric preened. "Now, he has a new project for you. A matter of great civic importance!"

Elian sighed. The last "matter of civic importance" had been a set of decorative birdhouses. The pine had wanted to be a ship's mast. It now housed sparrows.

"He wants a new desk," Loric announced, puffing out his chest. "Oak. Sturdy. Imposing. A desk that says 'I am the Mayor'."

"So, a desk that's slightly crooked and smells of bribery?" Elian asked, deadpan.

Loric's smile faltered. He clearly didn't get the joke. "Just... imposing."

"The wood chooses its form," Elian said, the words a tired mantra. "I am merely the midwife. I can't force an oak that wants to be a cradle to become a desk."

"Well, find one that wants to be a desk!" Loric said impatiently. He tossed a heavy pouch of coins on the screaming table. The maple groaned under the sudden weight. "The Mayor wants it by the Founder's Day festival. Don't be late."

Loric bustled out, leaving the jingling purse behind. Elian stared at the coins, then at the table.

"Did you hear that?" he said to the wood. "He wants me to find a tree that grew up with bureaucratic aspirations. The idiot."

He picked up the pouch. It was heavy. Enough to pay rent for three months and buy the rare Tung oil he needed for a secret project in the back. He hated the work, but he needed the coin.

He walked to the back of the shop, past the racks of "soulless" lumber he used for commissions. He pushed open a heavy, sound-proofed door and stepped into his sanctuary.

This room smelled different. It smelled of living things. Of resin and potential.

Here were the "True Pieces." A block of yew that hummed with the energy of a longbow. A burl of cherry that whispered stories of lovers who had carved their initials in its bark. And in the center of the room, on a velvet cloth, was his prize. A piece of Iron-Root, dark and gnarled, that had washed up in the river.

It didn't speak. It just... *was*. It had a presence, a density that felt ancient and powerful. He didn't know what it wanted to be yet. He was still listening.

He set the Mayor's purse down near the Iron-Root. The wood didn't react.

"Don't look at me like that," Elian said to the root. "A man's got to make a living. I can't just whittle away my days on you."

He paused.

"That wasn't intentional," he clarified.

He sighed and ran a hand over the Iron-Root's rough surface. The feel of it centered him. He could endure the Mayor's desk. He could endure the screaming wood and the ignorant clients. It was the price he paid to spend his nights here, in the quiet company of things that truly understood.

He turned to leave the back room, then stopped and glanced at the purse.

"Fine," he muttered to the Iron-Root. "I'll use the cheap stuff. But if the Mayor's desk collapses in a year, it's not my fault. You can't make a silk purse from a sow's ear, and you can't make a sturdy desk from a spineless sycamore."

He closed the door, the silence of the back room a stark contrast to the noisy demands of the world. He had a spineless sycamore to find. And it was probably going to complain the entire time.
