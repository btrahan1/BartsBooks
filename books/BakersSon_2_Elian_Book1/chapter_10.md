## Chapter 10: The Unmovable Mother

The two months after Finn's departure were quiet. Too quiet. The workshop felt like a tomb, the silence thick with the absence of Finn's earnest questions and the rhythmic thud of the oak staff in the yard. Elian threw himself into his work, taking on a few soulless commissions for merchants just to fill the silence with the sound of his tools.

He was in the middle of planing a particularly dull plank of pine (which wanted to be a fence post and was destined to be a bookshelf, a minor tragedy at best) when the bell chimed.

He didn't even look up. "We're closed."

"The sign on your door says 'Open'," a woman's voice replied. It was firm, clear, and held not a trace of intimidation.

"The sign is lying," Elian grunted, sending a long shaving of pine curling onto the floor.

He heard footsteps approach his workbench. "I need a crib."

"No."

A beat of silence. "You haven't even looked at me."

"I don't need to," Elian said, still focused on his work. "You want a box for your offspring. Carved with fawning lambs or some other nonsense. The wood will hate it. I will hate it. The answer is no."

"Yes."

The single word was so firm, so utterly certain, that Elian stopped planing. He finally looked up.

She was young, perhaps in her early twenties, and very clearly pregnant. She was not dressed like a noblewoman or a merchant's wife. Her clothes were simple but well-mended, and she carried herself with a ramrod-straight posture. Her face was set with a look of fierce, unshakeable determination.

"I said no," Elian repeated, narrowing his eyes.

"And I said yes," she countered, her chin jutting out.

"No!"

"Yes!"

My, she was stubborn! Elian felt a flush of frustration. He wasn't used to this. Most people withered under his first refusal. This woman seemed to be thriving on it. No means no, right?

"Why?" he finally asked, exasperated. "You can get a perfectly functional crib down the street at 'Timber Town'! They use nails! They probably have a sale on!"

"I don't want a crib," she said, her voice dropping, the fire in her eyes softening into something deeper. "I want a sanctuary. I want a place for my child to sleep that was made with respect. I want it to be... safe. Not just from splinters, but from... the world."

She looked around the workshop, at the way the light fell on the grain of a hanging plank, at the quiet order of the tools. "I have seen your work, Master Elian. The Mayor's desk. People say it's just a desk, but I sat at it once, and for a moment... I felt peace. The carvings... they tell a story."

She met his gaze, and her eyes were not the eyes of a demanding client. They were the eyes of a desperate mother.

"I have no money," she admitted. "But I am strong. I will work for you, for free, until the baby is born. Whatever you need. I will sweep the floors, haul lumber, sharpen tools, keep your books. You will not find a harder worker." She placed a hand on her swollen belly. "I am building a life. Please... help me build a home for it."

Elian was flustered. He was completely out of his depth. He knew how to deal with greed, with arrogance, with ignorance. He had no defense against this. This raw, fierce, selfless plea.

He looked at the woman. He looked at the pine plank on his bench. The wood, which had been complaining about becoming a bookshelf just moments ago, had gone silent. It was listening.

And in his mind, he heard a new, quiet whisper from the pine. A gentle, protective hum.

*"A cradle,"* it sang. *"I could be a cradle."*

Elian let out a long, ragged sigh, the sound of a fortress surrendering its gates.

"You'll start tomorrow," he grumbled, turning his back to her so she wouldn't see the conflict on his face. "The floor needs sweeping. And don't touch the Iron-Root."

He heard her let out a shaky, relieved breath. "Thank you," she whispered.

"Don't thank me yet," Elian said, picking up his plane. "You'll be regretting it by noon."

But as he began to work again, the monotonous scrape of the tool felt different. The quiet of the workshop no longer felt empty. It felt... expectant. He had lost a student, and now, it seemed, he had gained an apprentice. And a commission that, for the first time in a long time, the wood actually wanted him to build.

This is a perfect opportunity to show Elian's carefully constructed grumpy exterior being chipped away by sheer, relentless competence. He expects a damsel in distress, but he gets a force of nature. It's a classic "unlikely friendship" setup, built on a foundation of mutual, grudging respect for hard work.
