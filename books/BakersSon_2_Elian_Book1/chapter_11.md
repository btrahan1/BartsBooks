## Chapter 11: The Apprentice of Heartwood

Her name was **Lyra**. And Elian wasn't easily impressed, but she was something else.

He had expected her to be a burden, a well-meaning but clumsy presence in his meticulously ordered workshop. He had expected her to complain about the dust, the heavy lifting, the long hours. He had been wrong on all counts.

She arrived the next morning before he had even finished his first cup of tea. She didn't ask what to do; she saw the messy pile of pine shavings from the previous day and found the broom. The workshop floor had never been cleaner.

"The forge is cold," she stated, not as a complaint, but as an observation. "Do you need me to fetch kindling?"

Elian, who was trying to coax a response from a stubborn piece of cherrywood, just grunted an affirmation. She was back in ten minutes with a perfectly stacked armload of dry, resinous pine.

She really wanted this crib.

He tried to give her the worst jobs, the ones he hated, hoping to test her resolve. "Organize the scrap pile. Sort by wood type, then by size. Discard anything with rot."

It was a nightmare task, a mountain of splinters and off-cuts. He expected it to take her a week. She had it done in two days. She hadn't just stacked it; she had built neat, interlocking bins out of the larger pieces of scrap, labeling them with charcoal in a surprisingly elegant script.

"The oak pile was infested with powderpost beetles," she reported, handing him a small jar with one of the offending insects inside. "I quarantined and burned the affected pieces. You might want to check the main stack."

Elian stared at the jar, then at the impeccably organized scrap pile. He had been meaning to sort that for two years.

He watched her work. She was strong, her movements economical and efficient. She hauled lumber with a technique that used her legs and core, not her back. She learned the names of his tools after being told only once. She learned the difference between the scent of walnut and maple.

One afternoon, he was wrestling with a large, heavy slab of oak, trying to maneuver it onto his workbench. It was an awkward, frustrating process.

"If you place a wedge under the far side," Lyra's voice said from behind him, "you can pivot it on the corner. It would be easier."

Elian stopped, gritting his teeth, ready to snap at her for telling him how to do his job. But then he looked. She was right. It was a simple, elegant solution based on pure physics, one his own frustration had blinded him to.

He slid a wedge under the slab without a word. It pivoted easily onto the bench.

He didn't thank her. But he did stop what he was doing and go to the back room. He returned with a large, beautiful piece of pale, sweet-smelling pine. The same wood that had whispered *"a cradle"* to him.

He placed it on the empty workbench next to hers.

"Watch," he said.

It was the first time he had allowed her to see him begin a True Piece. He didn't speak. He just worked. He let her see the reverence with which he handled the wood, the way his hands listened to the grain, the slow, meditative process of releasing the form within.

Lyra stood there for hours, utterly silent, her eyes wide with a deep, profound understanding. She wasn't just watching a man build a crib. She was watching a priest perform a sacred rite.

That evening, as she was sweeping up the last of the day's shavings, she paused by the workbench. The rough shape of a cradle rocker was emerging from the pine. It was shaped like a crescent moon, a design of impossible grace and perfect balance.

She placed a hand on her belly, a soft, secret smile on her face.

Elian, watching from the shadows of his workshop, saw the gesture. He felt an unfamiliar warmth spread through his chest. It wasn't the Resonance of the wood. It was something else entirely.

He grunted and turned back to his tools. He still had a lot of work to do. And for the first time, he didn't feel like he was doing it alone.

That is pure gold. It's the perfect showcase of Elian's growth. He's not just a grumpy artisan anymore; he's the reluctant guardian, the master who solves problems with utter, grim confidence. That final line—"What wounds?"—is chilling and hilarious at the same time.

This scene also beautifully calls back to Finn, showing that Elian hasn't forgotten him and trusts in the skills he taught.
