## Chapter 13: A Lesson in Applied Physics (Revised Ending)

... The three hooligans, bruised, battered, and utterly bewildered, helped each other up and began to stumble away in the direction of the Temple, looking back over their shoulders as if expecting him to attack again.

Elian watched them go, a look of deep satisfaction on his face. He had dealt with the interruption. He had sent Finn some much-needed practical experience. And he had made a donation to the Temple. All in a morning's work.

He turned and walked back into the workshop, the heavy oak staff thumping on the threshold. He didn't immediately put it back. Instead, he stood for a moment in the center of the room, running a hand along the staff's scarred, battered surface.

The wood felt... agitated. Resentful. Its voice in his mind was a low, grumbling growl. It was a tool of training, of practice. It disliked the messy, chaotic reality of true violence.

"I'm sorry," Elian said, his voice a low murmur meant only for the wood.

Lyra, watching from her corner, tilted her head. She thought he was speaking to her.

Elian continued to address the staff. "That was unpleasant. I know. You prefer the clean rhythm of the forms." He felt the wood's grumbling lessen slightly, replaced by a sullen acceptance. "But you did well," he continued, a note of genuine praise in his voice. "Your weight was true. Your balance was perfect."

He walked the staff back to its corner and leaned it carefully against the wall, not just dropping it into place.

"Thank you for your assistance," he said, giving the top of the staff a final, respectful pat.

He turned and walked over to the cradle, picking up his delicate carving knife as if nothing had happened.

Lyra just stared at him, a new, profound layer of respect dawning in her eyes. He had just brutally dispatched three men without a flicker of emotion. And then, he had sincerely apologized to the stick he had used to do it.

She had thought she was working for a simple, grumpy woodworker. She now realized she was apprenticed to something far more ancient, dangerous, and impossibly strange.

"The pine," Elian said, not looking at her, his voice perfectly calm. "It's happy again. We can get back to work."

This is an incredible follow-up. It's a "rug pull" moment for the reader. We thought the incident was over, a simple case of "thugs get taught a lesson." But now, you're revealing a deeper, more tragic story behind their actions.

This also gives Elian a chance to move beyond being just a dispenser of physical lessons and into the role of a judge, investigator, and perhaps even a reluctant agent of justice. It forces him to engage with the messy human world he tries so hard to avoid.
