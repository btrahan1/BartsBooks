## Chapter 14: The Grain of a Lie

Three days later, the bell above the shop door chimed. Elian looked up from his work on the cradle.

It was them. The three hooligans.

They stood huddled in the doorway, looking less like thugs and more like frightened sheep. Their bruises were healing, but the fear in their eyes was fresh. The leader was clutching a small, meticulously wrapped package.

"We... uh... we're here to thank you," the leader stammered, his eyes darting nervously around the workshop.

Elian put down his chisel. He didn't say anything. He just waited.

"And... to thank him. Finn," another one added quickly. "He... he healed us. Didn't even charge us for the salves. Said the 'donation' covered it."

"He was... nice," the third one mumbled, looking at the floor.

The leader stepped forward and placed the small package on a nearby table. "The priestesses at the Temple... they gave us work. Mucking out the stables. It's not much, but... we bought you this."

Elian didn't look at the package. He looked at them. He used his [Grain Sight]. He saw the lines of their story. The fear was a chaotic red buzz. The gratitude was a pale, wavering blue. But underneath it all, he saw a deeper, darker color—a knot of tension and dishonesty. They weren't telling him everything.

He walked to the corner and picked up the heavy oak staff. He didn't brandish it. He just held it. The effect was immediate. The three young men flinched, their eyes locking onto the weapon that had so efficiently dismantled them.

Elian walked back and leaned against his workbench. He crossed his arms.

"Tell me again," he said, his voice quiet, but carrying the weight of stone. "In detail. The entire story."

"Wh-what story?" the leader asked, sweating now.

"The story of why three able-bodied young men are spending their days throwing rocks at a woodworker's shop instead of earning an honest wage," Elian clarified.

The three looked nervously at the staff. *THE* staff. They swallowed hard. Something in Elian's calm, unblinking gaze told them that lying would be a far more painful option than the truth.

And so, the real story poured out. It wasn't a story of malice, but of desperation. They weren't from this city. They were stonemason's apprentices from a village up north. They had come here for a job, a big commission to help build the new wing of the Merchant's Guild Hall. The job was run by a foreman, a man named Silas.

"He worked us for two months," the leader explained, his voice cracking. "Promised us a full season's wages. We finished the foundation last week. Then... he said the Guild was short on funds. He paid us a tenth of what we were owed and told us to get lost."

"We're stuck here," the second one added, his eyes welling up. "We don't have enough coin to get home. We don't have guild papers to find work here. We've been sleeping in the alleys. We... we were trying to get arrested. At least in the city jail, you get a meal and a roof."

The bravado was gone, replaced by the raw, humiliating truth. They weren't bullies. They were starving, homeless boys who had been cheated.

They cried harder now, in the quiet of the workshop, than they had while taking the beating in the street. It wasn't the pain of bruised ribs, but the pain of a bruised soul, of being utterly and completely powerless.

Elian listened, his expression unreadable. He felt Lyra's presence behind him, a silent, shocked witness.

He had misread the grain. He had seen their anger and desperation as a simple flaw, a knot of bad character. He hadn't seen the external pressure that had warped them.

He looked at the package on the table. He walked over and unwrapped it. Inside was a small, perfect apple pie, still warm from the Temple's kitchens. A peace offering. An apology. Bought with their first honest wages.

Elian stood in the silence, the warmth of the pie seeping into his hands. He had solved one problem with violence. But he had just uncovered a much deeper, much uglier rot. And a new, cold, and deeply unfamiliar anger began to smolder in his chest.

It wasn't the hot rage of a violated artist. It was the cold fury of a man who hated shoddy workmanship. And a man who doesn't pay his workers is the shoddiest piece of work there is.

That's a fantastic way for Elian to handle it. He doesn't offer them a handout; he offers them a job, a purpose, and a way to regain their dignity. It's a pragmatic, slightly grumpy act of profound compassion. He's not just fixing their immediate problem; he's setting them back on their own path.

It also perfectly sets up the next plot point: the confrontation with the foreman, Silas.
