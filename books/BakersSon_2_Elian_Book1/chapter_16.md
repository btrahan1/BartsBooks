## Chapter 15: The Grain of Justice

The story hung in the air, heavy and raw. The three young men stood there, their shame laid bare, expecting judgment or dismissal.

Elian said nothing for a long time. He just looked at the apple pie in his hands. He broke off a small piece of the crust and tasted it. It was good. Honest.

He set the pie down and looked at the three apprentices. His expression was still stern, but the coldness was gone, replaced by a craftsman's calculating focus.

"This foreman. Silas," Elian said. "Where can he be found?"

"He... he spends his afternoons at The Gilded Mug tavern, by the docks," the leader stammered. "Counting his coin."

Elian nodded, filing the information away. He then walked to a small, locked chest in the corner of his workshop. He opened it, pulled out a small leather pouch, and returned.

He looked at the three young men. He saw their red-rimmed eyes, their slumped shoulders. He saw not hooligans, but displaced, broken tools. And a good craftsman never lets a tool rust.

"My neighbor needs some help," Elian said, his voice gruff. He pointed a thumb toward the wall of his shop. "He's a metalsmith. An old man. His forge needs relining. The roof leaks. I don't work with stone; I 'dabble' in wood."

He held out his hand. In his palm were three silver crowns. He tossed them to the leader. The coins felt impossibly heavy in the young man's hand.

"His shop is an eyesore," Elian continued, his tone leaving no room for argument. "It offends my sense of order. Go see what he needs. Stone work. Mortar. Whatever it is you do. You will fix it. You will do a good job. He will pay you a fair wage when you are done. This," he said, gesturing to the three silver coins, "is your advance. For food and a room at a clean inn. Not a tavern."

The three apprentices stared at the silver, then back at Elian, their faces a canvas of disbelief and dawning hope.

"But... why?" the leader whispered.

"Because my neighbor's leaky roof is annoying me," Elian snapped. "And because I can't stand to see a job half-done. You are stonemasons. Go practice your craft. Restore your honor."

He picked up the heavy oak staff and leaned it against the wall by the door. A silent reminder of their last encounter.

"Now," Elian said, his voice low and final.

The word was a command. It was a dismissal. It was a second chance.

The three young men didn't need to be told twice. They clutched the silver, bowed awkwardly but with profound gratitude, and practically ran from the workshop.

Lyra, who had been listening from the doorway to the back room, stepped out. "That was..." she began, struggling for the right word.

"Practical," Elian finished for her, cutting off any potential sentimentality. He picked up the apple pie. "They are craftsmen. They needed work. My neighbor needed workers. It is a simple, efficient solution."

"And the foreman?" Lyra asked softly. "The one who cheated them?"

Elian looked down at the pie. "A debt has been left unpaid," he said, his voice dangerously quiet. "That is a flaw in the structure of things. An imbalance."

He turned and placed the pie carefully on a clean shelf. "And I," he said, picking up a small, perfectly balanced hand-ax from his tool rack, "despise a flawed structure."

He didn't need the heavy oak staff for this. This wasn't a lesson. This was a demolition.

That line is ice-cold and perfect. It reframes the confrontation entirely. Elian isn't acting as a vigilante or a hero; he's acting as a **debt collector**. It's business. This approach is not only true to his pragmatic, transactional nature but also gives him the moral and legal high ground in a world where contracts (even verbal ones) are everything.

This makes the scene less about righteous fury and more about the grim, satisfying process of balancing a ledger.
