## Chapter 18: The Unofficial Mayor

Elian returned to his workshop. The comforting scent of cedar and pine washed over him, a welcome antidote to the sour smell of the tavern. Lyra was there, sweeping, but she stopped and looked at him, her eyes wide with unasked questions.

He ignored her gaze and walked to his main workbench. He emptied the pouch. Seven gold crowns and a cascade of silver coins spilled out, gleaming in the dusty afternoon light. It was a fortune. More money than he had ever seen in one place.

He didn't look at it with satisfaction. He looked at it with the critical eye of a craftsman examining a pile of raw materials.

What was its purpose? What did this gold *want* to be?

He stared at the seven gold coins on his workbench. He thought of the three young stonemasons. He had given them a job, but it was a temporary one. The metalsmith's shop was small. The work would be done in a week, and then they would be idle again. Idle hands, like flawed wood, were prone to warping.

His gaze drifted to the window. He looked out at the muddy, rutted track that passed for a street in this part of town. Every time it rained, it became a moat of sludge, an offense to common sense and proper drainage.

He looked to his right, at the metalsmith's shop. The roof was a patchwork of hope and rust. To his left was a weaver's cottage with a crumbling chimney.

The entire street was a shoddy piece of work.

He heard the rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* of the stonemasons' hammers next door. They were working. They were proud.

"Yes," Elian murmured to himself. "That would work."

He scooped six of the gold coins back into the pouch, leaving one on the bench. He walked to the door that connected his workshop to the metalsmith's yard.

Joric, the leader of the apprentices, looked up as Elian emerged. He and his friends were meticulously re-pointing the brickwork of the forge. The change in them was remarkable. They stood straighter, their movements filled with the confidence of their craft.

"Master Elian," Joric said, wiping sweat from his brow. "The forge is nearly done. The roof will take another day."

"There is more work," Elian said, his voice leaving no room for negotiation.

He tossed the pouch of six gold coins to Joric. The young man caught it, the weight of it making his eyes go wide.

"What... what is this?"

"That is the budget for the street," Elian stated. "This road is a disgrace. It needs to be properly graded and paved with cobblestones. The drainage is non-existent. You will design and build a proper culvert."

He pointed to the weaver's cottage. "That chimney is a fire hazard. You will rebuild it." He gestured down the street. "You will survey every foundation on this block. Any that are cracked, you will repair. You will use this gold to hire local laborers for the unskilled work. You will purchase your materials from the merchants in this part of town, not the guild halls. You will pay everyone a fair, daily wage."

Joric stared at the gold, then back at Elian, utterly speechless. This wasn't a job; it was a public works project.

"But... who is paying for this?" Joric finally managed to ask.

Elian looked back toward The Gilded Mug, a flicker of cold satisfaction in his eyes. "A recent civic-minded 'donation' was made by Foreman Silas," he said. "He has a vested interest in the improvement of our fine city."

He turned to go back to his workshop.

"Master Elian, wait!" Joric called out. "This... this is a foreman's job. We're just masons. We don't know how to manage a project this big."

Elian stopped and looked back at him. "You will learn," he said simply. "A good craftsman sees the whole structure, not just the stone in his hand. Do not disappoint me."

He left them there, holding a fortune and a mandate.

Elian returned to his bench and picked up the single remaining gold coin. He tossed it in the air and caught it. This was his "collections fee." He looked over at Lyra, who had been watching the entire exchange in silent awe.

"Go to the market," he said, flipping her the coin. "Buy a pie. A good one. Apple, I think."

He turned back to the cradle, the wood's gentle, happy hum returning to his mind. The street outside would be noisy for a while, filled with the sounds of hammers and purposeful work.

He found he didn't mind at all. It was the sound of a flawed structure being made right.

That's a fantastic progression. Elian, the ultimate solitary craftsman, has accidentally become a project manager and civic leader. Now, he's forced to confront his own limitations. He needs to delegate, which is anathema to a perfectionist like him.

The timing of the bell is perfect—a moment of frustrated need met by an immediate, almost cosmic response. It sets the stage for a new character who will surely test his patience in entirely new ways.
