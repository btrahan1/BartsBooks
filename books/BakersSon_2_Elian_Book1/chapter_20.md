## Chapter 19: An Unwanted Necessity

The street outside Elian’s workshop was transformed. The mud and ruts were gone, replaced by a neat, professionally laid cobblestone path. The sound of Joric giving crisp, confident orders to a small crew of laborers had become the new morning rhythm. The entire block seemed to stand a little straighter.

Elian hated it.

Not the result. The result was orderly. It was correct. What he hated was the distraction. Every day, Joric would appear at his door with a question. "Master Elian, the chandler is asking for a premium on his wagon delivery because of the paving work. Do we pay it?" or "The culvert requires a different grade of mortar. Do I have your authorization to purchase it?"

Elian, who was trying to have a very important and nuanced conversation with a piece of stubborn walnut about whether it wanted to be a cabinet door or a decorative shield, had no time for "authorizations."

He needed to work. The street project, while necessary, produced no income. He had accepted a few small, soul-crushing commissions for things like spinning wheels and fence posts just to keep the lights on, but it wasn't enough. His mind was buzzing with a new, grand, and deeply unsettling plan. A plan to fix the rot he had uncovered in the city's underbelly, personified by men like Foreman Silas.

But to do what he had in mind, he needed gold. Lots of gold. The kind of gold you didn't get from fixing chairs. The kind of gold you got from building grotesque, gilded furniture for tasteless nobles.

He shuddered at the thought. He couldn't bring himself to do it. The idea of spending weeks arguing with a piece of wood that wanted to be a harp, forcing it into the shape of a gaudy wardrobe for some duke... it was a violation he was no longer willing to stomach.

"I need an apprentice," he muttered one afternoon, staring at a half-finished stool with utter disdain.

Lyra looked up from the cradle, which she was now polishing with a soft cloth. "An apprentice? Master Elian, I'm right here."

"Not you," he said, waving a dismissive hand. "You are my project manager for the cradle. You have... taste. I need someone else. A production apprentice. Someone with decent hands and no soul, who can churn out the boring, profitable work while I focus on... other things."

He paced the workshop, agitation rolling off him in waves. "Where am I going to find an apprentice like that? Someone skilled enough to not ruin my name, but dull enough to not argue with a chair about its destiny?"

He threw his hands up in frustration. It was an impossible problem.

At that exact moment, the shop door opened. The brass bell chimed its irritating, bird-like cry.

Elian spun around, ready to shout "We're closed!" but the words died in his throat.

Standing in the doorway was a young woman. She was perhaps seventeen, with a shock of bright red hair pulled back in a messy braid, and startlingly green eyes that seemed to take in the entire workshop in a single, assessing glance. She was covered in road dust, a large, clumsy-looking pack on her shoulders. But it was the calloused, capable-looking hands clutching a beautifully carved, but broken, wooden lute that caught Elian's attention.

"Heard this was the best woodshop in town," the young woman said. Her voice was confident, with a traveler's clipped accent. "Also heard the owner is a world-class grouch who hates money. I'm here to solve one of those problems for him. Maybe both."

She walked forward and placed the broken lute on his workbench. "The name's **Piper**. I can fix this, but I need a proper workshop. I'll work for you. I'll build your boring chairs, your soulless tables, your gaudy wardrobes. I don't care what the wood 'wants' to be. I care what the client is willing to pay. In exchange, I get a bench in the corner and access to your tools."

Elian was speechless. He stared at the girl, at her audacious proposal, at the sheer, unmitigated gall of her. He looked at the heavens.

"Ask and you shall receive?" he murmured under his breath. "Who would have thought?"

He looked from the fiery-haired girl to Lyra, who was watching the scene with wide, amused eyes. His quiet, orderly world had been invaded. Again.

"You're hired," he grunted, turning his back so she wouldn't see the flicker of stunned relief on his face. "Don't break my chisels."

That negotiation is perfect. It establishes Piper's character instantly—she's a sharp, fearless bargainer. It also cleverly elevates Lyra's position, making her the official "buffer" between Elian's artistic temperament and the harsh realities of commerce. This creates a fantastic new dynamic for the workshop.
