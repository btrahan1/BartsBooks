## Chapter 21: A Clash of Grains

The first three days of the new arrangement were a study in contrasts. Lyra was a natural manager, creating a ledger, organizing commissions, and acting as a serene buffer between the two volatile artisans. Elian, for his part, retreated into a state of blissful focus, spending his days in the back room, in silent communion with the Iron-Root, which was finally beginning to whisper its true name to him.

The workshop floor belonged to Piper. And she was a whirlwind.

She repaired the broken lute with a speed and precision that Elian, watching secretly from a crack in the door, had to grudgingly admit was impressive. She then took on three more instrument repairs, a chair re-caning, and a commission for a set of lacquered serving trays. She worked with a joyful, furious energy, the jingle of coin her driving rhythm.

Then came the hope chest.

It was a commission from a wealthy shopkeeper for his daughter's wedding. The wood was a beautiful, if slightly arrogant, piece of cedar. Piper had declared she could have it done in a week.

Elian was passing through the main shop on his way to get tea when he heard it. A faint, high-pitched *screee* of protest. It wasn't the sound of a tool; it was the sound of wood in pain.

He stopped dead. Piper was at the workbench, using a block plane on one of the cedar panels. She was moving fast, her strokes efficient, but she was planing against the natural direction of the grain. To Elian, it was the visual and spiritual equivalent of rubbing a cat's fur the wrong way.

"No! Not like that!" The words exploded out of him, a guttural cry of pure instinct.

Piper jumped, startled, nearly dropping the plane. She spun around, a fiery look in her green eyes. "Don't sneak up on me like that!"

"You're going against the grain!" Elian pointed a trembling finger at the panel. He could see it with his [Grain Sight]—the pale blue lines were being torn, creating a microscopic, fuzzy texture called "tear-out." The wood was screaming.

Piper rolled her eyes. "I know what I'm doing," she snapped back. "It's faster this way to get the rough shape. I'll smooth it out later with a scraper. We're building a hope chest for a shopkeeper's daughter, not a funerary urn for a princess! You want this done in a week, or a month?"

"A thing worth doing is worth doing right!" Elian retorted, his voice rising. "You are injuring the wood! You are leaving a scar that no amount of scraping will truly heal! It will be a flaw in its soul!"

"A flaw no one but you will ever see!" Piper shot back, planting her hands on her hips. "The client wants a sturdy box that smells nice. He does not care about its 'soul'! He cares about the price and the delivery date, both of which I am meeting!"

They stood there, glaring at each other across the workbench, two masters of the same craft with completely opposing philosophies. Elian, the purist, who served the wood. Piper, the pragmatist, who served the client.

Lyra stepped quietly between them, a calming presence in the storm. She placed a hand on Elian's arm. "Master Elian, perhaps you could show Piper the technique you use for shaping? The one that's both fast and follows the grain?"

Elian grumbled, but Lyra's logic was sound. He was a teacher now, whether he liked it or not.

He took the plane from Piper's hand. "Watch," he grunted. He turned the board around. Instead of pushing the plane, he used a skewed, slicing motion, holding the tool at a slight angle. The plane hissed across the wood, peeling off a perfect, translucent shaving. The wood's pained screeching stopped, replaced by a soft, contented hum.

Piper watched, her anger fading, replaced by a grudging curiosity. The technique was unorthodox, but it was undeniably effective. It was just as fast as her method, but the resulting surface was as smooth as glass.

"It's called a 'skewed shear'," Elian explained, unable to resist the urge to teach. "You're not tearing the fibers; you're slicing them cleanly. It's more efficient. It respects the material."

He handed the plane back to her. "Try it."

Piper took the tool. She mimicked his stance, his angle. She pushed. The plane glided across the wood with a satisfying hiss. She looked at the flawless surface, then back at Elian.

She didn't apologize. She didn't thank him.

"Huh," she said. "Not bad."

She turned back to her work, using the new technique. Elian stood there for a moment, then grunted and retreated to his back room.

Lyra smiled. It wasn't a truce, not yet. But it was a start. A bridge of mutual, hard-won respect, built one perfect, paper-thin wood shaving at a time.

That's the perfect culmination of Piper's arrival. The results are undeniable, forcing Elian to confront the fact that her pragmatic, commercial approach *works*. The "bonus" is his grudging, non-verbal way of admitting she was right, which is a huge step for his character. And his "walkabout" is his escape valve, showing he's still not entirely comfortable with this new, successful reality.
