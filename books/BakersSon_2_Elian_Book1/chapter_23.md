## Chapter 22: The Uncomfortable Weight of Profit

The end of the month arrived with the finality of a well-seated joint. Lyra, who had taken to managing the shop's finances with the same quiet competence she applied to everything, sat down with Elian at the now-finished (and purring) Mayor's desk.

She pushed a small, neat ledger across the polished surface. "The accounting is done for our first month as a team, Master Elian."

Elian grunted, picking up the book with the air of a man about to inspect a poorly made chair. He opened it. He was prepared for a modest sum, perhaps a few silver crowns left over after the cost of materials and Piper's incessant demands for new tools.

He stared at the final entry on the page. His eyes narrowed. He read it again.

**Net Profit: 10 Gold, 4 Silver, 8 Copper.**

He closed the ledger. He opened it again. The number was still there.

Ten gold. *Ten gold in a month.*

That was after paying for lumber. After Piper's material stipend. After the cost of the new forge bricks for the metalsmith next door. After *everything*. It was more money than his shop had cleared in the last two years combined.

It was obscene.

He looked across the workshop. Piper was in her corner, humming a jaunty tune as she meticulously replaced the fretboard on an expensive-looking mandolin. She had been a force of nature, a whirlwind of productive energy. She took commissions he would have sneered at, haggled with merchants he would have insulted, and finished projects with a speed that baffled him. She didn't just work; she *produced*.

Lyra had been the quiet engine making it all run, managing the inventory, scheduling the work, and somehow keeping the peace between his artistic soul and Piper's commercial one.

Elian looked at the number again. Ten gold. It was enough to fund his secret projects for a year. It was enough to start his real work. And it was all thanks to the two women who had invaded his sanctuary.

He let out a long, slow sigh. It was the sound of a man being proven profoundly, uncomfortably wrong.

He pushed back from the desk, walked over to his lockbox, and counted out twelve gold coins. He walked back to the main workshop floor. Piper and Lyra stopped what they were doing and looked at him.

He tossed one gold coin to Lyra. It spun in the air, a flash of yellow, and she caught it deftly.

He tossed another gold coin to Piper. Her reflexes were just as sharp.

"What's this?" Piper asked, her eyes wide. "My 15 percent share isn't this much."

"It's a bonus," Elian grumbled, avoiding eye contact. "For... efficiency. But don't get used to it."

He then counted out their shares of the remaining eight gold—one and a half for Piper, a half for Lyra, who had insisted on only a small stipend—and placed the coins on the bench. His own share, a hefty pile of six gold, he swept back into his pouch.

He turned and grabbed his worn traveling cloak from a peg by the door.

"Where are you going?" Lyra asked, a note of concern in her voice.

"I am going to do a walkabout," he announced. "In the forest. To listen. The shop is getting too loud."

He wrapped the cloak around his shoulders and strode out the door without another word, the bell chiming his departure.

Piper and Lyra looked at each other, then at the gold coins in their hands.

"A 'walkabout'?" Piper said, a slow, disbelieving grin spreading across her face. "He's running away from his own success."

"He's going to think," Lyra corrected softly, though she was smiling too. "And to thank the wood."

Piper just shook her head, jingling the coins in her palm. "Whatever he wants to call it. As long as he keeps coming back."

She turned back to her work, the jaunty tune she was humming now a little louder, a little more triumphant. The Knot & Burl was officially profitable. And their grumpy, brilliant, and deeply weird master was slowly, grudgingly, learning how to be a boss.

This is a perfect scene for his "walkabout." It's not just an escape; it's a mission. And the way he "negotiates" with the wood, apologizing for its mundane fate while justifying the necessity, is the core of his character. It's his personal torment and his unique gift, all in one.

This scene beautifully illustrates his internal conflict: his artistic soul versus his newfound pragmatic purpose.
