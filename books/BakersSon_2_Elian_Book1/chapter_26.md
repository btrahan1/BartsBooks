## Chapter 25: The Hummingbird

Piper was a fast learner. Annoyingly so.

Elian had expected her to struggle, to fight against his methods. Instead, she had attacked the challenge with the same ferocious energy she applied to everything. She didn't just listen to the wood; she interrogated it.

She would hold a piece of ash to her ear, close her eyes, and mutter, "Alright, you. What's your story? You want a curved backrest? Straight legs? Speak up. We haven't got all day."

To Elian's profound irritation, the wood seemed to respond to her aggressive, no-nonsense approach.

Now, a week later, she was assembling the final piece of the "Good" chair. She had followed his instructions to the letter, using the skewed shear, carving with the grain, taking her time. And as she worked, a low, happy, and incredibly distracting hum filled the workshop. It was Piper.

"Stop that humming!" Elian finally snapped from his corner, where he was trying to sketch a design. "You sound like a freight train bearing down on a beehive!"

Piper just smiled, not missing a beat as she fitted a tenon into its mortise. The joint slid home with a soft, perfect sigh of wood meeting wood. Lyra, who was quietly updating the ledger nearby, let out a small snort of laughter but quickly smothered it with her hand.

"Yes, Master Elian. As you say, Master Elian," Piper chirped, her voice laced with cheerful mockery.

She knew calling him "Master" annoyed him to no end, but she was in her zone. The chair on her workbench was unlike anything she had ever made. It wasn't just functional; it was graceful. The lines flowed. The grain seemed to shimmer. It didn't just look like a chair; it *felt* like one, radiating a sense of welcome and repose. She had followed the wood's lead, and it had guided her to a design more beautiful than any she could have forced upon it.

Her results spoke for themselves.

"Done," she announced, stepping back to admire her work. She ran a hand over the backrest. The wood was practically vibrating with contentment. "The client is coming for it this afternoon."

She walked over to Lyra's desk and pointed at an entry in the ledger.

"And," she said, her voice dropping to a stage whisper loud enough for Elian to hear, "we'll get five gold from this piece alone."

Elian froze. "Five what?"

"Five gold," Piper repeated, a triumphant grin on her face. "The client is a spice merchant who fancies himself a connoisseur. I told him it was a 'meditative seating experience crafted from a single, spiritually aligned ash tree, harvested only under the light of a full moon'."

"You what?" Elian was horrified.

"He loved it," Piper said, shrugging. "Rich people don't buy chairs; they buy stories. So I gave him a good one. Five gold. Insane, right?"

Elian stared at the chair. It was, he had to admit, a very good chair. It was probably worth two gold on craftsmanship alone. The story was nonsense, of course. He had harvested that ash in the pouring rain, and its primary spiritual alignment had been with a patch of stubborn mud.

But... five gold.

For a single, honest chair.

He watched as Piper and Lyra shared a conspiratorial giggle. He saw the pride in Piper's work, the undeniable quality of the piece she had created. He saw the neat, clean entry in Lyra's ledger.

He was surrounded by competent, productive, and infuriatingly cheerful women. His workshop was profitable. His street was paved. And he was being paid a fortune to do work he could actually be proud of.

It was, he decided, the most confusing and deeply irritating period of his entire life. He grumbled, picked up his tools, and retreated to the glorious, silent solitude of his back room. He had a masterpiece to attend to, far from the maddening sound of humming and commerce.

This is the perfect external conflict to introduce. Elian has created his own bubble of order and grudging contentment. Now, the outside world, in its most bureaucratic and irritating form, is coming to knock on the door.

A confrontation with a tax collector is a fantastic way to showcase Elian's unique brand of logic and his utter disdain for systems he deems "flawed." He won't see it as a legal obligation; he'll see it as a poorly designed structure that needs to be dismantled.
