## Chapter 26: The Flawed Structure of the Crown

The bell above the door chimed, its sound sharper and more unwelcome than usual. Elian didn't look up. He was in the middle of a delicate inlay on a small, personal project, a gift for Lyra.

"We're not taking new commissions until next week," he said, his voice a low growl of concentration. "See the manager."

"I am not here for a commission," a dry, reedy voice replied. "I am here on behalf of the Queen's Treasury."

Elian's hand froze. Slowly, he looked up.

The man standing in his shop was the physical embodiment of a dusty ledger. He was thin, wore pinched spectacles, and was dressed in the severe black robes of a royal tax assessor. He held a clipboard like a weapon.

The tax collector, a man named Fisc, peered around the workshop, his eyes lingering on Piper's five-gold chair, the recently delivered Mayor's desk, and the general, undeniable air of prosperity that the shop now exuded.

"It has come to our attention," Fisc said, making a small tick on his parchment, "that 'The Knot & Burl' has seen a significant, and as yet unreported, increase in revenue."

Lyra stepped forward from her desk, her ledger held in her hands, a polite but firm expression on her face. "Greetings, Assessor. I am the shop manager. All of our earnings are accounted for. We are prepared to pay the standard artisan's tithe."

"Ah, but you are no longer just an artisan's shop, are you?" Fisc said with a thin, unpleasant smile. He gestured vaguely at the newly paved street outside. "You have overseen public works. You have managed payroll for a crew of laborers. That elevates you to the level of a 'Guild-level Enterprise', which is taxed at a significantly higher rate."

He consulted his clipboard. "Factoring in your estimated gross income, materials expenditure, and the 'Civic Improvement Levy'... the preliminary assessment of your tax debt for this quarter is... one hundred and twenty gold crowns."

The silence in the workshop was absolute. Piper, who had been sanding a lute body, stopped, her mouth agape. Lyra's face went pale.

Elian slowly placed his inlay knife down on his workbench. He stood up. He looked at the tax collector.

"You want *how much?*" he asked, his voice dangerously quiet.

Fisc puffed out his chest, enjoying the shock. "One hundred and twenty gold. It is the law. Payment is due by the end of the fiscal moon. Failure to comply will result in seizure of assets." He eyed the glowing Iron-Root in the back room, the door to which was slightly ajar. "All assets."

Elian didn't rage. He didn't shout. A strange, frightening calm settled over him. He looked at the tax collector not as a threat, but as a puzzle. A piece of badly designed furniture. The "law" Fisc was quoting was a flawed joint, a rotten piece of wood in the structure of the city.

He walked over to a large, dusty chalkboard on the wall where he sometimes sketched designs. He picked up a piece of chalk.

"Explain it to me," Elian said.

"Explain what?" Fisc asked, irritated.

"The math," Elian said, turning to face the board. "I am a simple woodworker. I require you to demonstrate the logic of your calculations."

Fisc scoffed, but the command in Elian's voice was undeniable. Annoyed, he began to drone on about tax brackets, levies, and depreciation schedules.

As he spoke, Elian began to draw on the chalkboard. He wasn't writing numbers. He was drawing a structure. A diagram.

"So, the 'Artisan's Tithe' is the foundation," Elian said, drawing a solid base. "The 'Guild Enterprise' tax is a support beam." He drew a thick, heavy beam on top of the foundation. "And the 'Civic Improvement Levy' is... what? A load-bearing wall?"

"In a manner of speaking, yes," Fisc said, warming to the topic, pleased that this simple craftsman was finally understanding.

"I see," Elian said. He drew the final piece—a massive, impossibly heavy roof labeled "120 Gold" resting on the single support beam.

He stepped back from the chalkboard. The drawing was a blueprint of a building that was structurally, fundamentally, impossible. A building designed to collapse.

"Your design is flawed," Elian stated. "This structure cannot bear the weight you have placed upon it. It is designed to fail."

"It's not a design, it's the tax code!" Fisc sputtered. "It is not for you to question!"

"I question any structure that is built to break," Elian said, his voice dropping. He turned from the chalkboard and looked the tax assessor dead in the eye. "You are not collecting a tax. You are attempting a demolition. And I," he said, picking up a heavy wooden mallet from his workbench, "do not tolerate shoddy demolition work either."

He took a step forward. "The assessment is incorrect. You will go back to your office. You will recalculate. And you will return with a design that makes sense. Or I will come to you."

The tax collector, for the first time, felt a prickle of genuine fear. This wasn't a negotiation about money. This was a craftsman's rage against a flawed system, and it was far more terrifying than any bandit's greed.

That is a brilliant pivot! Elian isn't going to fight the system head-on with an axe this time. He's going to outsmart it. He's going to use his craft not as a weapon of destruction, but as a tool of political maneuvering.

This is a high-stakes gamble that leverages Piper's ambition and his own legendary skill. It's not about refusing to pay the tax; it's about making himself too valuable to be crushed by it.
