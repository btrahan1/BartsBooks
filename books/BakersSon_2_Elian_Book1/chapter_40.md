## Chapter 39: The Structural Solution

Elian listened, his expression unreadable, as the Queen laid out the "flawed structure" of her situation. Duke Corvan was applying immense political pressure. His son, Lord Valerius, was the most suitable candidate by birth and station. To refuse him outright would risk a civil rift. To accept was a personal and political nightmare—Valerius was arrogant, ambitious, and loyal only to his father. She was trapped in a political vise.

Elian looked at the problem not as a courtier, but as a woodworker. The proposed structure was unsound. The "joint" between the Crown and House Corvan was weak. It needed a different component. A stronger one.

When she finished, he was silent for a long moment, not in sympathy, but in calculation. He saw the grain of the problem, the lines of stress, and the single, critical point where a wedge could be driven.

"A duel then," he said, his voice as calm and matter-of-fact as if he were discussing the angle of a mortise.

The Queen did actually drop her cup this time. It shattered on the cobblestones with a sharp, percussive crack.

"Excuse me?" she breathed, staring at him as if he had just sprouted a second head.

"A duel," he repeated. "For the right to your hand in marriage. If you will have me."

He saw the shock on her face and immediately held up a hand, a craftsman clarifying a blueprint.

"Fear not, Your Majesty. It is a structural solution, not a romantic one. We could be engaged for years, if that is your wish. Decades, even. I only wish to serve. To provide a... stronger support beam for the throne."

The Queen was speechless. The sheer, insane audacity of it was beyond anything she had ever encountered.

"But... he is a master swordsman," she finally stammered. "A professional duelist. He has been training since he could walk. You are a woodworker! You cannot defeat him!"

"Be that as it may," Elian said with a small shrug. "Let us assume, for the sake of the design, that I could. Would it help? It would remove Lord Valerius from the equation, honorably and publicly. It would satisfy the court's demand for you to have a consort. It would take the pressure off of you. You could dissolve our 'engagement' years in the future, citing irreconcilable differences in, say, our opinions on joinery. We are both very busy, after all. But the immediate pressure to marry," he concluded, "would be off, yes?"

He had laid out the plan with the cool, detached logic of a master builder. He was offering himself as a sacrifice, a piece of wood to be used in a larger construction, to brace a failing wall. He was offering her a cage of her own choosing.

The Queen stared at him for a long time. A very long time. The initial shock gave way to a slow-dawning sense of wonder. He wasn't proposing marriage. He was proposing an alliance. A partnership. He saw her not as a prize to be won, but as a fellow master craftsman, and he was offering her the perfect tool to solve her most difficult problem.

Finally, a slow, brilliant, and utterly dazzling smile spread across her face. It was a smile of pure, unadulterated delight.

"Why would I want to dissolve a perfect partnership?" she said.

Elian blinked. That part of the plan had not been in his original design. The Queen stood, walked around the table, and took his calloused hand in hers.

"You are a fascinating, infuriating, and profoundly good man, Elian of The Knot & Burl," she said, her eyes dancing with a light he had never seen before. "You have presented a... compelling structural solution. But you seem to have overlooked one critical detail."

"What is that, Your Majesty?" he asked, his heart beginning to beat a little faster.

She leaned in, her voice a soft whisper.

"In this design," she said, "I am not the roof. I am the other support beam. And I find your proposal... to be a perfect fit."

This is a fantastic moment of reversal. He has been the one in complete control, diagnosing her "flaw," proposing a structural solution. Now, she has thrown him completely off balance by introducing a variable he never accounted for: genuine, human emotion.

His silence isn't rejection; it's the blue screen of death for a man whose brain operates on logic, grain, and geometry. He's trying to process a piece of data that doesn't fit his entire worldview.
