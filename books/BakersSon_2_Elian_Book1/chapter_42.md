## Chapter 41: The Steak Knife

The Queen was a woman of terrifying efficiency. The moment Elian agreed to the duel, the entire machinery of the royal court shifted into motion. A royal proclamation was issued, announcing that Master Elian, the Queen's Champion Artisan, would duel Lord Valerius for a "matter of high honor," the prize of which was delicately left unstated but universally understood.

Valerius, arrogant and eager to reclaim his lost honor, accepted instantly.

And Elian was unceremoniously handed over to the Queen's personal trainer.

His name was Ser Kaelen, Captain of the Royal Guard. He was a mountain of a man with a scarred face, a perpetually sour expression, and eyes that had seen a hundred battles. He was, Elian quickly learned, a man with absolutely no appreciation for the philosophical nuances of wood.

They stood in the royal training grounds, a pristine square of packed sand. Kaelen had handed Elian a dueling saber, a light, wickedly sharp blade designed for speed and precision.

Elian held it away from his body as if it were a dead fish.

"The duel is in two weeks, yet you hold that saber like a garden fork," Kaelen grumbled, circling him like a wolf. "I was told you could fight. The rumors from the city are that you defeated three ruffians in the street, barely breaking a sweat!"

"It was five seconds," Elian corrected, his tone grumpy. He shifted his grip on the saber, and the balance felt all wrong. It had no weight, no soul. It didn't speak to him. "And I was using a staff. A proper tool. Not a pointed, metal steak knife."

Kaelen stopped circling and stared at him. "A steak knife? This is a master-forged blade, worth more than your entire workshop."

"It's unbalanced," Elian retorted, giving the blade a tentative swish. "The tang is too thin. The pommel is purely decorative. It's a piece of jewelry, not a tool. I wouldn't even use it to carve a chicken."

Ser Kaelen's face turned a dangerous shade of red. "You are to face the finest duelist in the kingdom, and you are critiquing the sword's aesthetics?"

"I am critiquing its flawed design," Elian snapped back. "How can a man fight with a tool he doesn't respect?"

"You will learn to respect it!" Kaelen roared. He drew his own saber. "En garde!"

What followed was not a lesson. It was a humiliation.

Kaelen was a blur of motion. The world became a whirlwind of hissing steel. Elian, for all his strength and speed, was utterly outclassed. His [Grain Sight] was useless; the movements of a master swordsman had no grain. They were a chaotic storm of sharp, unpredictable angles.

He blocked, he parried, he backpedaled. The *clang, clang, clang* of steel on steel was a jarring, unpleasant sound. Within thirty seconds, his arm was numb, his wrist ached, and Kaelen's blade had tapped him lightly on the chest a dozen times.

Finally, with a flick of his wrist, Kaelen disarmed him. The saber spun out of Elian's hand and landed in the sand.

Elian stood there, panting, his velvet training tunic soaked with sweat.

"You are a brawler," Kaelen said, his voice dripping with disdain. "You have strength and reflexes. But you have no form. No discipline. You fight like a cornered badger. Lord Valerius will cut you to ribbons in ten seconds."

Elian didn't argue. He just stared at the saber lying in the sand. Kaelen was right. In this world, with this tool, he was a complete and utter novice.

"Again," Kaelen commanded.

Elian picked up the sword. He hated it. He hated the feel of it, the sound of it, the arrogant, soulless way it moved.

But he had made a promise to the Queen. He had offered himself as a support beam. And a support beam that snaps under the first test is a piece of shoddy work.

"Fine," Elian grunted, raising the saber into a clumsy guard. "But I'm telling you, the balance is all wrong."

That's a fantastic scene. It's the "reveal" that Elian hasn't just been struggling; he's been *adapting*. He's translating the problem into his own language. The Queen's question is the perfect "aha!" moment, cutting through the trainer's rigid assumptions and getting to the heart of the matter.
