## Chapter 5: The Unwilling Students

Master Kael's quiet, humbled "Show me how" was a turning point. A crack had appeared in the wall of pride. But Elian knew he had to drive the wedge deeper.

He gave the old smith a sharp, appraising nod. "I can't 'show you' how to forge steel, Master Kael. That is your art. But I can 'teach you' how to listen to its story before you strike the first blow."

He gestured for Kael to take a seat in the front row, a clear signal that the smith was now a student.

"Class will begin shortly," Elian announced to the tense, silent room. "But first..."

His eyes, sharp as a hawk's, scanned the assembled masters and landed on a barrel-chested man with clay dust permanently embedded in the lines of his face. His name was Borin, and his pottery was known for being incredibly durable and utterly devoid of inspiration.

"...YOU! Master Potter! Front and center!"

Borin, the Master of the Potter's Collective, flinched as if he'd been struck. He looked around, hoping he had misheard.

"Yes, you," Elian said, his voice leaving no room for argument. "And bring that... *thing*... you call a 'vase' with you."

The master potter's face turned the color of wet clay. He had brought a piece to the meeting as a show of his guild's quality. It was a large, technically perfect vase, its glaze a uniform, boring brown. He had been quite proud of it. Now, he felt a deep, sinking dread.

He shuffled to the front of the hall, holding the vase like it was a bomb.

Elian didn't even take it. He just circled it, his eyes narrowed in critical assessment.

"Clay," Elian began, his voice taking on the tone of a lecturer addressing a particularly slow student. "It is the memory of the earth. It remembers the mountains it came from, the rivers that carried it. It remembers the pressure, the heat. What does this vase remember?"

He tapped the side of the vase. It gave a dull, solid *thunk*.

"Nothing," Elian answered his own question. "It remembers being thrown on a wheel, fired in a kiln, and glazed a color I can only describe as 'muddy regret'. It is a container. It is functional. It has all the artistic merit of a bucket."

Borin looked like he wanted the floor to swallow him whole.

"Now imagine," Elian continued, his voice dropping, painting a new picture for the assembled artisans. "Imagine if, when you threw the clay, you didn't just force it into a shape. Imagine you followed the memory within it. Imagine the glaze wasn't a flat, boring brown, but a cascade of blues and greens that mirrored the mountain stream the clay was dug from. Imagine you etched a subtle pattern around the rim, the story of the fossils you found in the clay bed."

He looked at Borin, his gaze intense. "That is not a bucket. That is a story. That is a piece of the earth itself, given a voice. That is what the nobles who line our streets will pay a fortune for. That is what the Iron Assembly, with their perfect, soulless molds, can never hope to replicate."

He dismissed the potter with a wave of his hand. "Take a seat, Master Borin. Your education is about to begin."

He turned to the last of the major guild heads, the wizened old woman from the Weaver's Guild, Mistress Elara.

She stood up before he could even call on her.

"Let me guess, Master Elian," she said, her voice dry as old parchment, but with a sharp, intelligent glint in her eyes. "This tapestry I brought," she gestured to a technically flawless but artistically stiff depiction of a royal hunt, "is just a 'threaded sheet'?"

A slow, rare grin of genuine respect spread across Elian's face.

"It seems, Mistress Elara," he said, "that you are the first student to have already read the lesson. Please, take a seat in the front row with Master Kael."

The battle was over. He had shattered their pride, challenged their mastery, and then offered them all a new, more difficult, and infinitely more rewarding path. He had their attention. All of it.

"Alright," Elian said, clapping his hands together, the sound echoing in the silent hall. "The Iron Assembly thinks craftsmanship is a science. We are about to remind the world that it is, and always has been, an act of magic. Lesson one: Finding the Soul."

That's a fantastic scene. It's the perfect "show, don't tell" moment for Elian's teaching philosophy. To the Queen and the Duke, it looks absurd. To the artisans, it's about to be a profound, foundational lesson. It highlights the gap between the world of politics and the world of true creation.
