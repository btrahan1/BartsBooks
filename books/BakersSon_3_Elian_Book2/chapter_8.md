## Chapter 8: The First Piece

The silence in the workshop was thick with hesitation. The masters looked at each other, the sheer, unprecedented nature of the "homework" overwhelming them. How could a weaver's thread harmonize with a potter's clay? How could a smith's iron support a woodworker's design without dominating it?

Mistress Elara, the old weaver, raised a thin, hesitant hand. "Master Elian, the logistics of such a project... coordinating workshops, acquiring materials..."

"That was not a question! Put your hand down, Weaver!" Elian's voice was a sharp crack, startling them all. He was not here to debate logistics. He was here to break them out of their old, rigid thinking.

He sighed, running a hand through his hair. He had pushed them too far, too fast. He had forgotten the most important lesson. He had to show them the grain in their own souls before they could find it in their work.

"Okay," he said, his voice softening, becoming less of a commander and more of a teacher again. "First, let's take a step back."

He looked at each of them, his gaze seeing not the guild master, but the young, hopeful apprentice they had once been.

"I want each of you to go back to your shop. Forget the alliance. Forget the prototypes. Forget everything I've said for the last hour."

A wave of relief seemed to pass through the room.

"Instead," he continued, "I want you to create the first thing you ever made as an apprentice. The very first project your own master assigned you. A simple knife. A lopsided pot. A poorly woven sash. I want you to make it exactly as you first made it, with all its flaws and imperfections. I want you to remember the feeling of your hands, the hope, the frustration."

He let that sink in, forcing them to walk back down the long road of their own histories.

"Then," he said, his voice dropping, becoming a low, intense command, "I want you to make a new one. The same object. But this time... I want you to put your *heart* into it. I want you to pour every lesson from the garden, every ounce of your decades of skill, every memory of why you fell in love with your craft in the first place, into that one, simple piece. I want you to put your *soul* into it."

He looked at them, his eyes burning with a craftsman's passion.

"Don't impress me," he said. "Don't impress the Queen. Impress *yourselves*. Remember who you are."

He pointed a finger at the door. "You have one day. The results will be presented here tomorrow at noon."

He turned his back on them, a clear and final dismissal.

"GET OUT!" he roared, the sound echoing in the rafters. He then added, his voice a low, almost pleading whisper they could barely hear. "And don't disappoint me."

The masters filed out of the workshop, not in a grumble of protest, but in a state of stunned, profound introspection. They had come expecting a lesson in business strategy. They had been given a quest to find their own lost souls. The homework was no longer an assignment; it was a pilgrimage.

That's a fantastic opening line for the next scene. It's so perfectly Elian—gruff, demanding, but also deeply insightful. He's not asking for a technical report; he's asking for a *story*. He's forcing them to connect their craft to their personal narrative, which was the entire point of the exercise.

This is the "thesis defense" for his unconventional class, and he's making it clear that passion and purpose are just as important as technical skill.
