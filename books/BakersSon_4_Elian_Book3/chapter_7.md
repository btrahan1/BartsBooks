## Chapter 7: The Unpredictable Ground

The "School of Fish" doctrine for the archers was being drilled relentlessly, the results even more devastatingly effective than Valerius had imagined. The infantry, under the "Fists of Freedom" model, had become a fluid, adaptable force.

But one piece of the grand design remained stubbornly, frustratingly flawed.

"Cavalry," Elian grumbled, pacing the floor of the Royal War Room. He stared at the massive map, at the pins representing the heavy horse units. "They need work."

The Duke of Corvan, himself a former cavalry commander, raised an eyebrow. "Our heavy cavalry is the finest in the known world, Master Elian. They are a hammer, meant to shatter the enemy line."

"A hammer is a clumsy tool," Elian retorted, not breaking his stride. "It only works if the nail is held steady. You cannot hammer a moving target. They too need to flow. But the ground... the ground is unpredictable. It is treacherous. A hidden ditch, a patch of soft mud, and your 'hammer' shatters itself. What can we do?"

He was thinking aloud, grappling with a problem of physics and terrain. He needed a way to make the most rigid, powerful, and inflexible unit of the army as fluid as the archers.

He stopped his pacing and his sharp, analytical gaze landed on a young, ramrod-straight officer standing by the map table. The man's uniform marked him as the First Officer of the Queen's Royal Lancers.

"You there!" Elian's voice was a sharp command. "First Officer. Explain your basic cavalry tactics to me. Now."

The officer, a young nobleman named Ser Petyr, was taken aback by the direct, unceremonious order from the Queen's consort. He glanced at the Queen, who simply gave a slight, encouraging nod.

"Sir," Petyr began, his voice crisp and formal. "The Royal Lancers operate on the principle of overwhelming force. We form a wedge. We build momentum. We charge. The goal is to break the enemy's center with a single, decisive impact."

"A single, straight line," Elian mused, a critical look on his face. "And what happens if the 'center' moves? Or if the ground is broken?"

"We... we reform and attempt the charge again, sir," Petyr admitted. "Or we are forced to engage in a melee, where our lances are less effective."

"Inefficient," Elian declared. "You are relying on a single, perfect condition. You are fighting the grain of the battlefield itself."

He began to pace again, the gears of his mind turning. "A hammer... no. Not a hammer. You are not a hammer."

He thought of the forest. He thought of how a wild boar, a creature of immense weight and power, could crash through the thickest underbrush, its path a whirlwind of unstoppable force. It didn't rely on a clear field. It made its own path.

"You are a saw," Elian said suddenly, stopping and looking at the young officer.

"Sir?"

"A rip saw," Elian clarified, his eyes alight with the new vision. "A rip saw does not shatter a log. It cuts *with* the grain, separating the fibers. It is fast. It is relentless. It does not stop."

He grabbed a piece of charcoal and went back to the map. He didn't draw a wedge. He drew a series of flowing, interlocking lines.

"Your cavalry will no longer charge as a single wedge," he announced. "They will advance in waves. Smaller, more agile units. The first wave does not break the line; it *finds the grain*. It probes for the weakest point, the gap in the shield wall. The moment it finds it, it does not stop. It punches through, and the waves behind follow, flowing through the gap like water through a crack in a dam."

He drew arrows showing the units peeling off, attacking the flanks, circling back.

"You will not be a hammer that strikes once," he declared. "You will be a saw that cuts continuously, relentlessly, until the entire structure of the enemy army has been ripped apart."

The First Officer stared at the map, his mind reeling. He was a student of the great cavalry treatises, of a thousand years of military doctrine. And this woodworker had just, in the space of five minutes, rendered all of it obsolete.

"But... the ground, sir," Petyr finally managed. "A single unit could still be lost to a hidden obstacle."

"Ah," Elian said, a small, cunning smile on his face. "But that is where the real work begins. We do not just change the tactic. We change the tool."

That's the final, brilliant piece of the puzzle. He's not just redesigning each unit in isolation; he's creating a fully integrated, symbiotic war machine. The archers aren't just for killing; they're a support system, a tool that enables the cavalry's new, more complex role.

This is Elian's masterpiece: an army built with the same principles of harmony, balance, and interconnectedness as one of his finest creations.
