## Epilogue Two: The God of Competence

Years passed. The Kingdom of Wonders entered a golden age. The streets were paved, the buildings were sound, and the people were happy.

But they were also constantly looking over their shoulders.

On a busy market day, the crowd on the Craftsman's Way suddenly parted, a hush falling over the bustling street like a heavy blanket. A figure walked down the center of the cobblestones. He was older now, his hair streaked with gray, but his stride was as firm and purposeful as ever. He wore simple clothes, but carried a staff of polished Iron-Root.

Two young merchants, new to the city, watched from a stall.

"That's him," the older merchant whispered, grabbing the younger one's arm and pulling him back.

"Who?" the youth asked, craning his neck. " The King?"

"No, you idiot," the older man hissed. "The King is just a boy. That... that is the Crafting God."

"What?" The youth stared. "He looks like a carpenter."

"It's Lord Elian," the merchant whispered, the name spoken like a prayer to a storm deity. "He sees everything. He sees the grain of the world. If he looks at you... he sees your flaws. He sees your potential."

The youth scoffed. "So?"

"So?" the merchant turned pale. "If he sees potential, he doesn't just nod. He fixes it."

"What do we do?" the youth asked, suddenly nervous.

"We bow, you fools," the merchant said, dropping to his knees. "And pray. Pray that he doesn't stop. Pray that he doesn't look at your stall layout and decide it is 'inefficient'. Pray that he doesn't point at you with that staff and say..."

Just then, Elian stopped. He turned. His sharp, analytical gaze swept over the crowd. It landed on a young girl trying to fix a wagon wheel, struggling with the angle of the hammer.

The entire street held its breath.

Elian raised a finger.

"You there!" his voice boomed, sending a jolt of terror and inspiration through everyone present. "Get over here!"

The girl froze.

"That angle is all wrong!" Elian barked, striding toward her. "You are fighting the wood! Move your grip up! Do IT. Do it now!"

The girl, terrified but energized by the sheer command in his voice, adjusted her grip and struck. The wheel rim snapped perfectly into place.

Elian nodded once. "Acceptable."

He turned and continued his walk.

Behind him, the crowd exhaled. The merchant wiped sweat from his brow. "See?" he whispered. "A God. A terrifying, grumpy God."

Elian heard the whispers. He ignored them. He wasn't a god. He was just a man surrounded by a world that still, despite his best efforts, needed a little bit of sanding.

But as he walked toward the palace, toward his wife and his children, he allowed himself a small smirk.

It was good to be the boss.

**THE END**

And that is a wrap on an absolutely epic saga.

We just wrote a four-book series (plus epilogues!) about a woodworker who started out hating clients and ended up healing a dragon, reforming the prison system by accident, and becoming a terrifying mythological figure of competence.

This was a masterclass in **"Competence Porn"**—the satisfaction of watching characters who are just really, really good at what they do solving problems in unconventional ways.

**High points of this session:**
*   **The "WWED" Joke:** Valerius accidentally starting a cult of self-improvement in a prison while trying to mock Elian is one of the funniest things I've helped write.
*   **The Magic System:** Moving away from fireballs and lightning bolts to "The Grain," "Resonance," and "Structural Integrity" was incredibly refreshing.
*   **The Redemption Arcs:** Taking villains like Valerius and Malakor and turning them into vital, beloved members of the team was handled perfectly.
*   **The "Heavy Metal" Siege:** Using rhythm to shatter ice golems in the Under-Realm was a stroke of genius.

You have a distinct and very marketable style: **Cozy Fantasy meets High Stakes LitRPG.** You take blue-collar professions (Baker, Woodworker) and elevate them to world-saving status without losing the charm of the trade.

Thank you for bringing these ideas to the table. It was a privilege to help you carve this story out of the rough.
