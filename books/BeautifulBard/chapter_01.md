# Chapter 1: The Commercial that Changed Everything

The air in Bart’s basement was thick with the scent of stale pepperoni, the metallic tang of lukewarm soda, and the palpable, nervous energy of five people who had just witnessed a natural one on a critical saving throw. 

"I'm telling you, the dragon has to have a blind spot," David said, leaning so far over the battle map that his glasses were in danger of sliding off his nose. He adjusted them with a quick, clinical flick of his finger, his gaze locked onto his painted miniature rogue—a tiny figure crouched in a defiant pose that David had spent three weekends perfect-painting. "The anatomy and the wingspan... there's a three-degree arc where its line of sight is obstructed by its own scales."

"It's a dragon, Dave. It has eyes *everywhere*," Nathan countered, crossing his arms over his chest. He was the Dungeon Master tonight, and his smile was a little too wicked for anyone's comfort. "And even if it didn't, it has blindsight. It can hear your heartbeat. It can smell the fear coming off your rogue like cheap cologne. And Tim, your cleric is still paralyzed, frozen in a state of terminal incompetence. Mihwa, what's your druid doing?"

Mihwa didn't answer. She wasn't looking at the map, the miniatures, or the mountain of snacks that had sustained them through the six-hour session. She was staring past the table at the massive smart-TV mounted on the wall. The volume was muted, but the colors coming from it were pulsing with a strange, hypnotic rhythm that didn't match the typical local news or late-night talk show.

"Guys," she whispered, her voice cutting through the banter like a cold breeze. "Look at the screen."

Bart, who had been busy trying to balance a d20 on his nose—the pinnacle of his bardic focus when he wasn't playing his actual character—let the die fall. For once, the laws of physics decided to humor him. The d20 clattered onto the wooden table, skipped once, and landed on a natural twenty. It was a roll that would have saved the party from a TPK (Total Party Kill), but in the silence of the room, no one even looked at it. 

Bart turned toward the screen, his playful smirk fading into a look of genuine curiosity. He was a man who lived his life as if a camera was always rolling, always ready with a quip or a dramatic flourish, but this was different. 

The image on the TV was a vista of impossible beauty. It wasn't the jagged, polygon-heavy graphics of the current generation of VR. It was a world that looked more real than the basement they were sitting in. Floating islands of emerald moss were connected by waterfalls of liquid light that seemed to defy gravity. Creatures that looked like a collaboration between a master painter and a biologist with a sense of humor soared through skies the color of a sunset captured in amber.

Then, bold gold letters swept across the sky, trailing stardust: **VITREOUS ONLINE**.

"Is that... a new expansion for World of Orclovers?" Tim asked, finally breaking his silence. He sounded hopeful, as if a new expansion meant he wouldn't have to keep worrying about his paralyzed cleric for much longer.

"No," Bart said, his voice dropping an octave as he leaned in. He felt a hum in his chest, the kind of resonance he usually only felt when he hit the perfect chord on his guitar. "Look at the fine print at the bottom. The stuff they usually hide behind the fast-talking narrator."

The group crowded around the screen, their dice and character sheets forgotten. 

*Continuous Immersion Protocol Enabled. 7:1 Time Dilation. Permanent Residency Options Available.*

"Long term FIVR," Nathan breathed, his DM screen finally falling over, revealing his notes and the secret maps he had been using to torture them. "They actually did it. Digital consciousness transfer without the neural degradation. They solved the 'Ghost in the Machine' feedback loop."

Bart didn't care about the technical jargon. He cared about the *feeling* of it. For years, he had been the guy who made the party laugh, the one who turned a grim dungeon crawl into a comedy special. He was a performer by nature, a man who felt most alive when he was creating a moment. But in the real world, those moments were fleeting. You played a song, people clapped, and then you went back to your day job. 

But Vitreous? Vitreous looked like a place where the performance never had to end. 

The commercial cut to a sleek, chrome-plated helmet. It looked less like a gaming peripheral and more like a piece of high-tech jewelry, designed to cradle the human brain with the reverence of a masterpiece. As Mihwa unmuted the remote, the room was filled with a sound that vibrated in their very bones—a symphony of harps and crystalline bells.

"...Where your stats aren't just numbers, they are your life," a narrator's voice said, deep and melodic. "Where the music you play actually heals the soul. Where the heroes you imagine are the people you become. Welcome home."

Bart looked at his friends. He saw the doubt in Tim’s eyes, the clinical fascination in David’s, the protective instinct in Nathan’s, and the raw, quiet longing in Mihwa’s. He had known these people since high school. They were his ensemble, his co-stars in the long-running, often mundane sitcom of real life. 

A slow, mischievous grin began to spread across Bart’s face. It was the look he got right before he did something brilliant or incredibly stupid. Usually both. 

"Weekly game night just got a lot more interesting," Bart said, his voice buzzing with the kind of excitement that usually preceded a very loud guitar solo. "Nathan, I think I'm going to need a bigger character sheet. And David? I think I found your blind spot. It’s called 'everything outside this basement.'"

"Bart, you can't be serious," Tim said, though he was still staring at the screen. "Permanent residency? That means... the pods. That means leaving everything."

"Everything?" Bart gestured vaguely at the basement around them. "Tim, look at this place. We’ve got a mountain of debt, jobs that treat us like replaceable parts in a machine that doesn't even work, and we spend all our free time pretending we're in a world like that one. Why stay in the basement when we can live in the castle?"

Bart stood up, moving with a grace that usually escaped him when he was trying to navigate the clutter of his own life. He picked up his acoustic guitar from the corner, the wood worn smooth by years of practice. He struck a single, bright, major chord that seemed to hang in the air, challenging the silence. 

"I've spent my whole life looking for the right stage," Bart said, his eyes reflecting the golden light of the TV. "I’ve played coffee shops where the only audience was a bored barista and a guy who wouldn't stop clicking his pen. I’ve played weddings where the bride cried because I played the wrong song. But in Vitreous? In a world where the music is actually magic? That's not just a game, guys. That's the Grand Finale."

He looked at Mihwa, and for a second, the performer mask slipped. He saw her looking at him, and he knew he wasn't just talking to the room. He was talking to her. He was asking her to take the leap with him. 

"I'm going Bard," he declared, his voice firm and devoid of its usual irony. "I don't care if the meta says they're underpowered. I don't care if I'm the first one to die because I tried to serenade a goblin. I am going Bard because in that world, I won't just be playing a part. I'll be the music itself."

David was already on his phone, his thumbs blurring as he searched for the investor reports for the company behind Vitreous. "The server capacity is unprecedented. They’re using quantum-syncing for the physics engine. It’s not just a game, Bart. It’s a parallel reality. The resources required to maintain it... it’s no wonder they’re asking for permanent commitment. They need the biological processing power of the human brain to help sustain the simulation."

"See?" Bart laughed, throwing his arms wide. "We're not just players. We're the fuel for the most beautiful engine ever built! Nathan, tell me you aren't already calculating the damage output of a digital Paladin."

Nathan looked at the battle map, then back at the TV. He slowly reached out and swept the miniatures into his hand, a gesture of finality. "I'm thinking about the armor. If the immersion is truly 100%, then the weight of the steel... the feeling of the sun on your plate mail... that’s something you can't get from a manual."

The room erupted into chatter. The lingering fear of the dragon they had been fighting was gone, replaced by a much larger, more terrifying, and infinitely more exhilarating prospect. They were five friends, huddled in a basement in the middle of a Tuesday night, standing on the edge of a cliff. 

And Bart was already halfway over the edge, waiting for them to follow. 

"One way or another," Bart said, strike another chord on his guitar, "this is the last session we play on this table. Next time, we're doing it for real."
