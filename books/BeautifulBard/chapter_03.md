# Chapter 3: The Protector

Nathan was still holding the character minis in his palm, the plastic clinking softly as he sat back in his oversized DM chair. He had spent the better part of the last decade being the architect of their adventures, the impartial god who balanced the scales of fate. But tonight, the scales had been shattered by a commercial and a dream of permanent immersion.

"Nathan. You're up," Bart said, breaking the silence with a sudden, sharp clarity. He leaned across the table, his acoustic guitar resting against his thigh. "You use AI every freaking day, man. We need you to game-theory the hell out of this."

Nathan looked up, his eyes narrowing behind his glasses. He was a senior machine learning architect at a firm that specialized in predictive behavioral modeling. His entire professional life was spent looking at data, finding patterns, and teaching machines how to anticipate human irrationality. 

"It’s not just about the rules, Bart," Nathan said slowly, his analytical mind already beginning to dissect the commercial’s promises. "The fine print mentioned 'traditional D&D rules,' but that’s a marketing hook. They can’t use the 5e SRD verbatim without paying a massive licensing fee to Hasbro, and a company building a persistent FIVR world isn't going to want to be beholden to someone else's IP for their core engine."

"Exactly!" Bart snapped his fingers. "Copyright stuff and all that. It’ll be 'traditional' the way a veggie burger is 'traditional.' It looks like the real thing, but it’s got its own chemical composition. I need you to work your magic and figure the min-max stuff. If we’re going in for the long haul—seven lifetimes for every real-world week—we can't afford to be suboptimal."

Nathan stood up and walked to the wall-mounted white board where he usually tracked initiative and hit points. He wiped it clean with a single, aggressive swipe of an eraser and began to draw a grid. This was his "thinking space," and the rest of the group knew to stay quiet when the markers started squeaky-dancing across the surface.

"Seven to one," Nathan muttered, writing the ratio at the top in bold, black letters. "Let's assume the neurological sync is as clean as they claim. That’s not just a speed-up; it’s a compression of experience. In the real world, your brain processes information at a certain rate because of biological constraints. In Vitreous, they’re bypassing the sensory organs and feeding data directly into the neural pathways. The 7:1 ratio is likely the limit of human cognitive plasticity before the subjective experience begins to fragment."

He drew five circles on the board, representing the party. "If the rules are 'traditional but not quite,' then they’ve likely mapped the six core stats—Strength, Dex, Con, Int, Wis, Cha—to specific neural feedback loops. Strength isn't just a number; it’s a recalibration of your perceived muscle tension. Intelligence is likely a boost in working memory and recall speed. But Charisma..." He looked at Bart. "Charisma is the wild card. In a world where your brain *is* the engine, the force of your personality might be the only stat that isn't capped by the game’s hardware."

"See? I told you!" Bart laughed, though there was a hint of apprehension beneath his bravado. "Charisma is the God Stat. And I'm the God of Charisma."

"Don't get ahead of yourself," Nathan warned, pointing his marker at Bart. "If Charisma is uncapped, it means the enemies can use it against you, too. A dragon with a high enough Charisma score wouldn't just breathe fire; it could literally dominate your willpower with a single roar. That brings me to the min-maxing."

Nathan began scribbling formulas and class names. "David, you want Rogue. If the physics are 1:1, stealth isn't about a Stealth check; it’s about understanding the lighting engine and the AI's detection algorithms. You have to optimize for Agility and Perception. Mihwa, Druid relies on Wisdom, which in this system is likely tied to environmental awareness and predictive intuition. You'll be our radar."

He turned back to his own circle. "And then there’s me. The DM who’s hanging up his hat."

"You already said Paladin," Tim reminded him, leaning forward. "Why the sudden game-theory doubt?"

"Because if the rules are different, the role of a tank is different," Nathan explained. "In a tabletop game, I can use a 'Taunt' ability to force an enemy to attack me because the rules say they have to. In a 7:1 immersion world where the enemies have tactical AI? A 'Taunt' isn't a button you press. It’s a psychological manipulation. I have to *make* them want to kill me. I have to be the most annoying, visible, and unkillable target on the field."

He wrote **OATH OF THE SENTINEL?** under his circle. "If I go Paladin, I’m not just a guy with a shield. I’m the group's point of failure. If I die, you all become vulnerable to a 7:1 psychological trauma event. If the game is permanent residency, 'dying' in Vitreous might not just mean a respwan timer. It could mean neural cascade failure."

The room went silent again. The weight of the 7:1 ratio was starting to sink in. Seven weeks of life compressed into one real-world day. Seven weeks of hunger, pain, fear, and triumph. 

"Nathan," Mihwa said softly. "You're scaring us. Is that part of the game theory?"

Nathan looked at her, then back at his friends. He saw the trust they were placing in him. He had been their DM for years, the one who kept the story moving and made sure everyone had fun, even when the dice were being cruel. 

"It's not about fear," Nathan said, his voice regaining its composure. "It's about the Protector mindset. I’ve spend years throwing monsters at you to see how you’d handle them. I’ve analyzed every build you’ve ever made, every mistake you’ve ever made in a dungeon. I know your weaknesses better than you do."

He capped his marker and looked at the miniatures in his hand. He placed the Paladin miniature—a figure in heavy, gothic armor—on the center of the battle map. 

"Bart is right," Nathan continued. "The rules won't be exact. They’ll be designed to maximize 'engagement' and 'retention.' That usually means a steeper difficulty curve than what most players are used to. They want you to struggle so the success feels more real. But I’m not going to let you struggle more than necessary. I'll take the Paladin. I'll max out Constitution and Charisma. I’ll be the beacon and the shield."

"And the AI part?" David asked. "How does that help you?"

"I’m going to spend the next forty-eight hours running simulations," Nathan said, his eyes already flickering with the light of a man who was planning a multi-threaded attack on a server. "I have access to several proprietary LLMs and predictive engines at the office. I can feed them the commercial’s metadata, the company’s patent filings, and the leaked technical specs from the beta testers. I won't just figure out the rules, David. I’ll figure out the *intent* of the creators. I’ll find the shortcuts they tried to hide."

Bart grinned, a genuine, wide-eyed look of admiration. "I knew we picked you for a reason, Nathan. You’re our very own tactical genius."

"Just remember," Nathan said, his tone turning serious as he looked Bart in the eye. "When we’re in there, and the time dilation kicks in, a single second of hesitation is seven seconds of reality. We don't have the luxury of out-of-game discussions once we dive. We have to be a unit. We have to be the masterpiece you keep talking about, Bart."

Bart stood up and extended his hand. One by one, the others stood up and placed their hands over his in the center of the table. 

"Vitreous Online is waiting," Bart said, his voice vibrating with the thrill of the premiere. "And we’ve got the best protector in the business."

Nathan felt the warmth of their hands, a physical anchor that he knew he would soon lose. He was the one who understood the risks better than anyone—the neural strain, the psychological drift, the sheer scale of a world lived at seven times the speed of light. But he also knew that he couldn't let them go without him. 

He was the Protector. And his game had just begun.
