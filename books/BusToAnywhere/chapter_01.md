# Chapter 1: The Idle Metric

Ben stared at the gray fabric of the seat ahead of him. It smelled of stale dust, diesel fumes, and someone else's cigarettes. The Greyhound had been humming that same low, vibrational note for six hours—a steady D-flat drone that usually helped him meditate. 

But today, his mind was too loud.

*Time Off Task.* That was the phrase that kept looping. *TOT.*

He closed his eyes, his hands resting lightly on his thighs, palms up. He tried to find his center, the *Dan Tien*, just below the navel. He summoned the memory of Master Lee’s voice, dry and crinkled as old parchment. 

*"Movement is not speed, Benjamin. Speed is panic. Flow is arrival."*

Ben had arrived at the fulfillment center three years ago. It was a cathedral of chaos, a sprawling temple to modern logistics—miles of conveyor belts screaming, scanners beeping, and the terrified panting of humans trying to outrun algorithms.

He didn't run. He flowed.

When a fifty-pound box of kitty litter came crashing down the slide, Steve in the next lane would grunt, heave, and torque his lower back, wasting precious energy. Ben just... harmonized with it. A slight pivot of the right hip, a shifting of weight from the back foot to the front, and the box was on the pallet. No grunting. No wasted breath. Just the soft *thud* of perfect placement.

For the first year, he was a god to the floor supervisors. His stow rates were 300% of the station average. He turned the frantic scramble of "Peak Season" into a Tai Chi form. *Grasp the Sparrow’s Tail* became *Grasp the 12-Pack of Soda*. *Part the Wild Horse’s Mane* became *Sort the Fragile Electronics*.

But he got too good.

He remembered the day it turned. It was a Tuesday. He had cleared his entire lane. The chute was empty. The pallet was wrapped, tagged, and staged. Perfection.

So, he did what he had done every day since he was twelve. He stood. He dropped his shoulders, unlocked his knees, and entered *Zhan Zhuang*—standing meditation. He was recharging his battery, letting the chi settle so he could attack the next rush with the same precision.

Then came the tap on the shoulder.

It wasn't a person; it was a clipboard with a person attached to it. Gary. A manager who had never lifted a box in his life and wore a vest that was far too clean.

"Ben. The system flagged you."

"Flagged me?" Ben didn't break his stance. His breathing remained diaphragmatic. "The lane is clear, Gary."

"Your scanner has been inactive for fourteen minutes. That's fourteen minutes of TOT. Time Off Task."

"I finished the work. I'm waiting for the line to catch up."

"The algorithm doesn't know you finished. It just knows you stopped moving." Gary tapped the tablet screen aggressively, as if he could poke a hole in the logic. "Stillness is theft, Ben. We pay for activity. If you're not scanning, you're not working."

They didn't care that he practiced Tae Kwon Do in the break room to keep his hamstrings loose. They didn't care that his meditation made him the only picker on the floor who hadn't filed a workman's comp claim for a slipped disc. They only cared that the blue bar on the graph had stopped moving.

Fired for being efficient enough to rest.

The irony tasted like copper in his mouth. He had fifty dollars in his pocket and a backpack with two changes of clothes and a worn copy of the *Tao Te Ching*. He had walked up to the ticket agent two towns over and asked for the furthest point fifty bucks would buy.

The bus gears ground down, shifting lower. The drone changed pitch, jolting him out of the memory. The air brakes hissed—a long, weary sigh.

"End of the line for this ticket," the driver’s voice crackled over the intercom, sounding like gravel in a blender. "Pecos. This is your stop."

Ben opened his eyes. All that empty efficiency was behind him now. Outside the grease-streaked window, the world was brown, dusty, and vast. 

It looked like a place that had plenty of work for a man who knew how to move.
