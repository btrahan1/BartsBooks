# Chapter 2: The Flow of Feed

Pecos, Texas, did not smell like diesel or floor wax. It smelled of baked earth, sagebrush, and something sharper—like hot metal cooling in the shade.

Ben stepped off the bus and felt the heat settle on his shoulders like a heavy wool blanket. The air was dry enough to crack a lip in ten minutes. He adjusted his backpack, checked his wallet—forty-two dollars remaining after a vending machine breakfast—and started walking.

He didn't know what he was looking for, but he knew the vector. He needed density. In logistics, density meant opportunity.

He found it three blocks down Main Street, past a faded cinema and a diner with a "Closed for Remodel" sign that looked ten years old. It was a sprawling metal building with a corrugated tin roof that shimmered in the noon sun. 

*Abernathy’s Feed & Farm Supply.*

The sensory data hit him before he even crossed the street. The chaotic geometry of the front lot made his left eye twitch. stacks of potting soil were leaning precariously to the south. Rolls of chicken wire were tangled like a bad hair day. It was a system shouting for alignment.

But the real signal was the handwritten cardboard sign tapped to the glass door:
*HELP WANTED. Must lift 50lbs. No whiners.*

Ben pushed the door open. A bell jingled, announcing his arrival into a cooler, dimmer world that smelled of sweet molasses, grain dust, and rubber tired.

"I ain't buying nothing today, salesman!" a voice barked from the back.

Ben followed the sound down the center aisle, navigating around a haphazard display of gardening gloves. He found an older man—seventy, perhaps—wrestling with a manual pallet jack. The man wore a stained trucker hat and a plaid shirt clinging to his back with sweat.

The pallet jack was loaded with fifty-pound bags of horse feed, and it wasn't moving. The man heaved, his boots slipping on the concrete dust.

"I'm not selling anything," Ben said, his voice quiet. "I'm here for the help."

The man stopped and leaned against the handle, wheezing slightly. He looked Ben up and down—taking in the slim build, the clean hands, the lack of work boots.

"You?" The man scoffed. "Son, this pallet weighs twelve hundred pounds. It’s stuck on a damn rock or a crack, and my back is screaming louder than my ex-wife. You look like you’d blow away in a stiff breeze."

Ben didn't take offense. He stepped forward. "May I?"

The man grunted and stepped aside, wiping his forehead with a red handkerchief. "Be my guest. Don't pop a vessel."

Ben approached the pallet. He didn't grab the handle. First, he looked.

He saw the vector. The left fork wheel was indeed caught on a lip of uneven concrete. Pulling straight back, as the man had been doing, only wedged it tighter. It was a physics problem.

Ben let his breath out. *Sinking the Chi.* He widened his stance, dropping into a shallow horse stance that looked casual but rooted him to the floor.

He placed one hand on the stack of feed to stabilize it. He placed his other hand on the pallet jack handle, not gripping it, but resting against it. He visualized the energy traveling from the floor, through his legs, rotating at the waist.

He didn't pull. He *spiraled*.

A sharp, explosive twist of his hips sent a shockwave of force into the handle. The jack hopped—just an inch—laterally. 

*Clack.*

The wheel cleared the lip.

Ben exhaled, stood up, and pulled the handle gently. The twelve-hundred-pound load rolled forward smoothly, silent as a ghost.

He looked at the man. "Where do you want it?"

The man’s mouth was slightly open. "Shed out back. Loading bay two."

Ben nodded. He pushed the load, finding the rhythm. *Step, push. Step, push.* He didn't muscle it; he guided the momentum.

For the next hour, Ben didn't speak. He worked. 

The truck outside had three more pallets. Ben unloaded them. But he didn't just dump them. As he stacked the bags in the shed, he instinctively sorted them. *Sweet Feed* on the left, near the door for quick access. *All-Stock* in the middle. *Chicken Scratch* on the low pallets because the bags were prone to tearing.

He built a wall of grain. It was perfectly plumb, leveled, and interlocked for stability. It was beautiful.

At 1:45 PM, the truck was empty. The shed was organized. Ben swept the floor because leaving dust is inefficient—it reduces traction.

He finished. And then, the old habit kicked in.

He walked to the patch of shade near the bay door. He put his heels together, unlocked his knees, and stood. *Zhan Zhuang.* He closed his eyes, regulating his heart rate, letting the adrenaline drain away.

He heard boots crunching on gravel. The anxiety spiked. *TOT. I’m standing still. I’m not scanning.*

He opened his eyes. The older man—Mac, according to the embroidery on his shirt—was standing there, holding two glass bottles of Dr. Pepper.

Ben straightened up, ready to be fired. "I finished the truck, sir. I was just—"

"I know what you were doing," Mac said gruffly.

"I can find something else to sweep," Ben said quickly. "I didn't mean to be idle."

Mac stared at him. Then he looked at the shed. He looked at the perfect, impossible stack of feed bags. He looked at the clean floor.

"Idle?" Mac let out a short, sharp laugh. He extended one of the cold bottles toward Ben. "Son, you just did a four-hour job in sixty minutes, and you ain't even breathing hard. If that’s idle, I’ll take two of 'em."

Ben took the bottle. The glass was freezing against his palm. "I... I work fast so I can rest."

"That ain't rest," Mac said, taking a long pull of his soda. "That’s efficiency. My name's Mac. You got a name, or do I just call you 'The Crane'?"

"Ben," he said.

"Well, Ben," Mac gestured to an overturned bucket in the shade. "Sit your ass down. You’re on the clock till five, but you’re done working for now. We don't punish good work with more work around here."

Ben stared at the bucket. He looked at Mac’s crinkly, honest smile. 

He sat down. 

For the first time in three years, the clock was ticking, and he wasn't afraid.
