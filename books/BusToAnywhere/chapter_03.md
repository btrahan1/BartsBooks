# Chapter 3: Shelter and Sustenance

At 5:00 PM exactly, the rhythm of the feed store shifted. The dust motes seemed to hang suspended in the golden late-afternoon light coming through the bay doors.

Ben was sweeping the loading dock for the second time—not because it needed it, but because a clean perimeter prevented accidents.

Mac walked out of the office, wiping his hands on a rag. He reached into his back pocket and pulled out a leather wallet that looked like it had survived a war. He thumbed out three twenty-dollar bills and held them out.

"Sixty," Mac said. "Fifteen an hour for four hours. Cash for today. If you come back tomorrow, we’ll fill out the tax forms like honest citizens."

Ben took the money. It felt crisper, heavier somehow, than the digital deposit notifications he used to get from the fulfillment center. "I’ll be here at eight, sir."

"Seven-thirty," Mac corrected. "Coffee’s hot by seven-fifteen. You drink coffee?"

"Tea, mostly. But I can adapt."

Mac snorted. "Tea. Alright, The Crane. Now, you got a place to crash, or are you planning to meditate in my shed all night?"

"I don't have a place yet."

Mac pointed a calloused finger west, toward the edge of town where the highway bled into the desert. "The Starlight Motor Inn. It ain't the Ritz, and the neon sign buzzes like a pissed-off hornet, but Mrs. Gable changes the sheets every day and she don't tolerate drug dealers. Tell her Mac sent you. She might knock five bucks off."

Ben nodded, tucking the cash into his pocket. "Thank you."

***

The Starlight Motor Inn lived up to its name only in the sense that you could see plenty of stars through the holes in the awning. It was a U-shaped single-story structure painted a color that might have been turquoise in the 1970s but was now just 'faded'.

The lobby smelled of lemon polish and old carpet. Mrs. Gable was a tiny bird of a woman with glasses on a chain and a suspicious squint.

"Mac sent me," Ben said.

The squint relaxed by a millimeter. "Forty-five a night. No loud music, no guests after ten, and if you smoke in the room I keep your deposit."

"I don't smoke. And I don't know anyone to invite."

She gave him key number 12. "Ice machine is broken. Don't kick it."

Room 12 was sparse. A double bed with a polyester spread, a laminate dresser with a cigarette burn on the corner, and an air conditioner unit under the window that rattled with a rhythmic *thwack-thwack-thwack* of a loose fan blade.

Most people would have thrown their bag on the bed and turned on the TV to drown out the noise.

Ben dropped his backpack by the door. He walked to the AC unit. He listened. The vector of the sound was clear—the front grille was vibrating against the plastic housing.

He reached into his pack and pulled out a small travel kit. He didn’t have tools properly, but he had resourcefulness. He took the cardboard tag from his spare shirt, folded it into a dense wedge, and slid it precisely between the housing and the grille.

The *thwack-thwack-thwack* vanished. The room hummed with a smooth, steady white noise.

Satisfied, Ben unpacked. He placed his toothbrush and razor on the bathroom sink, parallel to each other. He took his two shirts and folded them into perfect squares in the drawer. He owned very little, but what he owned was now in order. 

The chaos of the road was gone. This was his grid.

***

Hunger finally pierced his focus. He walked back toward the town center, guided by the smell of grilled onions and cumin.

He found a food truck parked in the gravel lot of a closed gas station. *El Rey del Taco.* The line was three people deep.

Ben watched the ecosystem of the kitchen through the serving window. It was a husband and wife team. The man was on the grill, chopping carne asada with a frenetic, choppy rhythm. The wife was assembling tacos, taking money, and handing out drinks.

They were fast, but they were fighting each other.

*Inefficiency,* Ben noted automatically. *The salsa containers are on the far left, but the plating happens on the right. She crosses over him every time she needs a lid, forcing him to step back.*

He watched them dance their awkward, collision-prone dance. If they swapped the register to the left side and moved the salsa station... they could increase throughput by 20%.

He stopped himself. *Not your job, Ben. Just eat.*

When he got to the window, he ordered three tacos al pastor and a bottle of mineral water. He paid with one of Mac’s twenties.

He sat on a plastic crate turned upside down near the truck. He unwrapped the foil. The steam hit his face—pork, pineapple, cilantro, onion.

He took a bite. 

The flavor was explosive. Fatty, acidic, spicy, sweet. It was messy. It wasn't optimized. It wasn't a protein nutrient bar eaten during a 15-minute break.

It was real.

He ate slowly, savoring the texture. He watched the cars pass on the highway, their headlights cutting beams through the dust. He watched the stars coming out over the desert, clearer and brighter than he had ever seen them from the city.

He had sixty dollars minus the room and dinner. He had a job at 7:30 AM. He had a room where the AC didn't rattle.

Ben finished the last taco, wiped his hands carefully on a napkin, and took a sip of the cold water. He felt a sensation in his chest that he hadn't felt in years. 

It wasn't the flow of work. It was the flow of life.

And for tonight, that was enough.
