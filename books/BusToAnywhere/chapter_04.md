# Chapter 4: The Morning Maintenance

Ben woke at 5:30 AM without an alarm. His body was a clock, calibrated by years of shift work and circadian discipline.

The room was cool and silent, save for the steady, non-rattling hum of the air conditioner. He sat up, drank a glass of water, and spent twenty minutes stretching on the small patch of carpet between the bed and the wall.

He dressed in his second set of clothes—clean jeans and a grey t-shirt he had washed in the sink the night before and hung over the shower rod.

He stepped out into the pre-dawn air. The sky was a deep, bruised purple, bleeding into orange on the eastern horizon. The desert air was surprisingly cold.

Ben walked toward the lobby to see if the coffee was ready, but stopped at the vending alcove near the office. The ice machine sat there, a hulking beige monolith with a handwritten sign taped to the front: *OUT OF ORDER. DO NOT KICK.*

Ben looked at the machine. It was humming, but straining—a high-pitched whine that suggested a motor trying to turn against resistance.

He glanced at the lobby. Mrs. Gable wasn't at the desk yet. The lights were off.

Ben put his ear to the metal casing. He visualized the mechanism inside. Compressor. Condenser. Auger. The whine was coming from the auger motor.

He reached around the back. The machine was pushed almost flush against the brick wall, choking off its own airflow. The heat coming off the back was intense.

*Thermal overload,* Ben diagnosed. *The motor isn't broken; it's suffocating. It heated up, expanded, and seized.*

He gripped the side of the heavy machine. He rooted his feet. Using the leverage of his hips, he walked the machine six inches away from the wall.

He felt a wave of trapped heat escape.

Next, he looked at the intake vent at the bottom. It was caked with a thick grey felt of dust and tumbleweed debris. The machine was trying to breathe through a wool sweater.

Ben found a discarded plastic coffee stirrer in the trash can. He used it to gently peel the mat of dust away from the grille. It came off in a single satisfying sheet, like peeling a sunburn.

The airflow hissed, suddenly unobstructed.

Ben waited three minutes. As the coils cooled, the high-pitched whine dropped in pitch, then smoothed out into a rhythmic *chug-chug-chug*.

*Clatter.*

A cascade of ice cubes tumbled into the bin.

Ben smiled. He walked to the lobby door. He pulled it open, and it let out a shriek of dry metal hinges that echoed across the parking lot.

*Unacceptable.*

He checked his pocket. He had a small tube of lip balm. It wasn't WD-40, but it was a petroleum-based lubricant.

He utilized the coffee stirrer again to dig out a chunk of the balm. He applied it precisely to the pin of the top hinge, then the bottom. He worked the door back and forth three times.

*Screech... scrr... silence.*

The door now swung open with nothing but a whisper of displaced air.

"What are you doing to my door?"

Ben froze. He looked into the dim lobby. Mrs. Gable was standing there in a floral housecoat, holding a pot of coffee. She looked like a startled owl.

"Good morning, ma'am," Ben said. "The hinge was dry. I lubricated it."

Mrs. Gable blinked. She walked to the door and pushed it. It swung silently. She looked at Ben, then looked past him toward the vending alcove, where the sound of falling ice was now audible.

"Did you kick the ice machine?" she asked suspiciously.

"No, ma'am. I pulled it away from the wall and cleaned the intake. It was overheating."

Mrs. Gable walked outside. She stick her hand in the bin and pulled out a fresh, cold cube. She looked at the machine, then back at the silent door, then at Ben.

"You a mechanic?"

"No, ma'am. I just hear things."

She grunted. "Coffee is fresh. Don't take all the sugar."

She turned to go back inside, then stopped. "You said Mac sent you?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you got a job with him?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"You staying the week?"

"I'd like to, if the work holds up."

Mrs. Gable did mental math, her eyes narrowing. "Forty-five a night is three-fifteen a week with tax. I'll give it to you for two hundred flat. Pay upfront."

Ben did his own math. That was a savings of over 35%. "That sounds very fair. Thank you."

"Don't thank me," she snapped, "thank the door. I've been meaning to oil that thing since Easter."

She disappeared into the back office.

Ben poured himself a black coffee in a Styrofoam cup. He took a sip, listening to the quiet hum of the ice machine and the silence of the door.

The day hadn't even started, and already, the world was running a little smoother.
