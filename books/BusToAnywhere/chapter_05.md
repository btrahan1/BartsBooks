# Chapter 5: The Driver

The sun was already baking the asphalt by the time Ben walked down Main Street. He arrived at *Abernathy’s* at 7:25 AM, five minutes early for the "seven-thirty" shift, ten minutes late for the coffee.

But the scene in the parking lot wasn't right.

The Vector of the moment was chaotic. Violent.

A rusted-out pick-up truck—mid-90s Ford, primer grey on the passenger door—was idling crookedly across two spaces. The engine was missing on one cylinder, a sputtering *cough-cough-cough* that ruined the morning quiet.

And there were no license plates.

Ben’s brain cataloged the detail instantly. *Transient pattern. Remove plates to avoid identification on highway cameras. Hit small towns. Steal high-margin goods. Move on.*

Three men were walking fast from the store entrance toward the truck. They were young, edgy, carrying armfuls of power tools and coil hoses.

Mac was right behind them, moving faster than Ben would have thought possible for a man with a bad back.

"Hey!" Mac shouted, his voice cracking with rage. "You put that down! You pay for that!"

The tallest of the three—a guy in a torn tank top with a barbed-wire tattoo on his bicep—spun around. He didn't argue. He shoved.

It was a hard, dismissive push to the chest. Mac stumbled back, his boots catching on the gravel. He hit the ground hard, a puff of dust rising around him.

"Buzz off, old man!" the guy sneered. He turned back to the truck, laughing.

Something in Ben’s chest clicked. It was the same feeling as seeing the stuck pallet, but colder. *Disorder. Injustice. Bullying.*

"If there's two things I hate," Ben whispered to himself, "it's bullies and thieves."

He didn't run. Running was panic. He walked. smooth steps, eating up the distance between the street and the truck.

He arrived at the driver's side door just as the three men were throwing the stolen DeWalt drills into the bed. They hadn't noticed him yet. They were too busy high-fiving.

Ben stood perfectly still. *Zhan Zhuang.*

"Which one of you is the driver?" he asked. His voice wasn't loud, but it cut through the engine sputter.

The three men froze. They turned. They saw a skinny guy in a grey t-shirt standing with his hands at his sides, looking bored.

The one with the barbed wire tattoo—the Shover—stepped forward. He was big. Probably two-twenty. "What's it to you, twig?"

One of the others, a guy wearing a backwards cap, raised his hand with a smirk. "I'm driving. You need a ride to the hospital?"

Ben looked at him. He nodded once, acknowledging the data.

"It's your lucky day," Ben said.

"Huh?"

The Shover moved first. He wound up for a haymaker, a sloppy, telegraphed punch that started way back in Kansas.

Ben didn't block it. He simply wasn't there when the fist arrived. He pivoted on his left heel—forty-five degrees inside the arc.

He struck. Not with a fist, but with a Phoenix Eye knuckle—the middle knuckle protruded—driven precisely into the floating rib.

*Crunch.*

It wasn't a bone breaking. It was the sound of all the air leaving a human body instantly. Use of force: Level 3. Pain compliance.

The Shover folded in half, making a sound like a deflating tire.

The second guy—the one who hadn't spoken—lunged. Ben side-stepped, grabbed the man’s wrist, and utilized the man's own momentum. He pulled, twisted, and delivered a swift, stinging slap to the heavy muscle of the thigh (the common peroneal nerve).

The guy’s leg turned to jelly. He fell face-first into the gravel next to his friend.

Ben finished with a full palm strike to the guy’s shoulder blade as he went down, pinning him to the earth for a second just to make the point. He’d be seeing a handprint there for a week.

Silence returned to the parking lot, save for the wheezing of the Shover and the idling truck.

Ben turned to the Driver. The guy in the backwards cap was standing by the open door, his face pale, his keys dangling from a shaking hand.

"The only reason you're still standing," Ben said, his voice entirely flat, "is because someone needs to drive your sorry asses out of town."

He pointed a finger at the horizon. "Go."

The Driver scrambled. He essentially threw his friends into the cab, not caring about limbs or dignity. He jumped in, ground the gears, and the truck peeled out, spraying gravel before fishtailing onto the main road.

Ben watched them go until they were just a dust cloud.

Then, the adrenaline vanished. He walked over to Mac.

The old man was sitting up, dusting off his jeans. He looked at the truck, then at the two piles of recovered tools, then at Ben.

"You realize," Mac said, grunting as Ben offered a hand to pull him up, "that you’re terrifying, right?"

"I don't like bullies, sir."

Mac took his hand. His grip was firm. "Well. Remind me never to piss you off. Now help me get these drills back inside before someone else decides to go shopping."
