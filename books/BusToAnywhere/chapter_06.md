# Chapter 6: The Offer

By 11:45 AM, the store was silent.

The morning rush of contractors buying PVC fittings was over. The delivery truck from the coop had been unloaded, sorted, and inventoried. The floor was swept. The shelves in Aisle 4 (Pesticides and Poisons) had been reorganized by toxicity and bottle size, creating a pleasing visual gradient that probably violated no regulations but satisfied Ben’s soul.

Ben walked up to the front counter. Mac was sitting on a stool, staring at a fishing magazine, a half-eaten sandwich on a paper plate next to him.

"I'm done, Mac," Ben said.

Mac looked up. He looked at the clock on the wall—a plastic largemouth bass with a tail that wagged every second.

"It's noon," Mac said.

"Yes, sir. I checked the back lot. The fencing wire is restacked. I oiled the sliding gate on the bay door because it was grinding. I also consolidated the open bags of concrete mix into a sealed bin so they won't harden."

Mac stared at him. He closed the magazine slowly. He stood up and walked out from behind the counter. He took a lap around the store. He checked the shelves. He checked the back room. He even checked the bathroom (which Ben had scrubbed because he had ten spare minutes).

Mac came back, scratching his chin. He let out a long, heavy sigh.

"Ben," he said, his voice sounding tired. "There's just not enough work here to keep you busy."

Ben felt his stomach drop. It was the Fulfillment Center all over again. *You're too fast. You break the curve.*

He hung his head, looking at his clean hands. "I understand, sir. I can slow down if—"

"No, no," Mac waved a hand, cutting him off. "That ain't what I mean. I mean **I** can't keep you busy. I can't chase you around finding things for you to fix."

Mac leaned against the counter, the wood creaking under his weight. He looked at Ben, really looked at him, with a gaze that was shrewd and assessing.

"But I have an idea," Mac said.

Ben looked up. "Sir?"

"See, I'm an old man, Ben. I've been running this place since 1982. Every day. Six days a week. Sunday I do the books." Mac rubbed his lower back. "I want to do things! I want to go fishing. I want to see my grandkids in Dallas more often. Hell, I'd like to take my wife to Sunday Lunch now and then without worrying that the teenager I hired is selling copper wire out the back door."

Ben waited, listening.

"Truth is son," Mac said, waving his hand around the dusty, sunlit store, "I'm too old for this shit."

He locked eyes with Ben.

"Give me a vacation, Ben. Run this store for a month. You clearly know logistics. You know hard work. And after what I saw in the parking lot this morning, I know you can handle the... *element*."

"Run the store?" Ben asked. "You mean manage it?"

"I mean run it. Open to close. You order the stock, you handle the customers, you balance the till. I come in on Fridays to sign checks and drink a Dr. Pepper."

Mac paused, gauging Ben’s reaction. "And then, maybe longer. We'll see how the month goes. If you haven't burned it down or turned it into a Zen garden."

Mac stuck his hand out. It was a hand that felt like sandpaper and trust.

"One thousand dollars a week. Deal?"

Ben looked at the hand. A thousand dollars a week. That was real money. That was 'fix the Starlight Motor Inn' money. That was 'buy a truck' money.

But more than that, it was a system. A whole store. His to optimize. His to flow.

Ben took the hand.

"Deal."
