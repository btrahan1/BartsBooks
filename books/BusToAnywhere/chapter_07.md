# Chapter 7: The Tribal Knowledge

The hardest part wasn't the physical labor. Ben could move fifty-pound bags of alfalfa until the sun burned out.

The hardest part was downloading the operating system of *Abernathy’s Feed & Farm* from the only server where it existed: Mac’s brain.

For the next six days, Ben shadowed Mac like a forensic accountant at a crime scene.

"How do we know when to order more fencing wire?" Ben asked on Tuesday, holding a clipboard that Mac viewed with deep suspicion.

"I don't know," Mac grunted, taking a bite of a pickled egg. "I just look at the pile. If I can see the back wall, I call Jerry."

"Who is Jerry?"

"Jerry. The wire guy. Copied his number on the wall by the phone in '04."

Ben checked the wall. Under layers of dust and faded calendars, scrawled in permanent marker on the plaster itself, was: *JERRY - WIRE - 555-0192*.

"And if Jerry doesn't pick up?"

"Then I call his cousin Earl. But Earl drinks before noon, so check the time."

Ben wrote it all down. *Supplier: Jerry. Backup: Earl (Time Sensitive).*

It was an archeological dig. The "Inventory System" was a collection of coffee-stained receipts stuffed into a shoebox marked *2019*. The "Accounts Receivable" was a spiral notebook where Mac wrote down who owed him money, usually with notes like *'Bill says he's good for it after the harvest'* or *'Don't sell Sam any more fertilizer until he pays for the last batch.'*

Ben didn't try to change it all at once. That would cause system shock. Instead, he translated.

He took the shoebox and entered it into a ledger. He took the wall-scrawled numbers and created a rolodex. He verified the inventory counts and created reorder points based on math, not the visibility of the back wall.

Every afternoon, around 2:00 PM, they sat in the office with two cold Dr. Peppers. This was the data transfer protocol.

"Tell me about the Co-Op delivery," Ben would say.

" Drivers are good," Mac would reply, leaning back in his creaking chair. "Except for new kid, Steve. You gotta watch Steve. He tries to offload the broken bags on us. You tell him 'No rip, no slip' and he'll straighten up."

Ben noted it. *Quality Control: Monitor Steve.*

By Saturday, Ben’s clipboard was full. He knew the margins on dog food (slim) and the margins on specialized horse tack (massive). He knew that Mrs. Higgins came in every Thursday for birdseed and expected five minutes of conversation about her cats. He knew that the front door lock stuck if you didn't lift the handle up and to the left.

The store was still dusty, but the information flow was clean.

Saturday afternoon, the heat started to break. The air conditioner in the office rattled—until Ben jammed a folded matchbook under the casing, silencing it instantly.

Mac looked at him. "You got a fix for everything, don't you?"

"Only things that are broken, sir."

Mac looked at the ledger open on the desk. For the first time in ten years, he could see exactly how much cash he had, how much stock was on the floor, and who owed him what, all on one page.

He looked tired. Not the 'bad day' kind of tired, but the deep, bone-weary fatigue of a man who hasn't put his burden down in forty years.

Ben took a sip of his Dr. Pepper. He remembered the conversation from Monday.

"Grandkids in Dallas, huh?" Ben asked.

Mac blinked, pulled from his thoughts. "Yeah. Two of 'em. Little terrors. Cute, though."

"When are you leaving?"

Mac frowned. "Leaving? I ain't planned nothing yet. I gotta—"

"Mac," Ben said. He smiled wide, a genuine expression that crinkled the corners of his eyes. "The wire is ordered. Jerry picked up on the first ring. The broken bags are returned. The till balances to the penny."

He gestured to the store. "The system is stable."

Mac looked at the ledger. He looked at Ben. He looked at the fishing rod standing in the corner that hadn't seen water in three seasons.

"You really think you can handle Mrs. Higgins?" Mac asked, a twinkle of a challenge in his eye.

"I read up on cat breeds last night," Ben replied. "I'm ready."

Mac let out a laugh that sounded like a rusty gate finally opening. He slapped the desk.

"Monday," Mac said. "I'll leave Monday morning. If you burn the place down, call Earl. He knows a guy with a water truck."

"Deal," Ben said.

Mac stood up, grabbed his keys, and for the first time Ben could remember, he didn't check the lock on the safe. He just walked to the door.

"See you in a month, Crane."

"See you in a month, Mac."

The bell jingled. The door closed silently on its oiled hinges.

Ben was alone. He looked around the silent, dusty empire of feed and seed. He took a deep breath.

It was time to optimize.
