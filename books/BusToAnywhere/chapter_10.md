# Chapter 10: The Syntax of Home

The server was humming—a steady, reassuring whir that sat underneath the noise of the oscillating fan in Mac’s office.

Sarah was sitting in Mac’s chair, her legs tucked under her, fingers flying across a mechanical keyboard she had brought with her. The screen was a waterfall of SQL queries.

Ben was sitting on an overturned milk crate, watching the data populate.

"You index fast," Ben observed.

"I type fast," Sarah corrected, not looking up. "I index efficiently. There’s a difference."

She hit *Enter* with a flourish. "Okay, tables are normalized. The importer script is chewing through Mac’s spreadsheet now. Probably take twenty minutes."

She spun the chair around. "Feed me."

Ben handed her a foil-wrapped burger from *Pecos Pit BBQ*. It was the size of a hubcap and smelled of hickory smoke and grease. He had also remembered the extra pickles.

Sarah unwrapped it like it was a holy relic. She took a bite that defied the physics of her jawline.

"Mmmph," she groaned happily. "Okay. You pass the first test. This is acceptable currency."

Ben opened his own burger. He ate neatly, wiping his hands after every few bites. He watched her. The oversized glasses, the messy bun, the Mario Kart shirt that had a small grease stain on the hem. She didn't fit the vector of this town. Pecos was cowboy boots and oil rigs. She was Python scripts and mechanical switches.

"So," Ben said, keeping his tone casual. "You write SQL like a DBA. You solder like an engineer. And you negotiate like a pirate."

Sarah grinned around a fry. "Thank you."

"Why are you here, Sarah?"

The grin faltered, just for a microsecond. She put the burger down on the wrapper. She adjusted her glasses.

"I live here, Ben. Miller’s Electronics. Family business."

"I know," Ben said. "But you didn't learn normalization rules at Pecos High. And you didn't learn to salvage enterprise hardware at the local community college."

He took a sip of his tea. "You're overqualified. Just like the hardware you put in this box."

Sarah sighed. She picked at the foil wrapper.

"I was at UT Austin," she said quietly. "Computer Science. I was going to intern at Dell. I had a track, you know? Austin is... it’s where the flow is. The energy."

"What happened?"

"My dad," she said. "He’s a genius with a vacuum tube radio, but he thinks 'The Cloud' is something that ruins picnics. Three years ago, he broke his hip. I came back for the summer to run the shop while he rehabbed."

She looked at the humming server.

"I looked at the books, Ben. It was a disaster. He was losing money on every repair. He was ordering parts from catalogs instead of wholesale. So... I fixed it."

Ben nodded. He knew the feeling. You see a broken system, you fix it.

"I digitized the inventory," she continued. "I set up a repair ticket system. I started selling refurbished corporate salvage online to supplement the walk-in business. Revenue went up 40% in three months."

"That sounds like a success," Ben said.

"It was," Sarah said, a wry smile twisting her lips. "Too much success. Dad recovered, but he couldn't run the new systems. He didn't understand the POS software I built. He couldn't manage the eBay listings. I had built a machine that only I could pilot."

She looked at Ben. Behind the thick lenses, her eyes were a little sad.

"I tried to leave. I really did. But every time I packed a bag, I’d think about him staring at a screen he couldn't read, watching the business fail again. So I stayed. Just for another month. Then another."

She shrugged. "It’s been three years. I'm the Queen of Pecos Tech Support. And I’m stuck."

Ben looked at her. He saw the trap clearly. It was the Competence Trap. She was so good at solving the problem that she became the structural load-bearing wall of the solution.

"You're not stuck," Ben said softly.

"I feel stuck."

"You just haven't documented the exit protocol yet," Ben said. "You optimized the business for revenue, but you didn't optimize it for redundancy. You made yourself a single point of failure."

Sarah blinked. She stopped chewing. She stared at him.

"Oh my god," she whispered. "You're right. I deprecated the user."

Ben smiled. "We can fix that. That’s just a training vector."

Sarah picked up her burger again. The sadness was gone, replaced by a spark of engineering curiosity.

"You know," she said, pointing a fry at him. "For a guy who moves feed bags for a living, you talk a lot like a Systems Architect."

"I just hate waste," Ben said. "And wasting talent is the biggest sin of all."

Sarah smiled, and this time, it wasn't mischievous. It was warm.

"Eat your burger, Ben. The script is almost done."
