# Cultivator in NYC: Book 3 - Chapter 4: Back to the Basics

Central Park had always been Han Wei’s sanctuary, but today, it felt like a battlefield. Or perhaps, more accurately, a circus that was dangerously close to becoming a riot.

The sun was barely peeking over the skyscrapers of the Upper East Side, casting long, violet-tinted shadows across Sheep Meadow. In the early days, when Wei had first arrived in this strange, metal-clad world, he had come here to move his limbs in the quiet dawn. Back then, "Wei Chi"—as the regulars had jokingly called it—was just a curiosity. A few retirees, a couple of bored joggers, and the occasional stray dog had been his only audience. They had followed his slow, deliberate movements with a mixture of amusement and mild interest, treating it like a more athletic form of Tai Chi.

Today, the crowd was different. 

There were thousands of them. They were packed into the meadow, standing in ragged rows that stretched back toward the trees. Some were in designer athletic gear, others in jeans and hoodies, and a few had even shown up in crudely made robes that looked like they’d been stitched together from old bedsheets. Every single one of them was staring at Wei with a hunger that was almost physical.

They didn't want curiosity. They wanted power. They wanted that 0.1 increase they’d seen on their Status screens.

Wei stood on a small, portable stage that Guzman had insisted on hauling into the park. Jax was hovering ten feet above his head, the drone’s red light pulsing with the steady rhythm of a global heartbeat. Twenty million people were watching the live feed. 

"I remember when I first came to this grass," Wei began, his voice carrying clearly across the silent meadow without the need for a microphone. He didn't yell; he simply spoke from his center, the Qi carrying the sound like a ripple on a still pond. "There were four of us. We didn't have 'Status.' We didn't have a violet moon. We just had the breath in our lungs and the ground under our feet."

He began to move. It was the same kata he had performed a thousand times—the Azure Cloud Sect’s *Foundation Flow*. It was the most basic of the basic, a series of movements so simple that even the youngest Outer Disciple was expected to master them in a week.

He moved slowly. His arms traced wide, elegant arcs through the air, his feet sliding across the wood of the stage with a whisper-thin friction. He focused on his core, his Dantians, feeling the meridians in his arms and legs light up like glowing filaments. The Qi in the park was dense, swirling around him like a heavy fog, but he didn't try to grab it. He simply invited it to move with him.

*Inhale. Expand. Exhale. Contract.*

"Some of you," Wei said, his voice as steady as his movements, "say that balance is boring. You see your numbers. You see 10, and you want 11. You see 11, and you want 12. You think that by Moving faster, you are growing faster."

He reached the midpoint of the kata, his hands coming together in front of his chest. Beside the stage, Miller and Sarah stepped forward, each holding a thick, solid mahogany board. These weren't the thin, balsa-wood boards used in strip-mall karate demos. These were two inches of kiln-dried hardwood.

Wei didn't stop his slow, rhythmic breathing. He didn't even look at the boards. He simply shifted his weight, his eyes fixed on the distant line of the horizon.

Then, he exploded.

There was no telegraphing. One moment he was a statue in slow motion; the next, he was a blur of blue cotton. His hand flashed forward, fingers rigid and infused with a concentrated, needle-point Surge of Qi. 

*CRACK.*

The sound was like a gunshot. The mahogany board didn't just break; it shattered, splinters flying into the air like shrapnel. Wei’s hand stopped centimeters past where the board had been, his arm perfectly still, his breath finally escaping in a sharp, controlled hiss.

He didn't pause for the gasps from the crowd. He immediately flowed back into the slow motion of the kata, his hands tracing the air as if nothing had happened.

"Balance," Wei said, "is the only thing that keeps you from breaking yourself. If you put a Ferrari engine on a go-cart, what do you think happens the first time you hit the gas? You don't win the race. You smash into the wall at two hundred miles an hour because your wheels can't hold the road."

He spun slowly, his leg rising in a high, effortless arc. Above him, Miller and Sarah had already swapped positions, holding two more boards high over the center of the stage. 

Wei didn't rush. He felt the Qi pooling in his heel, the resonance of his body aligning with the gravity of the earth. He waited for the heartbeat, the exact moment of perfect equilibrium.

*Snap.*

His leg whipped out in a roundhouse kick so fast that the camera on Jax’s lens barely caught the frame. Both boards disintegrated simultaneously, the impact sending a small shockwave of dust across the stage. 

He landed softly on one foot, held the pose for a three-beat count, and then slowly lowered his leg back to the wood. He was perfectly calm. Not a bead of sweat on his brow.

"Sustainable growth," Wei said, looking out at the thousands of stunned faces. "That is the goal. Your body is the go-cart. Your 'Status' is the engine. If you want the engine to be bigger, you must first make the wheels stronger and the frame more solid."

He walked to the edge of the stage and picked up a simple wooden staff. It wasn't a spirit-artifact. It wasn't even polished. It was just a sturdy piece of oak he’d found in Guzman’s basement.

His voice turned solemn, the warmth of the demonstration replaced by a cold, hard edge of warning.

"Changes are coming," Wei said. "The Violet Moon didn't just give you a Status screen. It woke up the world. And humans aren't the only ones who can cultivate."

A murmur of unease rippled through the meadow.

"In my world," Wei continued, "the change didn't start with dragons or soaring phoenixes. It started small. It started with the things that live in the dark. Rodents. Insects. The scavengers who have always lived alongside us, but were always too small to be a threat."

"Giant rats," Wei said, his eyes scanning the crowd. "They are already gathering in the subways. They are growing in the sewers. They are faster than you, and they are starting to get hungry. And they don't care what your 'Strength' score is if you don't know how to hit a moving target."

He held up the wooden staff.

"Your first weapon shouldn't be a sword. It shouldn't be a gun that might jam the moment the Qi spikes. It should be a stick. Any stick. A broom handle, a fallen branch, a piece of pipe."

"Learn to use it," Wei commanded. "Learn the reach of it. Learn the weight of it. Pair up. Watch each other’s backs."

He looked out at the city, the skyscrapers glowing with that eerie, bioluminescent mist.

"Go hunt the rats," Wei said, his voice dropping to a low, dangerous growl. "Clean your own streets before the streets decide to clean you. That is your first Quest. Not for glory. Not for a level-up. For the safety of your neighbors."

He turned and walked off the stage, leaving the tens of thousands of people in a stunned, frantic silence.

"Master," Jax whispered as he followed Wei toward the trees. "I have already processed three hundred thousand requests for 'Best Rat-Hunting Sticks' on Amazon. Also, the NYPD is calling. They want to know if 'Rat Hunting' constitutes a public disturbance."

Wei didn't stop. He just kept walking, his staff tapping a steady, rhythmic cadence against the asphalt path.

"Tell them," Wei said, "that the rats are already a public disturbance. I’m just providing the solution."

He looked at Miller and Sarah, who were following close behind, their faces pale but determined.

"Get your gear," Wei said. "We’re going to the 42nd Street station. If the rats are growing as fast as I think they are, the 'Status Craze' is about to get a very bloody reality check."

As they headed toward the subway entrance, the sun finally cleared the buildings, turning the violet mist into a shimmering, golden haze. It looked beautiful. It looked like a dawning age of wonder.

But Han Wei knew better. He knew that in a world of monsters, the most dangerous thing wasn't the beast in the dark. It was the man who thought he was a god because he could do ten push-ups.

It was time to see if New York could learn to sweep.
