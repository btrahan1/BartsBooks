# Cultivator in NYC: Book 3 - Chapter 11: The Gilded Entrance

The trek to 42nd Street was no longer a simple commute. For Han Wei and his small group, it was a tactical traversal of a territory that was rapidly becoming alien.

They moved in a compact, disciplined diamond. At the front were the three "Spartans"—construction workers who had been the first to master Wei’s spear-and-shield basics. They didn't have the gleaming plates of the Dungeon Killers, but their scrap-steel shields were dented and scarred, proof of the hundreds of rats they had already cleared from the Upper East Side.

Miller walked just behind them, his heavy iron pipe resting on his shoulder like a siege weapon. Sarah and Tupi stayed in the center, Sarah’s eyes scanning for the tell-tale shimmer of Qi-poisoning in any of their neighbors, while Tupi’s hand remained in contact with a small, potted spirit-fern she carried in a silk sling. Jax hovered ten feet above, his red eye darting frantically as he managed the thirty million people still glued to the live feed.

And at the rear was Han Wei. 

He moved with an effortless, lazy grace that belied the focused cerulean aura shimmering around his feet. He wasn't carrying a spear today; he had his simple oak staff, tapping it against the cracked subway tiles with a steady, rhythmic cadence. He only stepped in when a particularly large 'Veteran Rat' tried to lunge from the shadows, his staff blurring into a single, precise strike that sent the beast back into the dark.

"The resonance is changing," Tupi whispered as they approached the Grand Central mezzanine. "It’s not just violet anymore. It’s... gold. But a cold gold. Like jewelry that’s been buried in a grave."

"I see it," Sarah said, pointing her tablet toward the ceiling. "The constellations on the terminal roof—they’re moving, Wei. They’re not just lights anymore. They’re forming a celestial map."

They rounded the corner into the main concourse, and for the first time in his Earth life, Han Wei was genuinely stunned.

Grand Central Terminal was gone.

In its place was a cathedral of high-functioning Qi. The iconic clock in the center of the room was now ten feet tall, its gears visible and made of shifting, translucent emerald. The floor wasn't marble; it was a mosaic of spirit-silk and liquid gold that rippled underfoot like the surface of a pond. The great windows were no longer looking out onto 42nd Street, but into a swirling, amber void of dimensional static.

And standing outside the main 'Gate'—the massive, shimmering doorway that had once been the entrance to the Lexington Avenue lines—was a group of forty men in full plate armor.

The Dungeon Killers.

At their head stood Krull. His two-handed broadsword was resting point-down on the golden floor, his hands folded over the cross-guard. He looked like an avatar from a high-fantasy epic, his iron mail glowing with a faint, disciplined white light.

Wei stepped forward, his staff held in a gesture of peace. He inclined his head in a shallow, respectful bow.

"Greetings, honored warriors," Wei said, his voice carrying clearly through the humming, golden silence of the terminal. "It seems the vanguard has reached the gate ahead of the teachers."

Krull narrowed his eyes, his expression unreadable behind his steel visor. He didn't return the bow, but he did incline his head slightly. "Master. Your Spartans have been busy in the sewers. My boys report that the 'Broomstick Brigades' are surprisingly effective at holding the line."

"A spear is a simple truth," Wei mused, his eyes scanning the massive, pulsing gateway behind Krull. "Have you been inside yet?"

Krull shifted his weight, the metal of his armor clanking with a heavy, professional sound. "We didn't go far. Just a trial run. We hit the first three 'chambers.' There are things in there, Wei. Things that aren't rats. Sculpted things. Gargoyles made of brass that bleed liquid mercury. We took out four before the 'Dissonance' got too heavy and we had to pull back."

Wei nodded slowly, his fingers tracing the grain of his staff. "The Dungeon is not yet complete. It is still gestating, drawing on the memory of the city to build its walls. We are fortunate—if that is the word—that it is allowing us in for a 'taste' before the thirty-day stabilization is finished."

"A taste?" One of Krull’s lieutenants scoffed. "We almost lost two men to a trap made of golden wire. This isn't a snack, Master. This is a meat-grinder."

"It is an Instance," Wei corrected gently. He looked at Krull. "As you are aware, with your gaming experience, parties each get their own copy of the space. They are separate reflections of the same core intent. We will go inside as well, then perhaps we can exchange notes afterwards? Information is the only currency that will matter when the overflow hits."

Krull looked at the shimmering archway, then back at Wei’s small, scrappy group. He saw Miller’s iron pipe. He saw Sarah’s glowing hands. He saw the three spearmen standing in a perfect, quiet phalanx.

"Exchange notes," Krull repeated. "You want to treat a planetary apocalypse like a wiki-update?"

"I want to make sure the people who survive the first room don't die in the second," Wei said simply. "You have the steel, Krull. You have the soldiers. But I have seen these things grow before. I know the 'Breath' of a Labyrinth."

Krull was silent for a long moment. Then, he stepped aside, gesturing with his sword toward the portal.

"Go ahead, Master," Krull said, his voice dropping to a low, competitive growl. "Take your 'taste.' Let’s see if your breathing exercises can stop a mercury-gargoyle. My boys will be waiting. We’ll see who's still standing to compare notes at the end of the hour."

"Gratitude," Wei said, bowing again. 

He turned to his group. "Miller, points forward. Sarah, keep the Purifying Wave ready—the mercury in there isn't just liquid; it’s Corrupted Qi. Tupi, keep your fern alive. If it wilts, we leave immediately. It’s our only compass for the 'Life-Breath' of the Instance."

"Let's go, Spartans," Miller grunted, his iron pipe held low. 

The three spearmen locked their shields, their sharpened points leveled toward the shimmering amber void. They didn't look like soldiers from a movie. They looked like New Yorkers going into a very dangerous subway station on a Monday morning. 

Wei stepped into the center of the formation, his blue aura intensifying, pulsing with a steady, protective beat. 

"Master," Jax whispered as they approached the threshold. "The live feed has reached sixty million concurrent viewers. The chat is... well, it’s mostly screaming. But there is a significant sub-set of people who are taking bets on whether the 'Broomsticks' or the 'Plate Mail' will handle the gargoyles better."

Wei stepped onto the liquid-gold floor of the gateway, his staff making a sound like a gong as it struck the threshold. 

"Tell them," Wei said, his voice echoing into the golden void, "that the winner isn't the one with the best gear. It’s the one who remembers to breathe when the floor disappears."

He looked back at Krull one last time—the iron general standing over his broadsword, watching with a mixture of professional curiosity and silent, gamer-honed calculation.

"We will see you on the other side, Guild Leader," Wei said.

Then, Han Wei stepped into the amber light. 

The sound of the city—the sirens, the hammers, the distant shouts of the Rat Wars—vanished instantly. In its place was the hum of a thousand invisible violins, a sound that was both beautiful and terrifying.

The Gilded Labyrinth had accepted its second party. And the 'Mediocre Master' was about to find out if Earth’s memory of the Renaissance was as dangerous as his world’s memory of the Abyss.

"First Instance," Wei whispered as the golden walls formed around them. "Let’s see if we can find the center."
