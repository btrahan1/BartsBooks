# Chapter 1: The Stubborn Rock

The village of Little Pond was not known for producing great cultivators. It was mostly known for its turnips, which were tough, fibrous, and difficult to chew. The villagers shared a similar constitution.

But even by Little Pond standards, Gong was... dense.

At five years old, Gong did not run. He trundled. He moved with the tectonic inevitability of a small boulder rolling uphill. While other children chased chickens or climbed the lychee trees, Gong could usually be found sitting perfectly still, staring at a bug, or perhaps sleeping in a position that would shatter the spine of a normal child.

His mother, a woman named Wei who possessed the patience of a saint and the forearms of a blacksmith, often said, "Gong isn't slow. He's just saving his energy for later."

Later turned out to be a Tuesday in mid-summer.

The incident involved the Village Head’s prize goat, a creature named 'Old Iron' because of its temperament and the hardness of its skull. Old Iron was a mean spirited beast that terrorized the local toddlers. It had claimed the bridge over the creek as its personal domain, demanding a toll of dropped snacks or bruised shins.

Gong had been sent to fetch water. He carried a small wooden bucket. He reached the bridge.

Old Iron bleated—a sound like tearing metal—and lowered its horns.

A normal child would have run. A brave child would have thrown a rock.
Gong did neither. Momma had said, *“Fetch the water, Gong. Don’t stop for playing.”*

So Gong stepped onto the bridge.

Old Iron charged.

The impact was audible across the village green. *THWACK.*

The goat hit Gong squarely in the chest. By all laws of physics, the five-year-old should have effectively vanished, launched into the creek below.
Instead, Gong sat down hard. He slid backward about two feet, the wood of the bridge groaning under his denim trousers. He blinked slowly. He looked at his bucket. It was still in his hand, though empty.

Old Iron, confused by the fact that the obstacle was still there, backed up for a second run.

*THWACK.*

Gong grunted. He slid another six inches. A small bruise was forming on his cheek where the goat’s ear had slapped him, but his expression hadn't changed. It wasn't fear. It wasn't anger. It was mild inconvenience.

The goat prepared for a third strike.

But Gong had a rule. Momma said, *“If something hits you, hold onto it so it doesn't hit you again.”* She was usually talking about the shutters in a windstorm, but Gong found the logic sound.

As Old Iron charged the third time, Gong didn't brace. He reached out. His pudgy, dirt-stained fingers closed around the goat’s left horn.

*THUD.*

The goat hit him. Gong absorbed the blow, his little heels digging into the wood slats. But this time, when the goat tried to back up to charge again, it couldn't.

Gong held on.

The goat pulled. Gong stayed seated.
The goat thrashed, kicking its legs, bleating furiously. Gong stared at it, brow furrowed in concentration. His grip didn't waver. It was the grip of a child who refused to let go of a candy bar, applied to a seventy-pound farm animal.

Ten minutes passed.
The goat began to panic. It dragged Gong three feet to the left. Gong slid, collecting splinters in his pants, but the hand remained clamped.

Twenty minutes passed.
A crowd gathered. The village baker, the blacksmith, and the Village Head watched in fascination.

"Should we help him?" the baker asked.
"The goat looks tired," the blacksmith noted.

Thirty minutes passed.
Old Iron was hyperventilating. Its tongue lolled out. It had tried to headbutt Gong, but it couldn't get enough leverage because Gong was attached to its head. Every time the goat jumped, Gong simply became a heavy pendulum swinging from its face.

Finally, with a sigh of defeat that seemed to deflate its entire body, the goat collapsed. It lay on the bridge, panting, eyes rolling.

Gong blinked. He waited a full minute to be sure the "storm" was over. Then, carefully, he released the horn.

He stood up, dusted off his trousers, and picked up his bucket. He looked at the circle of stunned adults.

"Goat was in the way," Gong explained simply.

Then he trundled past the exhausted animal to get his water.

From that day on, the villagers didn't call him "Slow Gong." They called him nothing to his face, and kept their livestock well away from his hands.
