# Chapter 3: The Heavy Departure

Two years later, the tax collector came to Little Pond.

It was a bad year for turnips. The sericulture sheds were empty due to a blight. The Village Head sweat through his silk robes as he spoke to the man from the prefecture.
"We have no silver," the Head pleaded. "But we have... labor."

The tax collector sniffed. He was accompanied by a man in grey robes—a representative of the Iron Mountain Sect. The Sect was looking for raw recruits. Not for disciples (who needed talent), but for the "Logistics Division." They needed bodies to haul grain, mine ore, and serve as targets for the inner disciples.

"We need ten sturdy boys," the grey-robed man said. "Ten years of service. The Sect pays the village's tax."

They found nine easily enough. Farm boys who wanted glory. Or at least, wanted to see a city.
But they needed a tenth.
The Village Head looked at the tax collector. Then he looked at the bridge where Old Iron the goat used to graze (rest in peace). Then he looked at Gong's house.

Gong was ten now. He was the width of a barrel. He was currently holding up the roof of the blacksmith’s shed while the smith replaced a rotted support beam. He had been holding it for forty-five minutes. He looked bored.

"That one," the grey-robed man said, pointing. "He looks... durable."

***

The goodbye was quiet.
Gong packed his things: two pairs of trousers (extra large), a bag of dried turnip chips, and a smooth river stone he liked.
He stood by the gate. The other nine boys were bouncing on their heels, excited. Gong looked like he was attending a funeral.

Momma fixed his collar. She didn't cry. Moms in Little Pond were tough like the turnips.
"Listen to me, Gong," she said, her hands resting on his broad shoulders. "You are going to a big place. People there will be fast. They will be clever. They will talk circles around you."

"I know, Momma."

"Don't try to be fast. You aren't. Don't try to be clever. You'll just get a headache." She looked him in the eye. "You just be Gong. If they give you a job, you finish it. If they hit you, you stand up. If you make a friend, you hold onto them."

"Like the goat?" Gong asked.

Momma smiled, a rare, crinkling thing. "Ideally a little gentler than the goat. But yes. Never let go."

"I don't want to go," Gong admitted. His voice hitched. "I want to fix the blacksmith's roof."

"A man needs to see the world, Gong," she said firmness returning. "The village is too small for you. You're already eating us out of house and home."
It was a joke, but Gong nodded solemnly. He did eat a lot.

"I will send money," Gong promised. "And silk."

"Just send yourself back one day," she said. She patted his cheek—the one that still had a faint scar from the bully fight. "Now go. Don't look back. Walking backward makes you trip."

Gong turned. He picked up his bag. It was huge, filled with the other boys' extra gear because they had tricked him into carrying it. He didn't mind. The weight felt grounding.
He walked toward the caravan.

He didn't look back. Momma said not to.
But he listened. He listened to the wind in the lychee trees, and the creek rushing under the bridge, and the sound of his mother breathing until he couldn't hear it anymore.

Then, he just listened to the crunch of his boots on the gravel.
One step. Two steps. It was a long walk to the Iron Mountain. But Gong was good at walking. He wouldn't stop until he got there.
