# Chapter 4: The Whole Tree

The journey to the Iron Mountain Sect took three weeks by wagon. For most of the boys from Little Pond, it was an adventure. They spent the days hanging off the sides of the carts, pointing at strange birds and arguing about who would become the greatest swordsman.

For Gong, the journey was... educational.
He sat in the back of the supply wagon, squeezed between bags of rice and crates of pickled plums. He didn't talk much. He mostly watched the wheels turn. He realized that if the wheel hit a rock, the wagon bump-bumped. If it hit mud, it squelched. Aspects of the world were revealing themselves to him in a slow, rhythmic language.

On the fifth night, the caravan stopped in a dense forest. The trees were tall oaks, their canopies blotting out the stars. The air was cold, biting through the thin tunics of the village boys.

The caravan leader, a junior disciple named Brother Han who clearly resented being on babysitting duty, stood in the center of the clearing. He rubbed his hands together, shivering.

"We camp here," Han announced. "You lot, set up the tents. And you—" He pointed a finger at Gong, who was currently contemplating a particularly interesting patch of moss on a rock. "Big Recruit. What's your name? Gong?"

Gong looked up. "Yes."

"We need a fire," Brother Han said, his breath misting. "A big one. The wolves in these woods are bold, and I don't want to spend my night guarding you runts. Go get wood. Lots of wood."

Gong stood up. He nodded once. "Lots of wood."

"Yes. Now go. Don't wander too far."

Gong turned and walked into the darkness of the treeline.
The other boys scrambled to set up the tents, complaining about the cold ground and the lack of proper stakes. Brother Han sat on a log, cleaning his fingernails with a small dagger, looking bored.

Ten minutes passed.
"Where's the big guy?" one of the boys, a skinny kid named Pip, asked. "Did the wolves get him?"

"Probably lost," Han muttered. "Typical peasant stock. Directionally challenged."

Twenty minutes passed.
"I hear something," Pip whispered.

It started as a low vibration in the soles of their feet. Then came the sound—a rhythmic *crush, snap, CRUSH, snap*. It sounded less like footsteps and more like a landslide negotiating a peace treaty with the forest floor.

The bushes at the edge of the clearing shook violently. Birds took flight, squawking in terror.
Brother Han stood up, his hand dropping to the hilt of his sword. "Stay back," he hissed. "Could be a Spirit Bear."

The crashing got louder. A small sapling at the edge of the camp was flattened as if stepped on by a giant.
Then, Gong emerged.

He was walking backward. He was leaning forward at a forty-five-degree angle, his boots digging deep furrows into the earth. His thick arms were wrapped around something dark and rough.
He grunted—a low, resonant sound from his diaphragm—and took another step.

Drag. *CRUSH.*
Drag. *CRUSH.*

Into the firelight slid the trunk of a dead oak tree. It was easily forty feet long. The roots, still clotted with earth and stone, dragged behind it like a chaotic tail. It had branches as thick as a man's leg. It was, effectively, the entire tree.

Gong pulled it another three feet, until the root ball was clear of the bushes, then dropped it.
*BOOM.*
The ground jumped. The supply wagon rattled.

Gong wiped sweat from his forehead with his sleeve. He wasn't panting, but he was breathing deeply. He looked at Brother Han.

"Wood," Gong said.

The camp was silent. The other boys stared with their mouths open. Brother Han looked at the tree, then at Gong, then back at the tree.
"I..." Han started. He blinked. "I told you to get firewood. Like... sticks. Logs."

Gong looked at the tree. It was wood. It was dead (dry). It was definitely "lots." He didn't understand the problem.
"You said lots of wood," Gong explained. He patted the trunk. "This is lots of wood."

"You... you pulled the whole thing?" Han asked, his voice an octave higher than usual. "How?"

"It was lying down," Gong said. "Easier than the standing ones."

Pip started to giggle. Then the other boys joined in. Even the wagon drivers were chuckling.
"He brought the whole tree!" Pip laughed. "The Whole Tree Gong!"

Brother Han sighed, rubbing his temples. "Fine. Whatever. We certainly won't run out." He gestured vaguely at the behemoth. "Put the... end... in the fire pit."

Gong nodded. He walked to the finer end of the tree, lifted it as if it were a large breadstick, and dropped it into the center of the stone circle.
When they lit it, the fire was indeed massive. It roared like a furnace.

While the other boys huddled close, telling ghost stories and roasting dried meat, Gong sat slightly apart. He leaned his back against the unburned trunk of his tree. It was solid. It was sturdy.
He felt he had done a good job. The fire was big. The wolves would stay away.

He fell asleep to the sound of the flames eating the oak, satisfied. Momma would be proud. He had finished the task.
