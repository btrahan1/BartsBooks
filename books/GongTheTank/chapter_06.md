# Chapter 6: The Thousand Steps

The Iron Mountain Sect didn't have a front door. It had a staircase.
Carved directly into the granite face of the mountain, the "Stairway to Iron" rose three thousand steps into the clouds. It was a test. It was a barrier. It was, in Gong's opinion, a very inefficient way to get home.

The caravan stopped at the base.
"This is where we part ways with the wagons," Brother Han announced. He pointed up. "The Sect is at the top. If you make it up before sunset, you get dinner. If you don't..." He shrugged. "The mountain gets cold."

The recruits looked up. The stairs were steep, each riser nearly knee-high.
"Go!" Han shouted.

The boys from Little Pond sprinted. They wanted to impress the disciples waiting at the top. They bounded up the first fifty steps like gazelles.

Gong did not sprint.
He was currently finishing his fifth dried turnip cake of the morning. Since waking up from the wolf attack, his appetite had become a black hole. Healing took energy, and Gong was determined to refill the tank.

He adjusted the straps on his back. He wasn't just carrying his own bag (which contained the other boys' gear). He was also carrying a large, leather-bound medicine chest.
It belonged to Xiao.
Since the wolf incident, Xiao looked pale and thin. Using that much Qi had drained her. She stood at the base of the stairs, looking at the climb with dread.

"I can carry it," Xiao said weakly, reaching for her chest. "Brother Han said we have to climb under our own power."

"Rule says we have to move our own bodies," Gong said, chewing. "Didn't say anything about luggage."
He hoisted her chest onto his left shoulder, balancing his own massive pack on his right. He looked like a pack mule constructed of bricks.

"Let's go," Gong said.

He took the first step. *THUD.*
He took the second. *THUD.*

Gong had one speed: Forward.
He didn't run. He didn't pause. He just transferred his weight, lifted his leg, and drove his heel down. It was mechanical.

Xiao walked beside him. Without her pack, she felt light, but her legs were still shaky.
"Wait for me," she panted at step 50.
Gong stopped mid-stride. He didn't put his foot down. He just balanced on one leg like a gargoyle until she caught up.
"Okay," he said, and completed the step.

At step 500, they passed Pip and the other boys. The "gazelles" were sitting on the steps, gasping for air, their faces red. They had sprinted too hard, too early. Use pacing.
"Look at... the turtle..." Pip wheezed, pointing at Gong.
Gong ignored him. He offered Xiao a piece of dried meat.
"Eat," he commanded. "Fuel."
Xiao took it, chewing gratefully. "Thanks, Gong."

"You fixed my arm," Gong said simply. "I carry your heavy thing. Fair trade."

At step 1,000, the air grew thinner. The wind snatched at their clothes.
Most of the recruits had stopped to rest again. Brother Han, who was leaping up the stairs three at a time effortlessly, paused to look back. He expected to see the athletic village boys leading the pack.
Instead, he saw a square shape moving with the inevitability of a glacier.

Gong was sweating, but he wasn't slowing down. His breathing was a rhythmic huffing sound, matching his steps. * Huff. Step. Huff. Step.*
Xiao was struggling. She stumbled on step 1,204.
Gong’s hand shot out—the same hand that had held the goat, the same hand that had held the wolf. He caught her elbow. He didn't pull her up (that would be carrying her). He just held her steady until she found her footing.

"I can't," Xiao whispered. "My legs are jelly."

"You can," Gong said. He pointed up. "Dinner."

"I don't care about dinner."

"Meat buns," Gong lied. He didn't know if there were meat buns, but he felt it was a statistically probable motivation.
"Meat buns?" Xiao asked.
"Big ones. Steaming."

Xiao took another step.

They passed the 2,000 mark. The sun was dipping low, painting the stone steps in gold.
The other boys were specks below them now.
Gong’s legs burned. His shoulders screamed under the weight of two people's lives. But Momma said, *“If you start a job, finish it.”* The stairs were the job.

They crested the final step just as the sun touched the horizon.
A massive stone archway loomed ahead. The "Gate of Iron."
Gong stepped onto the flat plaza. He didn't collapse. He didn't cheer. He just gently lowered the medicine chest to the ground, then sat on it.

Xiao fell to her knees, kissing the flat stones.

Brother Han was leaning against the gate, arms crossed. He looked at Gong—sweaty, dusty, carrying enough luggage for a small army, and currently unwrapping another snack.
He looked at Xiao, who was alive largely because she hadn't had to carry her gear.

"First place," Han noted dryly. "The Turtle and the... Small Turtle."

Gong swallowed his snack. "Are there meat buns?" he asked.

Han smirked. "For first place? Yeah. We can find some meat buns."

Gong nodded, satisfied. It had been a good climb.
