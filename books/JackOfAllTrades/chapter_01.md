# Chapter 1: The Man About Town

Jack Of All Trades

Bart Trahan

### []{#anchor}Chapter 1: The Man About Town

The first rule of Jack's life was simple: good enough is, more often
than not, perfect.

He wasn't lazy. Far from it. Laziness, in Jack's estimation, was a form
of inefficiency, and he despised inefficiency. His philosophy was simply
a matter of applied mathematics, a principle he'd stumbled across in a
business textbook he'd skimmed one Tuesday afternoon: The Law of
Diminishing Returns. Or, as he preferred to call it, the 80/20 Rule of
Getting Things Done.

He could learn the basics of conversational Spanish in a month, enough
to navigate a market in Barcelona and charm a street vendor into giving
him an extra churro. Could he become fluent? Of course. But that would
take years of dedicated study, thousands of flashcards, and hours of
awkward conversation for that last 20% of polish. Why? He wasn't
planning on becoming a UN translator. 80% was good enough.

He could fix a leaky faucet, bake a respectable sourdough, change the
oil in his car, and even pick a simple lock (a skill learned from a
series of online videos one particularly rainy weekend). He wasn't a
master plumber, a Michelin-star chef, a certified mechanic, or a cat
burglar. He was just Jack. And for the residents of the sleepy,
tree-lined streets of the Maplewood district, that was usually more than
enough.

His morning began, as it often did, with a walk to "The Daily Grind," a
coffee shop run by a perpetually frazzled young woman named Maya. Today,
however, the usual comforting aroma of roasting beans was overpowered by
a sense of quiet chaos. A line of disgruntled, under-caffeinated patrons
snaked out the door.

Inside, Maya was frantically wiping down a counter, her expression a
mixture of panic and despair. The heart of her operation, a gleaming,
chrome-plated Italian espresso machine, was silent. It sat on the
counter, inert and mocking.

"Don't tell me," Jack said, bypassing the line and leaning on the
counter. "The great beast has fallen."

Maya threw her hands up. "It just... stopped, Jack. It made a sound like
a dying goose, hissed at me, and that was it. I called the repair
company, but they said they can't get anyone out until tomorrow
afternoon. *Tomorrow!* I'll be bankrupt by then."

This was Jack's element. Not the crisis itself, but the solvable problem
at its core.

"Pop the hood," he said, a slight smile playing on his lips. "Let's have
a look."

A customer in line groaned. "You're going to let *him* touch it? Call a
professional!"

Maya shot the man a withering glare. "Ask Jack, he can probably fix it,"
she said, her voice a local's mantra. It was a phrase that followed Jack
around Maplewood like a stray dog.

He pulled a small, multi-tool from his pocket---a well-worn Leatherman
that was practically an extension of his hand. He didn't have a
schematic for a La Marzocco GS3, but he'd spent a weekend fixing a
friend's Jura, and he'd read a fascinating article on the history of
pressurized coffee extraction. He understood the principles: water,
heat, pressure, mechanics. 80% of all machines operated on the same
handful of concepts.

He took off the side panel. Inside was a labyrinth of copper pipes,
wires, and brass fittings. To a professional, it was a precise
ecosystem. To Jack, it was a logic puzzle.

"Okay, so you have power," he murmured, pointing with a screwdriver bit.
"The boiler light is on, so the heating element is fine. Did you hear
the pump try to engage?"

"I... I don't think so," Maya said, peering over his shoulder.

Jack tapped a small solenoid valve. "Sometimes these get stuck. Mineral
buildup." He followed the tubing back. "And sometimes..." He paused, his
fingers tracing a water line from the intake. He pushed gently on a
connection, and it wiggled. "...the person who installed it didn't quite
tighten the compression fitting enough. Vibration over a few years does
the rest."

He didn't need a specialized wrench. He just needed leverage. Using the
pliers on his multi-tool, he gave the nut a firm, quarter-turn. It bit
down with a satisfying tension.

"Alright," he said, wiping his hands on his jeans. "Give her a try. But
gently."

Maya held her breath and flipped the switch. There was a click, a low
hum, and then the familiar, life-affirming gurgle of the pump engaging.
Water flowed. The pressure gauge climbed into the green. Maya let out a
sob of pure relief.

"Jack, you're a miracle worker!"

"Nah," he said, waving it off as he reattached the panel. "Just noticed
a loose nut. The repair guy would have charged you two hundred bucks to
do the same thing. One large black coffee for my trouble?"

"You can have free coffee for life!" she declared, already grinding
beans with renewed vigor.

He just smiled. "We'll start with today."

Leaving the now-buzzing coffee shop, cup in hand, Jack continued his
walk. This was the pay-off. Not the coffee, but the quiet satisfaction
of a system restored to order. He could have spent six months in a trade
school to become a certified espresso machine technician. He could have
made a career of it. But then who would have helped Mrs. Gable with her
tomato blight last week?

He'd spotted the tell-tale yellowing on the lower leaves of her
prize-winning Beefsteaks while walking by. He didn't have a degree in
horticulture, but he'd binge-watched a British gardening show one season
and learned that most fungal problems could be managed with better air
circulation and a change in soil pH.

"Here, let me help you," he'd said, vaulting her low garden fence. "I
think I read about this somewhere."

He'd spent an hour with her, carefully pruning the lower branches and
explaining how a simple spray made of baking soda and water could help.
It wasn't a miracle cure. It wouldn't win her first prize at the state
fair. But it would save most of her crop. It was an 80% solution. Good
enough.

He reached his own house, a modest bungalow with a porch swing and a
collection of well-tended but not obsessively manicured plants. He sat
on the top step, sipping his coffee, watching the neighborhood come to
life. A kid on a bike wobbled past, a loose chain slapping against the
guard. A neighbor struggled to hang a wind chime, the knot he was trying
to tie stubbornly refusing to hold.

Each one was a small, solvable problem. A world of systems waiting for a
practical hand. Jack took another sip of his coffee. He couldn't fix
everything. He wasn't a master of any one trade. But in a world full of
specialists, a man who was pretty good at almost everything could always
find work to do. And for Jack, that was more than good enough. It was
perfect.
