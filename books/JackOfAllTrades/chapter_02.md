# Chapter 2: Send Jack to Work

Of course. Let's send Jack to work.

The scent of hot metal, oil, and worn-out tires was a kind of incense to
Jack. It was the smell of problems waiting to be solved. He pushed open
the grimy door to "Ernest's Auto," the bell above it giving a tired
jingle. Inside, the cavernous space echoed with the rhythmic
*thump-thump-thump* of an impact wrench and the classic rock wailing
from a dust-covered radio.

Ernest was a man built like a refrigerator, with a face that looked like
it had been used to stop a puck one too many times. He slid out from
under a dilapidated Ford F-150 on a wheeled creeper, wiping a
perpetually greasy hand across his bald head.

"Any work for me today, Ernest?" Jack asked, his voice easily carrying
over the noise.

Ernest grunted, a sound that could mean anything from "good morning" to
"the world is ending." He pointed a thick thumb over his shoulder. "Eh?
If you have time, could you do the brake pads on the Toyota over there,
and change the oil on that Chrysler."

Jack glanced at the two vehicles. A sensible Japanese sedan and a
hulking American boat. A standard morning. "Easy enough."

"Not so fast," Ernest grumbled, pulling himself to his feet with a
groan. "My other guy couldn't get the drain plug off the Chrysler. I
think he stripped it. Young kid, more muscle than sense." He gestured
toward the cramped, cluttered office. "And finally, the POS is acting
funny."

Jack raised an eyebrow. "The Chrysler is a piece of---?"

"No, the Point-of-Sale," Ernest clarified, waving a dismissive hand at
the computer on the front desk. "The register. Freezes up. Won't print
receipts right. Maria's threatening to quit if I don't get it fixed."

Brakes, a stripped plug, and a computer glitch. Mechanical,
problem-solving, and light IT. A classic Jack trifecta.

"Sounds like a good morning's work," Jack said. "100 bucks sound fair?"

"Sure, Jack. A bargain!" Ernest clapped him on the shoulder, leaving a
perfect, greasy handprint on his jacket. "Parts for the Toyota are on
the bench."

Jack started with the brakes. It was rote work, the kind of task you
could do while thinking about something else entirely. He had the Toyota
jacked up and the wheel off in under three minutes. He didn't have the
specialized caliper spreader a dedicated brake mechanic might use, but a
large C-clamp from Ernest's bench worked just as well. 80% of the job
was just knowing which bolts to turn. He had the old pads out and the
new ones in within twenty minutes, his movements economical and sure. He
wasn't setting any speed records, but he wasn't wasting a single motion,
either.

Next, the Chrysler. He slid underneath, the single shop light glinting
off the oil-slicked undercarriage. The drain plug was a mess. The kid
had clearly used the wrong size socket, rounding the corners of the hex
bolt until it was little more than a nub. It wasn't malice, just
inexperience meeting stubborn steel. A master mechanic might weld a new
nut onto it. Jack's solution was more pragmatic.

He rummaged through a chaotic drawer labeled "MISC. BOLTS" until he
found what he was looking for: a bolt extractor set he'd convinced
Ernest to buy last year for fifteen dollars. He chose the right size,
hammered it firmly onto the ruined plug, and fitted a breaker bar to the
end. He gave it one smooth, steady pull. There was a groan of stressed
metal, a sharp *crack*, and the plug broke free. He let the old oil gush
into the pan below, a satisfied smirk on his face. He even found a new
drain plug with a fresh gasket in another bin, replacing the damaged one
so the problem wouldn't happen again. Good enough, and better for the
next guy.

Finally, the POS. He entered the office, which smelled of stale coffee
and ink. The computer, a beige tower that was probably a decade old,
hummed loudly, its fan wheezing. Maria's complaint of "acting funny" was
an open-ended invitation.

He wiggled the mouse. The cursor jumped across the screen. He tried to
open the sales program. It hung, the dreaded hourglass mocking him.

He didn't run a diagnostic or check the software logs. He started with
the basics. He put his hand on the tower. It was hot to the touch. The
wheezing fan was struggling.

"Ernest!" he called out. "Where's your compressed air?"

Ernest pointed to a red can on a shelf. Jack unplugged the computer,
hauled the tower onto the floor, and unscrewed the side panel. The
inside was a horror show. A thick, greasy blanket of shop dust and grime
coated every surface, clogging the CPU fan and the heat sink. The
machine wasn't failing; it was suffocating.

He took the tower to the bay door and blasted it with the compressed
air. A massive gray cloud erupted from the case, dissipating in the
morning light. He blew out the power supply, the fins of the heat sink,
and the blades of the tiny fan until they were all relatively clean.

He put it all back together, plugged it in, and booted it up. It whirred
to life with a quiet, happy hum. The sales program loaded instantly. A
test receipt printed out in a neat, crisp line.

Jack walked back out to the shop floor. Ernest was wrestling with a
stubborn tire.

"All set," Jack said.

Ernest looked from the Toyota, to the Chrysler draining its last few
drops of oil, to the office where the computer now sat humming
contentedly. He grunted his approval, pulled a wad of cash from his
pocket, and peeled off five twenties.

"Like I said," Ernest grunted, handing him the money. "A bargain."

Jack folded the bills and tucked them into his pocket. A brake
specialist, a senior mechanic, and an IT guy. That would have been three
separate calls, three separate bills, and a whole day of lost business
for Ernest. Jack did it all in just over an hour. He wasn't the best at
any of it, but he was pretty good at all of it. And on a Tuesday morning
at Ernest's Auto, that was a hell of a lot better than perfect.
