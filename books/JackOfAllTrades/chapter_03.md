# Chapter 3: Time and Again

The bell above the door to "Time & Again" didn't jingle; it let out a
singular, precise *ding*.

The air inside was cool and smelled of brass polish and velvet. It was a
sharp contrast to the gasoline fumes of Ernest's garage, but to Jack, it
was just another system of gears and levers. The walls were lined with
ticking clocks, a thousand tiny heartbeats out of sync.

Martha, a woman who wore reading glasses on a chain and sweaters that
looked like they were knitted from steel wool, looked up from her loupe.

"Hey Martha, any work for me today?" Jack asked, leaning against the
glass display case.

"Oh, there you are Jack!" She bustled out from behind the counter,
holding a small velvet tray. "Sure, I've got two watches that need
repairs. A Seiko and a Rolex."

She set them down. On the left, a sturdy, reliable Seiko 5 automatic. On
the right, a vintage Rolex Datejust, gleaming with an aura of expensive
intimidation.

Jack pulled a small jeweler's loupe from his own pocket---another skill,
another tool---and bent over the tray. He picked up the Seiko first. He
gave it a gentle shake. The rotor sounded gritty, sluggish.

"Movement's dirty," Jack murmured. "Probably dried oil in the mainspring
barrel. And the date wheel feels sticky."

He set it down and picked up the Rolex. He held it for a moment,
admiring the weight. It was a beautiful machine. He knew how it worked.
He understood the escapement mechanism, the Parachrom hairspring, the
sheer engineering brilliance inside the oyster case.

But then he thought about the specialized case opener he didn't own. He
thought about the microscopic tolerances that were far tighter than the
Seiko's. He remembered downloading a PDF of the technical service manual
for this specific caliber a few months back. He had read the first
twenty pages, fascinated, until he hit the section on specific
lubrication points requiring four different types of proprietary Swiss
oils.

To learn to fix this watch *properly* would take a hundred hours of
practice and a thousand dollars in tools. To fix it to his "pretty good"
standard risked turning a five-thousand-dollar heirloom into a
paperweight.

The efficiency graph in his head plummeted. The curve flattened.

"I can fix the Seiko in about an hour, I would guess," Jack said,
setting the Rolex back on the velvet with a respectful click. "Clean,
lube, regulate the timing. Simple enough."

He tapped the glass of the luxury watch. "The Rolex, I am afraid, is out
of my league."

Martha looked disappointed. "Really? You fixed Mrs. Higgins' grandfather
clock last month."

"That was big gears, Martha. Gravity and pendulums," Jack explained.
"This... this requires specialized tools, and more importantly,
specialized skills and knowledge." He pushed the tray slightly toward
her. "You'll need to send that one off for repair at headquarters."

Jack suppressed a sigh. He *could* probably fix the Rolex. If he really
hunkered down, improvised a tool to open the back, and spent three days
sweating over the balance wheel, he could probably get it running. But
the risk-to-reward ratio was garbage. It was inefficient. It was the top
20% of the difficulty curve, and Jack lived comfortably in the bottom
80%.

"Not worth it," he muttered to himself.

"What was that?"

"Nothing," Jack smiled. He picked up the Seiko again. "But this one?
Easy peasy. 50 bucks sound good, Martha?"

Martha's face brightened instantly. She snatched the Rolex away to ship
it out and handed him a small set of screwdrivers from under the
counter.

"Sure, Jack! I'll charge him \$200! You're a peach, Jack!"

Jack took the watch and moved to the small spare workbench in the
corner. He didn't mind the markup. Martha had overhead, rent, and
insurance. Jack just had a hundred bucks in his pocket from Ernest and
another fifty on the way, all before lunch.

He unscrewed the back of the Seiko, the familiar, comforting logic of
the gears greeting him. This he could do. This was good work. And for
Jack, that was exactly enough.
