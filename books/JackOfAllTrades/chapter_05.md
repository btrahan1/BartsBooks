# Chapter 5: Home Office and Piano

The morning sun slanted through the blinds of Jack's home office,
illuminating dust motes dancing in the air. He sat at his desk, a cup of
coffee steaming beside his laptop. It was the first of the
month---financial review day.

He logged into his brokerage account. Jack wasn't rich, but he wasn't
broke, either. He was, as in all things, comfortably proficient at being
solvent.

Years ago, when he'd first decided to get serious about his future, he'd
gone down the rabbit hole of personal finance. He'd read the bibles of
investing---Graham, Buffett, Dalio. He'd incorporated himself as
"Jack-of-All-Trades, LLC" not just for the liability protection, but
because he'd discovered the sweet tax advantages of a Solo 401(k) and a
Self-Directed Roth IRA.

For a while, he had been obsessed. He'd built spreadsheets with macros
that pulled real-time data. He'd written Python scripts to scrape
earnings reports, looking for undervalued gems. He'd spent his evenings
staring at candlestick charts, trying to divine the future from the tea
leaves of the market.

And he'd done well. He'd managed a solid 12% CAGR (Compound Annual
Growth Rate). He remembered explaining the difference between CAGR and
simple ROI to Ted once; Ted had just asked if he could buy a boat yet.

But then, the Law of Diminishing Returns had slapped him in the face.

He realized he was spending twenty hours a week researching, analyzing,
and stressing just to beat the market average by two percentage points.
Two percent. On his portfolio size at the time, that extra effort was
earning him about three dollars an hour.

Jack could make fifty dollars an hour fixing a toaster. The math didn't
check out.

So, he had fired himself as his own hedge fund manager.

He'd taken all that complex, algorithmic capital and dumped it into a
low-cost, total market index fund. VTSAX and chill. It averaged a
historical 10% return. It required zero hours of maintenance.

Now, his "investment strategy" consisted of transferring 10% of every
check he received---from Ernest, from Martha, from Mrs. Gable---into the
fund and closing the browser tab.

He looked at the screen now. The balance sat just shy of \$50,000.

It wasn't "retire on a yacht" money. But it was "fix the roof if it
caves in" money. It was "take a trip to Europe if I feel like it" money.
It was freedom money.

He sipped his coffee. 10% return for 0% effort versus 12% return for 20%
effort. The choice was obvious. He closed the laptop. The market would
do what the market did. Jack had other things to do.

He stood up and stretched. The day was open. No scheduled jobs, no
crises. Just time. And for Jack, time was the only asset you couldn't
buy on an exchange.

He walked over to his bookshelf. It was a chaotic library of manuals:
*Basic Plumbing*, *The Art of French Cooking*, *Introduction to
Mycology*, *Motorcycle Maintenance*.

He pulled down a book he hadn't touched in a while: *Fundamentals of
Piano Tuning*.

"Why not?" he mused aloud. He had a tuning hammer somewhere in the
garage, and Mrs. Higgins had mentioned her upright sounded a bit
'honky-tonk' last week.

He wasn't a concert pianist. But he had a good ear. And if he could get
it 80% of the way there, it would sound a hell of a lot better than it
did now.

Jack smiled. Another skill. Another day. Good enough.

"Okay, Mrs. Higgins. Are you ready to operate?" Jack asked, snapping on
a pair of latex gloves with a playful *thwack*.

Mrs. Higgins giggled, clutching her apron. "Oh, Jack, you make it sound
like open-heart surgery."

"In a way, it is," Jack said, kneeling before the instrument. "Open-harp
surgery, at least."

He unzipped the canvas pouch he'd picked up on eBay for thirty dollars.
It contained a tuning hammer with a star tip, a long strip of red felt
temperament strip, and a handful of rubber mutes. It wasn't the
top-of-the-line carbon fiber gear the pros used, but the steel was
hardened, and the handle was wood. It would turn the pins just fine.

He inspected the piano. It was a sturdy, walnut-finished upright.

"It's a Baldwin Acrosonic," Jack noted, running a hand along the
fallboard. "Built like a tank. It's not a Steinway Model D, thank
goodness. If it were, I wouldn't touch it with a ten-foot pole. Those
things have sensitivities that require a PhD in acoustics. But this?
This is the Chevy truck of pianos. I can work with this."

He removed the front panel, exposing the harp---the bronze plate and the
hundreds of steel strings stretched tight. It smelled of dust and old
felt. To most people, it looked like a confusing loom of wire. To Jack,
it was just tension and frequency.

He pulled out his phone and opened a tuning app. A professional
tuner---a "Registered Piano Technician"---would do this entirely by ear,
listening for the interference beats between intervals, counting the
wobbles per second. It was an art form that took a decade to master.

Jack didn't have a decade. He had technology. The app would get him 90%
of the way there, getting the fundamental pitch perfect. His ears would
handle the last 10%, making sure the unisons (the three strings that
make up one note) didn't sound like a sour choir.

"I'm not 100% sure how long this will take, Mrs. Higgins," Jack
admitted, inserting a rubber mute between two strings. "The pins might
be tight, or they might be loose. But I think \$150 would be fair, if
you threw in some of those delicious chocolate cookies you make?"

Mrs. Higgins clasped her hands together. "Jack, for \$150, I'll bake you
a whole batch. The man from the music shop downtown wanted \$250 just to
show up!"

"Highway robbery," Jack muttered, setting his phone on the ledge.
"Alright, I'm going in. It might get a little noisy. A little
repetitive. Just ignore the *bongs*."

He started in the middle---Middle C. He struck the key. *Bong.* The
needle on his app hovered to the left. Flat. No surprise there; humidity
changes played havoc with wood.

He fitted the hammer onto the tuning pin. He didn't crank it. He applied
gentle, firm pressure, feeling the friction of the pin in the maple
block. *Nudge.*

He struck the key again. *Bong.* The needle centered. Green light.

"One down," Jack whispered. "Two hundred and twenty-odd to go."

It wasn't fast work. It was meditative. Strike, listen, nudge. Strike,
listen, nudge. He wasn't trying to achieve the "Concert Pitch"
perfection required for a symphony hall recording. He was applying the
"Equal Temperament" that sounded good to the human ear in a living room.

An hour later, his arm was a little tired, but the piano was singing. He
played a C-major chord. It rang out, full and bright, without the
wobbly, underwater sound it had before.

"Mrs. Higgins!" he called out. "Come give it a test drive."

She bustled in, wiping flour from her hands, and sat on the bench. She
played a few bars of a hymn. She stopped, her eyes misty.

"Oh, Jack. It sounds... it sounds like it did when Mr. Higgins bought it
for me in 1978."

"It's pretty close," Jack smiled, packing up his eBay kit. "Close enough
for jazz, anyway."

"It's perfect," she corrected him, handing him a check and a Tupperware
container heavy with warm cookies.

Jack took the container. The warmth seeped through the plastic into his
hands. 80% effort on the tuning, 100% satisfaction on the cookies. The
math was definitely in his favor.
