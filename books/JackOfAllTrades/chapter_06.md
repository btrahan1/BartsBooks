# Chapter 6: Patties Place

The diner was called "Pattie's Place," and it served a burger that was
80% grease and 20% beef---a ratio Jack found delicious, if medically
inadvisable.

The booth vinyl was cracked, revealing yellow foam that looked like old
sponge cake. Across the table, Ted wasn't eating. He was staring at his
fries as if they were tea leaves predicting his doom.

"It's the rumors, Jack," Ted said, his voice low. "But it's not just
rumors anymore. They fired the third-shift QA manager yesterday. Walked
him right out the door."

Jack took a bite of his burger. "Cost-cutting?"

"Panic," Ted corrected. "Pure panic. We're bleeding money. The new
contract---the Apex Project? It's three months behind. Every day we're
late, the penalties eat another chunk of the profit margin. If we lose
the Apex contract, the plant closes. Five hundred jobs, poof."

Ted worked at Miller Dynamics. For forty years, they had been the
beating heart of the town, making precision pumps and valves for
everything from tractors to airplanes.

"Walk me through it," Jack said, wiping his mouth with a paper napkin.
"You're on the machining floor. What's the bottleneck?"

Ted threw his hands up. "That's the thing! There isn't *one*. It's
everything. The engineers upstairs send down the CAD drawings for the
new valve housing. Beautiful drawings. Perfect. But they demand a
tolerance of .0001 microns on a lathe that was built when Reagan was
president. We tell them the machine vibrates too much for that. They
say, 'Fix the machine.' Maintenance says, 'We can't fix it without parts
from Germany that take six weeks to ship.' So we try to run it anyway,
and the scrap rate is fifty percent."

Jack's eyes narrowed slightly. He stopped chewing.

Input 1: Engineering disconnection from physical assets.

"Go on," Jack said.

"Then there's the software guys," Ted continued, gaining steam. "They
installed this new 'Enterprise Resource Planning' system. It's supposed
to track inventory. But you have to input a twelve-digit code every time
you grab a screw. It takes thirty seconds to log a part that takes five
seconds to install. So nobody does it. Then, at the end of the week, the
system thinks we have zero screws, auto-orders ten thousand more, and
suddenly the loading dock is overflowing with screws we don't need, but
we're out of the aluminum billet we *do* need because the system didn't
flag it."

Input 2: UI friction leading to data corruption. Supply chain
inefficiency.

"And the floor managers?" Jack asked.

"Running around like headless chickens," Ted sighed. "They hold three
meetings a day to discuss why we aren't working. That's three hours we
aren't on the machines. They want 100% efficiency, but they're giving us
50% uptime."

Jack pushed his plate aside. He grabbed a handful of napkins and a pen
from his pocket.

"Ted," Jack said, smoothing out the paper. "Draw the workflow. Just
boxes. Where does the order come in, where does it go?"

Ted sketched it out. It was a mess of arrows looping back on themselves.

Jack watched the ink flow. In his mind, the diner noise faded. He didn't
see a factory; he saw a circuit board with bad soldering. He saw a
plumbing system with too many U-bends.

The problem wasn't that Miller Dynamics lacked talent. The engineers
were brilliant (Specialists). The machinists like Ted were skilled
(Specialists). The software was state-of-the-art (Specialized tool).

The problem was that everyone was obsessed with perfecting their own 10%
of the pie, and in doing so, they were destroying the other 90%.

The engineers were chasing a perfect tolerance that didn't matter for
the product's function, causing a bottleneck. The software designers
were chasing perfect data granularity, causing user fatigue. Management
was chasing perfect oversight, causing paralysis.

It was the Law of Diminishing Returns, weaponized on an industrial
scale. They were spending millions to squeeze out that last 1% of
performance, and it was costing them the whole company.

"They're trying to build a Ferrari with a team of people who have never
met each other," Jack murmured, drawing a thick line through the center
of Ted's diagram.

"What?" Ted asked.

"The tolerance issue," Jack said, tapping the napkin. "The valve
housing. Does it hold pressure?"

"Yeah, even the scrap ones hold pressure," Ted admitted. "But they don't
meet the spec sheet printout."

"So the spec is wrong," Jack said. "Or rather, the spec is idealized.
Who wrote the spec?"

"Some PhD in a glass office in Chicago."

"And the inventory system? Can you turn off the mandatory logging?"

"I think so, but IT says that makes the data 'impure'."

Jack laughed. It was a dry, sharp sound. "Impure data is better than no
data, Ted."

Jack looked at the scribbled napkin. It was a disaster. It was
beautiful. It was the most solvable problem he'd seen in years.

"Ted," Jack said, capping his pen. "Who runs the plant locally? Who's
the guy who actually has to turn the lights off if this place goes
under?"

"That'd be Mr. Henderson. Old School guy. He's tearing his hair out.
Why?"

Jack picked up the check for lunch. "Because I think I might need to pay
Mr. Henderson a visit. It sounds like he needs someone who doesn't know
anything about aerospace engineering."

"Then who does he need?"

Jack stood up, sliding the napkin into his pocket. "He needs someone to
tell him that 'Pretty Good' is the only thing that's going to save his
ass."
