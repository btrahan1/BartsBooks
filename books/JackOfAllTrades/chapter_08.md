# Chapter 8: The Factory Floor

The hum of the factory floor grew louder as they approached the double
doors---a low-frequency thrum that Jack felt in the soles of his shoes.

"We haven't discussed my fees," Jack said, nonchalantly checking his
watch as if he had a pressing appointment with a ham sandwich.

Henderson didn't stop walking, but he glanced at Jack side-eyed. A
shrewdness had returned to his expression, displacing some of the
exhaustion.

"Jack, I have heard a bit about you," Henderson said. "Well, more than a
bit, I suppose. Maya at the coffee shop says you raised the dead with a
screwdriver. Ernest says you fixed a computer with an air compressor."

Jack shrugged. "People exaggerate."

"You shoot straight. I have no doubt," Henderson continued, pushing open
the heavy industrial doors. The noise hit them---the screech of metal on
metal, the hiss of pneumatics. "If you can keep this plant from closing
down, I will hire you as our Quality Engineer. It comes with benefits.
Health, dental, the works. We'll discuss salary after you fix our next
problem."

Jack paused mid-step. A job? A real, corporate job? His first instinct
was to recoil. He liked his freedom. He liked his 10% returns and his
piano tuning. He didn't want to be trapped in a cubicle farm, attending
meetings about meetings.

Henderson seemed to read his mind. He held up a hand, stopping near a
pallet of raw steel.

"And don't worry," Henderson shouted over the noise of a forklift
backing up. "I won't expect you to sit at a desk for 40 hours a week. I
don't need another guy sending emails. I have plenty of those. I need a
fixer. You will be my on-call Consultant. You come in when we need you.
You walk the floor, you spot the stupid stuff we're too smart to see,
and you fix it. Sound fair?"

Jack considered it. On-call. Consultant. It was basically what he did
for the whole neighborhood anyway, just scaled up. And the "benefits"
part... well, self-insuring was inefficient. Group rates were
mathematically superior.

Plus, looking around the cavernous factory, Jack saw a playground.
Pipes, wires, people, systems. A thousand inefficiencies waiting to be
smoothed out. It was the ultimate puzzle.

"Throw in free access to the scrap metal bin for my personal projects,"
Jack negotiated, raising his voice slightly.

Henderson laughed, a bark of a sound. "You can take the whole damn bin
if you get these lathes running."

"Deal," Jack said.

They shook hands. Henderson's grip was firm, desperate but hopeful.

"Alright, Consultant," Henderson said, pointing toward a cluster of
frustrated men standing around a massive CNC machine. "Show me what you
know about vibration dampening."

Jack cracked his knuckles. "I don't know much," he admitted, pulling his
Leatherman from his pocket. "Just enough to be dangerous. Let's go see
why your German engineering is losing a fight with American gravity."

The Lead Engineer, a man named Sterling who wore a white lab coat that
seemed chemically repelled by dirt, looked at Jack as if he were a
bacteria culture that had escaped a petri dish.

"This is a DMG Mori 5-axis mill," Sterling spat, gesturing to the
house-sized machine that was currently shut down. "It relies on
sub-micron precision. The issue is the resonant frequency of the factory
floor foundation. We've run the simulations. We need to excavate the
slab, pour a suspended isolation block, and reinstall. Estimated cost:
fifty-thousand dollars. Downtime: three weeks."

Jack ignored him. He walked up to the machine. He placed his hand on the
heavy steel casing.

"Turn it on," Jack said.

"It will produce scrap," Sterling protested.

"Just for a second. Dry run. No material," Jack countered.

Henderson nodded at the operator. The machine whirred to life. As the
spindle spun up to 12,000 RPM, a low moan began to build. It wasn't a
rattle; it was a hum that vibrated the teeth. The digital readout on the
control panel flashed red: **VIBRATION LIMIT EXCEEDED**.

"Cut it," Jack said.

The machine wound down.

Jack knelt on the concrete. He looked at the leveling feet of the
machine. They were steel on concrete.

"It's ringing like a bell," Jack muttered. "You're hitting the natural
harmonic of the frame at that speed."

"Precisely," Sterling sniffed. "Which is why we need the isolation
slab."

"Or," Jack stood up, brushing dust off his knees, "we just change the
mass of the frame so the harmonic frequency moves somewhere else.
Somewhere the motor isn't spinning."

Sterling scoffed. "You can't just 'change the mass' of a twelve-ton
machine."

"Sure you can," Jack said. He turned to Henderson. "I need to run to the
hardware store down the street. I'll be back in twenty minutes. Can you
get the maintenance guys to find me some heavy-duty ratchet straps?"

Twenty minutes later, Jack returned. He was carrying four 50-pound bags
of "Play Sand" on a dolly.

Sterling looked ready to call security. "Sand? You're bringing *silica*
near a precision machine?"

"It's in plastic bags, double-sealed," Jack said, hoisting a bag. "And
we aren't opening them."

Under Jack's direction, Ted and another mechanic strapped the heavy bags
of sand tightly to the lower structural struts of the machine's frame
using the ratchet straps. It looked ridiculous---a multi-million dollar
piece of German engineering wearing a diaper made of Home Depot
sandbags.

"Physics 101," Jack explained as he tightened a strap. "Mass absorbs
energy. Loose sand is a great deadener because the grains rub against
each other and turn the vibration into tiny amounts of heat. We're
tuning the fork so it doesn't sing."

He stepped back and dusted his hands. "Okay. Spin it up."

Sterling rolled his eyes, but Henderson signaled the operator.

The spindle whined. It hit 5,000 RPM. Then 10,000. Then 12,000.

The moaning sound didn't happen.

The machine hummed, smooth and deadly quiet. The readout flickered:
**VIBRATION: NOMINAL**.

"Run a part," Henderson barked.

The operator loaded a block of aluminum. Coolant sprayed. Chips flew.
The machine executed the program perfectly. The finished part came out.
Ted grabbed a micrometer and measured it.

He looked up, grinning like a lunatic. "Dead center. Tolerance is
perfect."

Silence descended on the group. Sterling opened his mouth, closed it,
and stared at the yellow bags of sand.

Henderson picked up the receipt Jack had left on the control panel. He
looked at the total.

"Wait, are you telling me you can get this operating within tolerance
for... 36 dollars?"

"Well, plus tax," Jack pointed out. "And the ratchet straps were yours,
so that saved you a bit. It's not a permanent fix, mind you. Eventually,
the bags might wear through. But it'll hold you until you can afford
that fancy concrete slab next year."

Henderson looked at the machine, then at Jack. He started to laugh. It
was a wheezing, incredulous sound.

"Jack," Henderson said, wiping a tear from his eye. "Welcome to the
payroll."
