# Chapter 9: Breakroom Pizza

"All right. My first order of business as Quality Engineer is lunch!
Everyone likes pizza, right?" Jack announced, clapping his hands
together. The sound echoed strangely in the sudden quiet of the
successful test.

Sterling, still staring at the sandbags with the expression of a man
whose religion had just been disproven, blinked. "We... we have a
catered management lunch in the executive conference room at 1:00 PM."

"Yeah, I'm canceling that," Jack said cheerfully. "Those things are
inefficient. Too many forks, not enough honesty. We're doing pizza in
the breakroom. The greasy kind. The kind that makes you sleepy if you
eat too much, so everyone relaxes."

He turned to Henderson. "Can you get those guys together that can't seem
to decide on a solution so we can chat over some pepperoni? I want the
Lead Engineer---" he nodded at Sterling, "---the Shift Supervisor from
the floor, the head of IT, and... let's say, two random machine
operators. Ted, you're free, right?"

"I am now," Ted grinned.

"Good. Mix the hierarchy," Jack said. "It disrupts the social signaling.
Hard to be a pompous jerk when you're trying to pull a string of cheese
off your chin."

Thirty minutes later, the breakroom---a windowless box that usually
smelled of burnt popcorn---smelled of oregano and garlic. Jack had
ordered ten extra-large pizzas from "Tony's," the place downtown that
cut their slices into squares for no discernible reason.

Around the mismatched tables sat an awkward assembly. Sterling sat as
far away as possible from 'Grease-Monkey' Mike, the shift supervisor.
The IT Director, a nervous woman named Brenda who viewed the factory
floor as a hostile alien planet, hovered near the door.

Jack grabbed a slice of pepperoni, folded it, and took a bite.

"Okay," he said, chewing. "Here's the rule for this meeting. No
acronyms. If you say 'API' or 'CNC' or 'KPI' without explaining it like
I'm five years old, you put a dollar in the jar." He pointed to an empty
coffee can in the center of the table.

"This is ridiculous," Sterling muttered. "We have deadlines."

"We *had* deadlines," Jack corrected. "Now we have pizza. And a
functioning mill, thanks to play sand. So, let's talk about the next
bottleneck. The Apex Project is behind. Why?"

"The software isn't tracking---" Brenda started.

"Ah-ah," Jack wagged a finger. "We fixed the inventory tracking this
morning. The 'Button' app is live. Next?"

"The assembly line," Mike the supervisor grunted. "We can't get the
sub-assemblies fast enough. The guys in the clean room take forever."

"Why?" Jack asked.

"Because they're lazy," Mike said.

"Because the protocol requires a twelve-step sterilization process for
every single bolt!" Sterling snapped. "It's the spec!"

"Okay," Jack said. "Sterling, why is the spec twelve steps?"

"To prevent contamination. Debris causes failure."

"Mike," Jack turned. "Have you ever seen a failure caused by debris?"

"Once," Mike admitted. "In '98. A guy ate a sandwich over an open
turbine."

"Okay," Jack nodded. "So we have a risk. But twelve steps? Is that 100%
clean? Or is that 99.999% clean?"

"It's Aerospace Standard," Sterling insisted.

"Is the Apex Project for an airplane?" Jack asked.

"No," Ted piped up through a mouthful of sausage. "It's a high-flow pump
for a sewage treatment plant."

Silence.

Jack looked at Sterling. "We are sterilizing bolts... for a sewage
pump?"

Sterling flushed. "The contract stipulated 'Aerospace Grade Quality
Assurance' in the boilerplate text! We didn't write it, the client did!"

"Right," Jack said. "So the client copied and pasted their standard
contract. And we are treating sewage pumps like jet engines."

Jack took another bite of pizza. "Brenda, can you video call the client?
Not the CEO. The project manager on their side."

"I... I suppose."

"Do it. Put them on speaker."

Five minutes later, a voice crackled from Brenda's laptop. "This is
Dave."

"Hi Dave, this is Jack from Miller Dynamics. Quick question. We're
building your sewage pumps. Do you care if the mounting bolts are
sterilized in an argon atmosphere, or can we just wipe them down with a
lint-free cloth?"

There was a pause. "Uh... are they going in the sewage?"

"Yes," Jack said.

"Why the hell would we care?" Dave asked. "Just make sure they're
torqued tight."

"Thanks, Dave. Have a good one." Jack hung up.

He looked around the table. "Okay. Sterling, rewrite the process sheet.
Wipe-down only. Mike, tell your guys in the clean room they can stop
wearing the hazmat suits. Ted, pass the garlic knots."

Sterling looked like he was having an aneurysm. "You... you just changed
the SOP based on a thirty-second phone call?"

"I just saved us forty hours of labor a week," Jack said. "And I didn't
even have to use math. That's efficiency."

He looked at the jar. "Sterling, you said SOP. That's a dollar."

Sterling stared at the can. Then he stared at the pizza. Slowly, he
reached into his wallet, pulled out a dollar, and dropped it in. Then,
for the first time in ten years, he reached across the table and took a
slice of pepperoni from Mike's box.

"Pass the red pepper flakes," Sterling murmured.

Jack smiled. The synthesis had begun.
