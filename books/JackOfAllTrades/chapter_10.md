# Chapter 10: The Monster Problem

The pizza boxes were empty, stacked in a greasy tower of accomplishment.
The atmosphere in the breakroom had shifted from hostile to merely
drowsy, which Jack counted as a win. But now, the easy wins were over.
The low-hanging fruit---the sandbags, the inventory app, the
sterilization protocols---had been plucked.

Now they were staring at the monster.

Jack leaned back, nursing a lukewarm can of soda. "Well now... I don't
think I can solve this next problem in an hour. Let's go over it again."

Sterling wiped his hands with a napkin, his earlier defensiveness
replaced by a cautious, analytical focus. He pulled a tablet from his
lab coat.

"The Heat Exchanger," Sterling said. "It's the heart of the Apex system.
It's failing the pressure test. Every time."

"Define failing," Jack said.

"It leaks," Mike said bluntly. "Micro-fractures in the welds. We weld
it, X-ray it, it looks good. We pressurize it to operating load, and
*pop*. Tiny cracks. We re-weld, it gets worse."

"We've tried everything," Sterling added. "Changed the filler material.
Pre-heated the alloy. Slow-cooled it in an oven. We even brought in a
specialist welder from Detroit. Same result. The alloy is Inconel 625.
It's notoriously difficult, but it shouldn't be *this* brittle."

Jack nodded. Inconel. Superalloy. High strength, high heat resistance,
nightmare to work with.

"Ted," Jack asked. "You're welding these by hand?"

"Robotic arm for the main seams," Ted corrected. "Hand-finish on the
corners. The robot is perfect. The cracks happen randomly, sometimes in
the robot weld, sometimes in the hand weld."

Jack closed his eyes. He tried to synthesize the inputs. *Metallurgy:*
Inconel hardens when you work it. Too much heat makes it grainier.
*Physics:* Pressure tests stress the weakest point. *Environment:* The
shop floor is noisy, vibrating (though less so now), and...

"Wait," Jack opened his eyes. "Where are you welding these? Which bay?"

"Bay 4," Mike said. "Next to the loading dock."

"And when do you do the welding?"

"Second shift mostly," Mike shrugged. "That's when the power draw is
lower."

Jack stood up and paced the small room. Something was itching at the
back of his brain. A random factoid from a podcast about infrastructure?
A chapter in a book about weather patterns?

"Bay 4," Jack muttered. "Loading dock. Second shift. Night time."

He looked at Brenda. "Brenda, does the building have automated climate
control?"

"Yes," she said. "Smart HVAC. It lowers the temperature at night to save
energy."

"And the loading dock doors," Jack turned to Mike. "Are they open at
night?"

"Yeah, trucks coming in and out all night," Mike said. "It gets drafty."

Jack stopped pacing.

"Sterling," Jack said. "Inconel 625. What happens if you get a rapid
temperature fluctuation during the cooling phase? Not the oven cooling.
The initial bead cooling."

Sterling frowned. "Thermal shock. It would induce residual stress. But
we weld inside the bay. It's not *that* cold."

"It's November," Jack pointed out. "And you're next to a giant open
door. If a cold draft hits a 2,000-degree weld pool, even for a
second..."

"It would quench it," Sterling realized, his eyes widening. "Like
dipping it in water. It would lock in the crystal structure before it
can settle. Brittleness."

"And if the HVAC kicks on to 'Eco-Mode' right when a truck opens the
door..." Jack trailed off.

"We're creating a wind tunnel of freezing air right across the welding
station," Mike finished.

Jack grabbed the tablet from Sterling. He pulled up the floor plan.

"It's an invisible variable," Jack said. "The specialists are looking at
the metal, the robot, and the welder. But the problem isn't the welding.
The problem is the *architecture*."

"So we move the welding station?" Mike asked.

"Too heavy to move," Sterling said. "The robot is bolted down."

"Then we move the air," Jack said.

He grabbed a marker and drew on the empty pizza box.

"We build a tent," Jack said. "Welding curtains. Floor to ceiling.
Create a micro-climate around the robot. And we put a cheap space heater
inside to keep the ambient temp stable at 75 degrees. No drafts. No
thermal shock."

Sterling stared at the pizza box drawing. "A tent. We've been failing
for two months because we didn't build a tent."

"You were looking for a metallurgical failure," Jack said gently. "You
found one. But the cause was a drafty door."

"I can build a frame out of scrap Unistrut in two hours," Ted said,
already standing up. "We've got extra vinyl curtains in storage."

"Do it," Jack said. "And tell the night shift to keep the space heater
on. But for the love of god, tell them not to plug it into the same
circuit as the robot."

Sterling looked at Jack. He looked at the pizza box. He looked at the
dollar bill he'd put in the jar.

"I studied materials science for six years," Sterling whispered. "And I
got beat by a draft."

"You didn't get beat," Jack said, clapping him on the shoulder. "You
just needed someone to stand back far enough to feel the breeze."

He checked his watch. "Alright, that's three crises down. I think I've
earned my retainer. Anyone want the last garlic knot?"

Jack sat in Henderson's office, the check resting on the mahogany desk
between them. The number **\$5,000.00** was printed in crisp, confident
font.

"You weren't officially hired for the first day," Henderson explained,
leaning back in his chair with the first genuine look of relaxation Jack
had seen on him. "So I gave you premium rates. What the specialized
consultants would have charged me by the hour, plus their administrative
fee, plus travel and accommodations."

Jack stared at the check in silence. His brain did the math. \$5,000 for
roughly six hours of work. That was an hourly rate of... obscene.

He looked at Sterling, who was standing by the window. The engineer
wasn't scoffing anymore. He gave Jack a respectful nod.

"This is too much," Jack said finally, pushing the check slightly back
across the desk. "I didn't come here to fleece you. I came here to help
out. I used sandbags and a pizza box, Henderson. The materials cost less
than lunch."

"And you saved the Apex contract," Henderson countered. "Which is worth
twelve million dollars. You saved five hundred jobs. You saved *my*
job."

"And you did, Jack, you did," Sterling added, turning from the window.
"Maybe more than you know. The morale on the floor... I haven't seen the
guys laughing while they work in years. You didn't just fix the
machines; you broke the tension."

Henderson tapped the check. "Anyway, that's your new monthly pay. Plus
benefits."

Jack blinked. "Monthly?"

"You come by and do a walkabout say, twice a week," Henderson proposed.
"Just stroll through. Sniff around. Then a pizza meeting with a
different group of guys? Break down the silos. Get the electricians
talking to the janitors. Get the accountants talking to the machinists.
How does that sound?"

Jack looked at the check again. Five thousand a month. Sixty thousand a
year. For... walking around and eating pizza?

It defied the 80/20 rule. This was the 99/1 rule. 1% effort for 99%
reward.

But then he looked at Henderson's face. He saw the relief. He realized
that for Henderson, Jack wasn't selling time. He was selling peace of
mind. He was selling synthesis. And apparently, synthesis commanded a
premium market rate.

"Twice a week," Jack repeated slowly. "And I pick the pizza toppings."

"Done," Henderson grinned.

"And I still want access to the scrap bin."

"It's yours."

Jack picked up the check. He folded it neatly and placed it in his
pocket. It felt heavy, but in a good way. Like a tool he hadn't known he
needed.

"Alright then," Jack said, standing up. "I'll see you Thursday. I heard
the shipping department is having trouble with their label printers
jamming. I think I read somewhere that it's usually just cheap adhesive
gumming up the rollers. A little rubbing alcohol should fix it."

Henderson laughed. "Get out of here, Jack."

Jack walked out of the office, past the receptionist who was now
smiling, and out the front doors.

The afternoon sun was shining. He had \$5,000 in his pocket. He had a
job that wasn't really a job. And he had an idea that Mrs. Higgins'
fence needed painting. He wondered if he could synthesize a better paint
mixture using some of the surplus industrial solvents he'd just
negotiated access to.

He got into his car, the engine turning over with a reliable purr.

Life, Jack decided as he merged onto the main road, was running at
optimal efficiency. Pretty good indeed.

The balls cracked loudly, the sound distinct over the Eagles song
playing on the Silver Cue's jukebox. It was a week later, and the
universe had righted itself. Miller Dynamics was humming, and Ted was
back to missing shots on the pool table.

"So, Mr. Bigshot," Ted teased as he watched his solid 3-ball drift wide
of the corner pocket. He straightened up, chalking his cue with
aggressive motions. "You going to start wearing a suit and tie and drive
around in a fancy new car? Maybe get vanity plates that say
'CONSULTANT'?"

Jack laughed, lining up a bank shot on the 11-ball. He was wearing his
usual: faded jeans, a soft gray t-shirt, and sneakers that had seen
better days but were perfectly broken in.

"Not a chance, Ted," Jack said, stroking the cue. The 11-ball banked off
the rail and sank with a satisfying *thunk*. "Suits are inefficient. Dry
cleaning is a scam. And my car gets me from point A to point B just
fine."

He moved around the table, eyeing the layout.

"But," Jack continued, pausing to look at the ceiling, "I was thinking
of setting up a small shop. You know that florist shop on Main Street?
The one that closed last year? It's still vacant."

Ted blinked, lowering his beer. "Wait, you're going to start selling
flowers? Jack, you know geraniums from petunias, sure, but are you
really gonna spend your days arranging bouquets?"

"What? No!" Jack chuckled. "I don't have the patience for ribbons. I was
going to open a place where people could bring their problems to me!
I'll still make house calls---gotta keep the circulation going---but
having my own shop is much more efficient. Instead of me walking all
over town to find the problems, I can aggregate them. Centralized
intake."

Ted scratched his chin. "So, like... a repair shop?"

"More than that," Jack said. "A synthesis hub. Bring me your broken
toaster, your dying houseplant, your confusing tax forms, or your sticky
door handle. If I can fix it in twenty minutes, I fix it. If I can't, I
tell you exactly who can and what to tell them so you don't get ripped
off."

"Like a triage center for life's annoyances," Sarah piped up from the
nearby stool, looking up from her book.

"Exactly," Jack pointed at her with his pool cue. "Triage. Diagnosis.
And 'pretty good' solutions."

Ted grinned. "Well, with that Miller money, you can definitely afford
the rent. It's a good spot. Right next to the bakery."

"Strategic location," Jack nodded. "Smelling fresh bread increases
customer patience by 15%."

"Ahhh... so..." Ted leaned on his cue. "What are you going to call it?"

Jack sank the 13-ball. He stood up, looking at his friends. He'd thought
about fancy names. *The Solutionist*. *The Generalist*. *Maplewood
Consulting*. But they all sounded like they came with hidden fees and
forms to sign.

He wanted something that promised exactly what he delivered. No more, no
less.

"I think," Jack said, a slow smile spreading across his face, "I'm just
going to put a sign in the window."

"And what's it gonna say?"

"**Jack of All Trades.**"

Sarah raised an eyebrow. "Isn't the rest of that saying 'Master of
None'?"

"That's the popular version," Jack agreed. "But the full quote is
actually: '*A jack of all trades is a master of none, but oftentimes
better than a master of one.*'"

He leaned down for the eight-ball.

"And besides," Jack added, pulling the cue back. "I'm not aiming for
mastery. I'm aiming for pretty damn good."

*Clack.* *Plop.*

"Game," Jack said.

Ted shook his head, laughing. "You're impossible, Jack."

"I'm just efficient, Ted." Jack racked the balls for the next game.
"Now, who's buying the next pitcher? I think it's your turn."
