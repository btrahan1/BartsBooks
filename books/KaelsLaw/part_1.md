Kael's Law

Bart Trahan

He scanned the row of faces, a collection of soft jaws, pimpled cheeks,
and nervous eyes that darted everywhere but at him. *Lambs to the
slaughter*, he thought, a familiar bitterness rising in his throat.
Every spring, it was the same. The King's Law stripped the countryside
of its youth, sending them to him to be forged into weapons. He was the
hammer, the anvil, and the fire, tasked with hardening this soft metal.
Most of them would break long before they ever saw a real battle.

"You are not here to make friends!" his voice, a gravelly roar honed by
years of shouting over the clang of steel, cut through the morning mist.
"You are not here to write letters home to your mothers. You are here to
learn how to kill. And more importantly, you are here to learn how to
not get killed."

He paced before them, his worn leather boots sinking slightly into the
damp earth of the training yard. He was a bull of a man, with a thick
neck and calloused hands that rested instinctively on the pommel of his
own well-worn sword. He stopped in front of a boy who was trying his
best to stand straight but was failing miserably, his lanky frame all
awkward angles.

"You," Daniels barked, leaning in close. The boy flinched. "What's your
name, son?"

"T-Thom, Staff Sergeant," the boy stammered, his gaze fixed on a point
somewhere over Daniels' shoulder.

"Thom," Daniels repeated, drawing the name out with a sneer. "You ever
held anything heavier than a pitchfork, Thom?"

"No, Staff Sergeant."

"I didn't think so." He moved on, his shadow falling over the next boy
and the next. "The enemy will not care that you are a farmer's son. He
will not care that you miss your sweetheart. He will only care that his
sword finds a home in your belly before yours finds his. That is the
grim mathematics of war. It is my job to teach you that equation."

He let the words hang in the air, a palpable weight. It was his standard
speech, designed to terrify them, to break down the farm boy and leave
only the potential soldier. As he turned to continue, a flash of
movement in the sky caught his eye. A hawk, circling high above,
suddenly folded its wings and dove, a streak of brown and gold
plummeting toward the woods bordering the training ground.

Before Daniels could even process the sight, a blur of motion erupted
from the line of recruits. A boy, no bigger than the rest, had already
nocked an arrow to a short, rough-hewn bow he must have carried with him
from home. It was an instinctive, fluid movement---a single, seamless
act of drawing, aiming, and releasing. The string sang for a bare
second.

High above, the hawk jerked unnaturally in its dive, a feathered
pinwheel tumbling from the sky. It landed somewhere in the trees with a
distant crash of foliage.

A stunned silence fell over the yard. The recruits stared, mouths agape.
Daniels turned slowly, his eyes locking onto the boy who now stood with
the bow held loosely in his hand, his expression calm, almost placid. He
was one of the ones Daniels had dismissed at first glance---scrawny,
with a mop of unruly brown hair and a quiet demeanor.

Daniels walked down the line until he stood before him. The boy didn't
seem nervous, merely observant. He met the Sergeant's hard gaze without
flinching.

"I didn't give an order to fire, soldier," Daniels said, his voice
deceptively low.

"It was going for the chicken coop, Sergeant," the boy replied, his
voice even. "My ma would skin me if I let a hawk take one of hers."

Daniels stared at him for a long moment, the silence stretching. The
shot was impossible. A moving target, at that distance, with a simple
hunting bow. It wasn't just skill; it was an ingrained, natural talent
that couldn't be taught. He had seen veteran archers miss easier shots
in the heat of battle.

He felt a foreign sensation, a flicker of something that had been
dormant for years. It wasn't quite hope, but it was a sharp, undeniable
spark of interest. The King's Law spoke of identifying 'talent.' For the
first time in a decade, Staff Sergeant Daniels thought he might have
actually found some.

"What's your name, boy?" he asked, the sneer gone from his voice.

"Kael, Staff Sergeant."

Daniels grunted, a slow smirk finally breaking across his weathered
face. "Alright, Kael. Let's see if you can handle a sword half as well
as you handle that stick and string. The rest of you maggots, wipe those
stupid looks off your faces! To the armory! Move!"

As the line of boys broke into a clumsy, stumbling run, Daniels fell
into step beside Kael. The boy was an anomaly, a question mark in a sea
of predictable answers. And for a man who thought he had seen
everything, a new question was a very interesting thing indeed.

"Where did you find that bow, boy?" Daniels' voice was low, cutting
through the nervous energy of the other recruits who were scrambling
towards the armory. He hadn't moved, his eyes fixed on the simple, yet
clearly effective, weapon in Kael's hand.

"I made it, Sir!" Kael answered, his tone matter-of-fact. He held the
bow out for inspection. It was a single piece of pale wood, expertly
shaped, with a darker, almost reddish core visible along its belly. The
string shimmered with a faint metallic lustre, catching the morning
light in a way no normal bowstring should.

Daniels took it, his thick fingers surprisingly deft as he examined the
craftsmanship. The wood was smooth, the curve perfect. He could feel the
latent power in it. "And how did you do that?" he asked, his skepticism
warring with the evidence in his hands. A bow like this, simple as it
looked, took a master bowyer years to learn how to make.

"From a branch from a yew tree and the silk from an iron spider, Sir!"

Daniels rubbed a hand across his face. This might take a while. "A yew
tree," he repeated slowly. "You know how to work yew? You know how to
read the wood, to follow the grain so it doesn't snap on the draw? You
know how to balance the heartwood and the sapwood?"

Most veterans didn't know that. Yew was a legendary bow wood, famously
difficult to work. The pale outer sapwood was strong in tension, perfect
for the back of the bow, while the darker heartwood excelled at
resisting compression on the belly. To carve a bow from a single stave
required an intuitive understanding of the wood's warring properties. To
get it wrong meant the bow would shatter spectacularly on the first full
draw.

"Yes, Sir," Kael said simply. "You just have to look. The wood tells you
where it wants to be strong. You follow the lines it gives you. For the
string..." he trailed off, gesturing to the shimmering strand.

"Iron spider silk," Daniels finished for him, his thumb rubbing the
strangely smooth, yet abrasive texture. He'd heard whispers of the stuff
in taverns from grizzled mercenaries who had traveled to the deep woods
on the kingdom's borders. Spiders the size of hunting hounds, with venom
that could curdle blood and silk with the tensile strength of steel.
Most considered them tall tales. "Where in the seven hells does a farm
boy find an iron spider?"

"There's a nest in the quarry behind our farm," Kael explained, as if it
were the most normal thing in the world. "They're not so bad if you
don't spook 'em. I just leave 'em a bit of salted pork now and then.
They spin thick in the autumn. You have to harvest it before the first
frost, or it gets brittle."

Daniels stared at the boy, truly seeing him for the first time. Not a
soft farm boy, but something else entirely. Someone who had casually
mastered one of the most difficult skills in archery and tamed one of
the most fearsome creatures in the kingdom for a bit of string. The
King's Law was meant to find talent, but this... this was something
else. This was a weapon born in the wild, standing right in front of
him.

He handed the bow back. "The King's bows are yew, but the strings are
flaxen," Daniels said, his voice now devoid of any mockery. "They will
break under the strain of a bow like this. You will keep your own."

He turned, his mind racing. One impossible shot could be a fluke. The
story of how the weapon was made, however, was a confirmation. This boy,
Kael, was the one-in-a-million recruit the law was written for.

"Come on, Kael," he barked, his voice once again the familiar roar of a
Staff Sergeant. "Let's see if you can hold a shield without tripping
over your own feet. The rest of your training is going to be a long,
hard road."

But for the first time in years, Daniels felt a genuine sense of
purpose. He wasn't just training lambs for the slaughter anymore. He was
forging a sword.

Kael held the shield like a piece of wood. Technically, it was, but
banded by iron and backed by thick leather straps. It felt heavy, alien
in his grasp. With the bow, his arm had been a guide, a fluid extension
of his will. With the shield, his arm was just an awkward post, and the
shield was a dead weight dangling from it. He simply held it, dangling
from his straightened arm, with no idea what to do with it.

Daniels grimaced, the hope from the archery display deflating into
familiar frustration. "Not like that, boy! Like this!"

He strode forward and snatched a practice shield from the rack. In one
smooth motion, he slipped his arm through the straps. His forearm rested
flush against the wood, his hand gripping the leather handle near the
rim. The shield became a part of him, a turtle's shell fused to his arm.

"See?" he barked, standing in front of Kael. "Your arm is the bone; the
shield is the skin. It lives with you! You don't dangle it like a dead
fish!" He reached out and shoved Kael's shield, which swung wildly on
his loose grip, throwing the boy off balance. Kael stumbled, his boots
scuffing in the dirt.

"Bend your elbow!" Daniels commanded. "Keep it tight to your body. It's
not a wall you hide behind; it's a fist you punch with! A ramp you slide
their blades off of!"

Kael adjusted his grip, mimicking the Staff Sergeant. He bent his arm,
pulling the heavy shield in close. The strain was immediate, a deep burn
in muscles he rarely used. Holding the bow had been a dance of tension
and release; this was a static, grinding endurance.

"Better," Daniels grunted, though his expression was still sour. "Now,
stand your ground."

Without warning, Daniels gave Kael's shield a sharp, brutal kick. The
impact slammed the wood into Kael's forearm, rattling his teeth. He
staggered back a step, his arm screaming in protest, but his grip held.

"You feel that?" Daniels demanded, his face inches from Kael's. "That
was a gentle tap. A real strike will shatter your arm if you take it
flat like that. You are not a stone wall. You are water. You are a
sloping hill. Angle the shield! Let their anger slide past you and spend
itself on the air!"

He took a step back. "Again."

This time, as Daniels' boot swung forward, Kael instinctively angled the
shield's face downwards. The kick landed not with a flat, jarring thud,
but with a glancing *thump*. The force was redirected, sliding off the
edge of the shield into the ground. Kael's feet stayed planted.

Daniels stared at him, a flicker of something reassessing in his eyes.
The boy hadn't done it with the practiced grace of a soldier, but with
the innate physical intuition of someone who understood force and
angles, not from a training manual, but from the grain of a yew branch.

"You learn," Daniels stated. It was not a compliment, merely a fact. He
picked up a blunted wooden practice sword. "Now let's see how you handle
a blade."

The Sergeant fell into a low combat stance. The grimace was gone,
replaced by the focused, predatory expression of a lifelong fighter. The
archery display had shown him the boy's talent. This clumsy, painful
lesson with the shield was showing him something just as important: Kael
was a blank slate, but he was a slate that could be carved. The road
would be long, and his arm would ache for weeks, but the raw material
was there. The forging had begun.

Again, the boy had no idea what to do with a sword. He held the blunted
steel as if it were a farmer's tool, but one for a job he'd never seen
before. His knuckles were white, his stance awkward. It was a stark
contrast to the shield, which he was at least beginning to understand as
a concept.

Daniels sighed and began the drill, his movements slow and deliberate.
"Guard up. Watch my blade. Block."

His first few swings were lazy, easily caught by Kael's shield. But Kael
didn't try to block with his sword. He treated it like a dead weight,
relying solely on the broad face of the shield. Yet, even in his
clumsiness, his instinct was there. Instead of meeting Daniels' blows
with a solid, jarring crash, he subtly angled his shield at the last
second, letting the wooden practice swords slide off with a scraping
sound. After a few moments of this, Daniels picked up the pace, his
attacks coming faster, from different angles. Kael was a mess of
flailing limbs, but he was never quite where the blow was supposed to
land. He'd turn, shift his weight, or angle the shield, and the attack
would glance away. He was frustratingly, impossibly difficult to connect
with.

"Enough!" Daniels barked, stepping back. "You're a rock in a river, boy.
Nothing but defense. A soldier must also be a storm! You must attack!
Now, attack!"

Kael simply stood there, alert with his sword and shield held ready. His
eyes were wide and focused, tracking every muscle twitch in the
Sergeant's body. But he made no move to attack. He was a coiled spring,
waiting.

Exasperated, Daniels decided to force the issue. He thrust forward with
his sword, a powerful lunge aimed directly at Kael's chest, a move
designed to break any recruit's nerve and shatter their guard.

And Kael moved.

It wasn't a retreat or a panicked block. In one fluid, economical
motion, he used the edge of his shield to deflect the Sergeant's blade,
sending the thrust harmlessly past his side. The move created an
instantaneous opening. In that same heartbeat, Kael stepped into the
void Daniels' lunge had created and, with perfect control, tapped him
firmly on the sword arm with the flat of his own blade. It was not a
strike meant to harm, but a clear, undeniable touch. He then immediately
dropped back into his defensive stance, the entire sequence over in less
than two seconds.

Dead silence filled the training yard. The other recruits, who had been
watching from the armory steps, stared in disbelief.

Daniels stood frozen, his sword arm tingling where Kael had tapped it.
The move was a perfect parry and riposte. It was a textbook maneuver
taught to veteran soldiers, one that took years to master until it
became instinct. This boy, who had held a sword for the first time less
than an hour ago, had just executed it flawlessly.

Slowly, Daniels lowered his weapon. His face was a mask of stone. "Where
did you learn that, boy?" he asked, his voice a low, dangerous growl. He
was searching for a lie, for some hint of prior training the boy had
concealed.

Kael looked genuinely puzzled by the question. He lowered his own sword
slightly, his brow furrowed in thought.

"From you, Sir!" he said, his voice clear and honest. "You said to be a
sloping hill. You said to let your anger slide past. Your lunge... it
had a lot of anger. I just showed it where else to go."

Daniels felt a cold chill run down his spine that had nothing to do with
the morning air. The boy hadn't been taught the move. He hadn't
memorized a sequence. He had *understood* the principle. He had taken
Daniels' metaphorical advice about the shield and applied it, instantly
and perfectly, to the intricate physics of swordplay.

He wasn't just talented. He wasn't just a prodigy. He was something else
entirely. Staff Sergeant Daniels lowered his practice sword completely,
looking at the farm boy as if for the very first time. He had come here
looking for soldiers. The King's Law had just given him a weapon unlike
any he had ever imagined.

Just then, there was a sharp *whack* of wood on bone, followed by a raw
cry of pain and the metallic clatter of a sword hitting the packed
earth.

The spell of intense focus between Daniels and Kael was broken. They
both turned to see one of the lanky farm boys, tears streaming down his
face, clutching his wrist. His practice sword lay in the dirt beside
him. Another recruit stood over him, looking pale and terrified, his own
wooden sword held limply.

From the deep shadows along the stone wall of the armory, a figure
emerged. It was a woman, moving with a silence that was utterly alien to
the noisy training yard. She was dressed in simple, immaculately white
robes that seemed to repel the dust and grime around her. She paid no
mind to Daniels or the other recruits, her entire focus on the injured
boy.

As she moved, Kael observed, almost as if in a trance. The world seemed
to narrow and muffle around him. The shouting of the other boys, the
grumbling of the Sergeant, the very feeling of the sword in his
hand---it all faded into a distant hum. His gaze was fixed on the woman
in white. It wasn't just her appearance that held him captive. He saw
her the same way he saw the grain in the yew branch or the "anger" in
Daniels' lunge. A profound, unwavering calm emanated from her, a force
as real and tangible as the heat from a forge. It was a quiet river
flowing through a world of crashing waves.

Daniels' posture stiffened, his face setting into a mask of grim
tolerance. "Healer," he bit out the word, a curt acknowledgement that
held no warmth.

The woman didn't respond or even look at him. She knelt beside the
crying boy, her movements fluid and unhurried. She gently took his
injured wrist in her hands. The boy flinched, but her touch seemed to
soothe him instantly.

Kael watched, his breath caught in his chest. As the Healer's hands
enveloped the boy's wrist, Kael saw a faint, pale green light, like the
first leaves of spring, flow from her palms. It wasn't a light for the
eyes, but a light for that other sense he possessed---the one that felt
the life in a tree and the intent behind a sword strike. He watched the
"pain," a jagged, angry red energy around the boy's arm, smooth out and
dissolve under the Healer's touch. The boy's sobs subsided into
sniffles, his face a mixture of relief and awe as he flexed his fingers.

The Healer whispered something to the boy, then rose gracefully to her
feet. She finally turned her gaze to Daniels, and her eyes, the color of
a winter sky, were piercing. They held no judgment, only a deep,
unnerving perception. Her look lingered for a moment on the Staff
Sergeant before flicking to Kael.

For a single, electric second, her eyes met his. Kael felt a jolt, a
shock of recognition, as if she could see directly into him and
understood the strange way he saw the world. She gave a single, almost
imperceptible nod, an acknowledgement that passed between them alone.

Then, as silently as she appeared, she turned and walked back into the
shadows, vanishing from sight.

Daniels let out a breath he seemed to have been holding. He glanced from
the now-healed recruit to Kael, who was still staring at the spot where
the woman had disappeared. He saw the boy's trancelike focus and the
dawning realization on his face. This boy, who could read the heart of
wood and the flow of battle, had just seen something else, something
Daniels himself could only perceive as an unwelcome but necessary
interruption.

The Staff Sergeant's world was one of steel, muscle, and a thousand
shades of violence. He was beginning to understand that Kael's world was
far, far larger.

Daniels narrowed his eyes as he looked at Kael again, his gaze
unsettling and sharp, like a man trying to spot a flaw in a perfect
diamond. The other recruits were slowly picking up their practice
swords, the drama of the broken wrist already fading, but Daniels
remained rooted to the spot. His world, once a predictable landscape of
mud, steel, and sweat, had been tilted on its axis. The Healer, the bow,
the swordplay... it was all too much to be a coincidence.

He took a slow step closer to Kael, his voice dropping from a drill
sergeant's roar to a low, conspiratorial murmur that was somehow more
intimidating.

"Kael," he began, the name tasting foreign on his tongue. "I asked
earlier about your bow. You told me *what* it was made of. Now, I want
you to tell me something else." He leaned in slightly. "Could you please
tell me *how* you made the bow? How did you know *how* to make it?"

The question was a blade in itself, precise and aimed at the heart of
the impossibility. It wasn't about materials anymore. It was about
knowledge. Knowledge that a sixteen-year-old farm boy had no right to
possess.

Kael's focus shifted from the shadows where the Healer had vanished back
to the Sergeant. He didn't seem intimidated by the man's intensity,
merely thoughtful, as if processing a simple but important query.

"From a Ranger," he said, his voice quiet but clear. "He came and stayed
at the Farm for a few days, last autumn. He was tracking something in
the quarry. I asked him about the bow he carried, and he explained how
it was made."

Daniels' eyes hardened into chips of flint. "A Ranger," he repeated, the
words laced with disbelief. Rangers were ghosts, legends whispered about
in taverns. They were the King's unseen knives in the dark, and they did
not stop at farms to chat with boys about their craft. "He *explained*
it? Just like that? A Ranger of the Crown shared the secrets of a yew
longbow with a random child?"

"I asked," Kael said with a slight shrug, as if this were the most
logical thing in the world. "He said it was better for knowledge to be
used than to be hoarded. He showed me the branch, Sir. He didn't just
use words."

Kael's eyes unfocused slightly, looking past Daniels as he recalled the
memory. "He said the bow was already inside the branch. That a bowyer's
job wasn't to make it, but to find it. To cut away all the parts that
weren't the bow. He ran his hand along the grain and showed me how it
flowed... how the light wood on the back wanted to stretch and the dark
wood on the belly wanted to be pushed. Once you see it... you can't
unsee it."

Daniels stood in stunned silence. The boy's explanation was both utterly
insane and, somehow, profoundly true. It was the kind of mystical
nonsense a master artisan might spend a lifetime trying to articulate,
yet Kael spoke it as a simple, observable fact. This wasn't a rehearsed
lie. It was a glimpse into a mind that operated on a completely
different level.

He looked from Kael's honest, open face to the other clumsy recruits
fumbling with their swords. He had a boy who could learn the art of the
sword by feeling the "anger" in a thrust, who was tutored by a mythical
Ranger, and who saw the life force in a Healer's touch.

The King's Law was meant to find boys with talent. Daniels was starting
to believe it had just delivered him something that would change the
very nature of the war. And that thought was far more terrifying than
any enemy he had ever faced.

Just then, another whack. A bit louder than the first. Another cry of
pain.

This from the other boy---the one who had been the aggressor moments
ago. It would appear a little revenge was in play. The newly healed
recruit, his fear replaced by a surge of righteous fury, had swung his
own practice sword and caught his tormentor squarely on the shoulder.

Before Daniels could bellow a curse and wade into the fray, and just as
the Healer stepped from the shadows again, her face a mask of weary
resignation, Kael was already moving.

He didn't run. He simply flowed. He dropped his own sword and shield
with a soft thud, the act of disarming himself so utterly contrary to
the situation that it was breathtaking. Unburdened, he covered the
distance in three long, silent strides, arriving just as the boy raised
his sword for a second, more vicious swing.

Kael didn't grab the boy's arm. He didn't shout or shove. He simply
stepped into the space between the two recruits and placed his open palm
flat on the aggressor's chest.

And everything stopped.

They both stood and watched, in pure shock.

From Daniels' perspective, it was madness. A raw recruit had just
willingly placed himself, unarmed, between two brawling youths, one of
whom was in a blind rage. It was a recipe for getting his skull caved
in. Yet, the enraged boy's arm, which had been raised to strike,
simply... froze. The fury seemed to drain out of him, leaving him
looking confused and suddenly exhausted, his sword lowering with a
trembling hand.

But the Healer saw something else entirely. Her eyes, which perceived
the world in flows of life and pain, went wide. She saw the jagged, red
fury coiling within the vengeful boy, a chaotic energy ready to lash out
again. And she saw Kael's hand touch him, and a calm, steady, deep
green---like moss on an ancient stone---flowed from Kael into the boy.
He wasn't pushing. He wasn't blocking. He was grounding the rage,
drawing the poison out, offering the storm a quiet harbor until it
exhausted itself. It was a form of healing she had never conceived
of---not mending a wound after the fact, but disarming the violence that
caused it in the first place.

Kael stood there for a second longer, a still point in the suddenly
silent yard. He removed his hand, and the recruit swayed, blinking as if
waking from a fever dream.

Daniels stared, his mind struggling to process what he had just
witnessed. This boy hadn't just learned to parry a sword thrust by
observing his opponent's 'anger'. He had just parried a human being.

The Healer stood motionless in the shadows, her hands clasped before
her. She was looking at a Ranger, a swordsman, and a Healer of a kind
she had never known existed, all wrapped in the unassuming form of a
sixteen-year-old boy from a farm. The shock on her face was not one of
confusion, but of profound, earth-shattering revelation.

Having disarmed the cycle of revenge, Kael turned to the boy who had
just been struck. The recruit was cradling his shoulder, his face
twisted in pain and surprise. Without a word, Kael reached out and
touched him. His hand rested gently on the afflicted shoulder, his
fingers spread wide.

His face became a mask of pure concentration. His brow furrowed, and his
eyes, though open, seemed to be looking at something far beyond the
physical world. He was no longer a lanky farm boy; he was an anchor, a
conduit for something ancient and powerful.

Impossible!

The thought was a silent, simultaneous explosion in the minds of the two
onlookers.

For Staff Sergeant Daniels, the world had officially ceased to make
sense. He watched as the wincing boy's expression slackened, replaced by
one of slack-jawed wonder. The boy experimentally rotated his arm, his
eyes wide. The pain was gone. Daniels had seen Healers work before---it
was a necessary part of army life. But it was *their* work. It was a
formal, mysterious process that belonged to robed figures and quiet
tents. It was not something a sixteen-year-old recruit did in the middle
of a drill yard, moments after displaying the combat instincts of a
master swordsman.

For the Healer, the shock was infinitely more profound. From her vantage
point in the shadows, she saw what Daniels could not. When Kael touched
the boy, she saw that same energy flow from him again. But this time it
wasn't the calming, moss-green of pacification. It was a vibrant, vital
green, the deep, untamed color of a primeval forest. It wasn't a gentle,
coaxing stream like her own healing light. It was a torrent, a flash
flood of life that surged into the boy's bruised tissues.

She saw the "damage"---the chaotic, angry reds and purples of torn
muscle and shattered capillaries---be utterly and instantly overwhelmed.
He wasn't mending it like a weaver fixing a tear; he was telling the
flesh how it was supposed to be, and it was listening. It was an act of
raw, instinctual creation. It was the kind of power she had only ever
read about in the oldest texts, a power that could not be taught, a
force of nature itself. To wield it, one had to be born of it.

Kael pulled his hand back, a slight sheen of sweat on his brow. He
looked a little tired, but otherwise unharmed. He gave the healed
recruit a simple nod, as if he'd just helped him lift a heavy bale of
hay.

The training yard was now utterly silent, save for the wind whispering
around the armory walls. The two brawling recruits stood dazed and
whole. The rest of the boys stared, petrified with an awe that bordered
on fear.

And in the center of it all stood Kael, a boy who could talk to wood,
feel the flow of a fight, soothe a raging heart, and command flesh to
heal.

Daniels and the Healer exchanged a look across the yard. Their worlds,
one of steel and one of spirit, had just found a terrifying, impossible
new center. The Sergeant's hand instinctively went to the pommel of his
sword, not in aggression, but as a man grasps for a solid object in the
midst of a world-shaking earthquake.

Having completely upended the natural order of the training yard, Kael
simply turned away from the two boys he had just fundamentally altered.
He walked to the side of the dusty yard, his shoulders slumping with a
weariness that hadn't been there moments before.

He sat down, his back leaning against the rough-hewn wood of a shield
rack. He yawned, a heavy, jaw-cracking sound of pure, bone-deep
exhaustion. His eyes, which had moments ago held an unnerving, ancient
focus, glazed over. Then, his head lolled to the side, and he drifted
off to sleep.

There, in the middle of a stunned and silent military compound,
surrounded by boys who now looked at him with a mixture of terror and
awe, Kael decided to take a nap.

Just before his consciousness faded completely, his lips parted, and a
single, soft sentence escaped into the air.

"That was kind of hard," he whispered.

The whisper, barely audible, was a thunderclap in the unnatural silence.

The two recruits Kael had touched stood frozen, staring at their own
hands as if they belonged to someone else. The other boys, who had been
inching forward to see what was happening, now scrambled backward,
creating a wide, empty circle around the sleeping figure. They looked at
each other with wide, fearful eyes, unspoken questions hanging between
them. Was he a demon? A saint? Whatever he was, he was not one of them.

Staff Sergeant Daniels stood like a statue carved from granite. His hand
was still clenched around the pommel of his sword, a useless anchor in a
sea of impossibility. His mind, a sharp and orderly tool honed by
decades of predictable violence, had simply ceased to function. It kept
replaying the events: the impossible shot with the bow, the flawless
riposte with the sword, the calming touch, the instantaneous healing.
And now, the nap. The sheer, baffling mundanity of the nap was the most
insane part of it all. Gods and monsters didn't get tired and doze off
against a shield rack.

The Healer stepped fully from the shadows, her customary calm shattered.
Her hands were pressed to her mouth, her eyes fixed on the sleeping boy.
She understood the cost of power. To mend a simple fracture required
meditation, herbs, and a significant expenditure of her own life force,
leaving her drained for hours. Kael had performed two acts of profound,
world-altering power back-to-back, and the price was... a nap. It was
the equivalent of a man lifting a mountain and then complaining of a
slight backache.

The hardened soldier and the serene mystic stood on opposite sides of
the yard, separated by a lifetime of different experiences, but united
in a single, terrifying realization. The King's Law had not found them a
talented recruit.

It had found something the world had forgotten existed. And it was
sleeping in their midst like a child after a long day's play.

The Healer and the Staff Sergeant simply stared at each other across the
fifty feet of dusty ground that now felt like a chasm. The other
recruits, sensing a shift in the very fabric of authority, remained
frozen in a wide, fearful perimeter. The only sound was the gentle
breeze and the soft, rhythmic breathing of the sleeping boy. They were
two pillars of the established order---the master of the body and the
master of the blade---and both had been rendered utterly irrelevant.

Her eyes, usually a placid gray, were wide with a turmoil that Daniels
had never seen. "Kind of hard?" she whispered, the words barely carrying
across the yard. The question was not for him, but for the universe
itself. It was a sound of pure, unadulterated disbelief.

Daniels was still processing the whole "bow" thing. His mind, which was
built to analyze tactics and weaknesses, had snagged on the first
impossibility and was refusing to let go. The healing, the calming
touch---that was magic, a Healer's domain. Strange, yes, but it belonged
to a category of things he didn't have to understand. Craftsmanship,
however, was his religion.

"The Ranger 'told' him," Daniels said, his voice a low, gravelly rasp of
confusion, "and then 'showed' him... how to make a bow of yew?" He
looked at the Healer, his expression desperate for some kind of rational
anchor. "Do you know what that means? Master bowyers train their entire
lives and most never succeed with a single stave of yew. It's not a
lesson. It's a lifetime!"

The Healer took a slow step forward, her gaze locked on Kael's sleeping
form. "Sergeant," she said, her voice trembling slightly, "what you call
a lifetime of training, he calls an afternoon's conversation. What I
call the sacred art of healing... a power that drains my very soul to
mend a simple bone... he calls 'kind of hard'."

She finally looked back at Daniels, and for the first time, he saw a
glimmer of fear in her serene face. "I did not mend that boy's wrist,
Sergeant. I guided the flesh, coaxed it, pleaded with it to knit itself
back together. This boy... he did not guide. He commanded. He reached
into that other boy's shoulder and reminded it of what it was before it
was broken. I have never seen anything like it. It is not a power one
learns."

Daniels scrubbed a hand over his short-cropped hair, the gesture failing
to wipe away the fog in his brain. He was a man of practicalities. A
problem was something you could stab, flank, or intimidate. Kael was
none of those things.

"So what is he?" Daniels demanded, his voice dropping low. "Some kind of
sorcerer's get? A hedge-witch's bastard?"

"No," the Healer said, her voice firm, cutting through his crass
speculation. "He is none of those things. They manipulate the forces.
He... I think he *is* one."

They both fell silent again, the weight of her words settling between
them. The wind kicked up a small dust devil that danced across the yard
before collapsing. The King's Law was designed to find the sharpest
stones to hone into blades for the war. It was not designed to find a
mountain.

Daniels looked at the sleeping boy, this impossible child who learned
mastery from a conversation and healed wounds with a touch, only to be
tired out by it all. He wasn't a recruit. He wasn't a soldier. He was a
living, breathing violation of the laws of the world. And he was
Daniels' responsibility.

With a sharp, almost reflexive gesture, Daniels barked, "Dismissed! Get
to the mess hall! Not a word of this to anyone. You understand me? Not.
A. Word."

The recruits, grateful for an escape from the palpable tension,
practically scrambled over each other in their haste to obey. They cast
fearful, backward glances at the sleeping boy as they fled, the strange
events of the morning already beginning to curdle into the stuff of myth
and barrack-room legend.

In moments, the training yard was empty, save for the Staff Sergeant,
the Healer, and the sleeping boy who was the cause of their shared
crisis.

"He can't stay here," the Healer said, her voice a low, urgent whisper.
"The barracks... the drills... this is a forge for iron, Sergeant. He is
something else. This place will break him, or he will break it."

"And where do you suggest he goes?" Daniels countered, his voice a
frustrated growl. "He is an enrolled recruit under the King's Law. I
can't just send him home because he makes a yew bow and heals with a
touch. I have to report this. But who do I report it to? What do I even
say? 'Sir, Recruit Kael is a walking miracle who gets sleepy after
performing impossible feats. Requesting new orders'?" He spat on the
ground. "They'd have me in a madhouse."

They continued to whisper, a frantic, hushed council of war between two
people whose worlds had been violently merged. They circled the sleeping
boy, their voices a low murmur of disbelief and dawning, terrifying
responsibility.

About an hour later, Kael stirred. He blinked a few times, his eyes
adjusting to the afternoon sun, which had climbed higher in the sky. He
sat up, stretched his arms over his head with a groan of satisfaction,
and looked around the empty training yard at the two adults staring at
him as if he had just grown a second head.

He gave them a slightly sheepish, lopsided smile.

"Oh, I'm sorry," he said, his voice thick with sleep. "I must have dozed
off. Did I miss dinner? I'm kind of hungry."

Kael's simple, honest question hung in the air for a moment before the
Healer's composure finally, completely, and spectacularly shattered.

"An hour?" she burst out, her voice a reedy, incredulous shriek that was
utterly alien to her character. She took a staggering step backward, her
hands flying to her temples.

"AN HOUR?" she yelled, the sound echoing off the stone walls of the
armory. "How is that even FAIR!?"

Daniels, who had been bracing for some new form of mystical weirdness,
was so taken aback by her very human explosion that he flinched. He
stared at the Healer, this icon of tranquility, who now looked like she
was about to tear her hair out.

Kael just blinked, confused by the outburst. "I'm sorry," he offered,
thinking he had somehow been rude. "Was I supposed to sleep longer?"

The Healer let out a sound that was half-sob, half-laugh. "Longer? Boy,
what you did... the power you used... it should have scoured you to the
bone! It should have left you in a coma for a week! To mend a fraction
of what you mended, to calm a fraction of the rage you calmed... it
would take me a full day of fasting and meditation just to recover! I
would be bedridden! You sleep for an *hour* and you wake up complaining
that you're *hungry*?"

She threw her hands up in a gesture of absolute surrender to the
absurdity of it all. The cosmic balance she had dedicated her life to
understanding had just been mocked, relentlessly, by a farm boy's
supernatural metabolism.

Daniels stared from the frantic Healer to the bewildered Kael. The boy's
complete lack of understanding was the most convincing proof of all. He
truly had no idea what he'd done. He didn't see it as a monumental
expenditure of life-altering power. He saw it as a task that had made
him tired and hungry.

The Sergeant's mind, finally overwhelmed, bypassed all the complex,
impossible questions and landed on the only one it could solve. He
pointed a thick, trembling finger toward the barracks.

"The mess hall," he said, his voice a strained, gravelly command. "Is
that way. Go. Eat."

"Oh. Thank you, Sergeant," Kael said with genuine relief. He scrambled
to his feet, brushed the dust from his trousers, and with a final,
polite nod to the distraught Healer, he trotted off in the direction
Daniels had pointed, his mind clearly on nothing more than bread and
stew.

Daniels and the Healer were left alone in the silent yard, the force of
her outburst having dissipated into a stunned, trembling silence.

"Did you see his face?" Daniels finally grunted. "He has no idea. Not a
clue."

The Healer slowly lowered her hands, her breathing still ragged. "That,
Sergeant," she said, her voice filled with a new and profound dread, "is
the most terrifying thing I have ever seen in my life."

The Sergeant's gruff voice was so low it was almost swallowed by the
wind. It was the sound of a man admitting his map of the world was
useless, that he had sailed off the edge and into a land of monsters.

"We need a mage." Daniels whispered to the healer. "Do you know of any
nearby?"

The Healer, who had regained a semblance of her professional calm, let
out a long, slow breath. The question was a heavy stone, and she seemed
to weigh it carefully before answering.

"One does not find a mage 'nearby', Sergeant," she replied, her own
voice a hushed murmur. "They are not like traveling tinkers. The ones
who are worth anything are either locked away in the high towers of the
capital, drowning in politics and theory, or they are..." She trailed
off, her gaze becoming distant.

"Or they are what?" Daniels pressed, his patience worn to a thread.

"Or they are hermits," she said, finally meeting his gaze. "They are men
and women who have seen enough of the world and its rules that they have
chosen to leave it entirely. There is one. Perhaps."

She hesitated, chewing on her lip. To speak the name was to invite a
power far more complicated and dangerous than a boy who healed with a
touch.

"He lives on the slopes of the Greyfang mountains, a day's hard ride
from here. He keeps to himself in an old watchtower. The locals call him
the 'Old Man of the Tor'. His real name is Elias."

"Can he help?" Daniels asked, the simple, desperate question of a man
out of options.

"I do not know if 'help' is a word Elias still comprehends," the Healer
answered, a chill in her tone. "He was once a Master of Theory at the
White Towers. His specialty was... irregularities. Phenomena that did
not align with the known laws of magic. He saw a 'glitch' in the world
and would obsess over it until he understood its nature."

She looked pointedly in the direction Kael had gone. "This boy is not a
glitch, Sergeant. He is a whole new design. To Elias, he would be...
irresistible."

Daniels processed this, his brow furrowed in a deep, troubled scowl. A
powerful, obsessive recluse who specialized in freaks of nature. It
sounded like a terrible idea. It was also the only idea they had.

"What's the risk?" he asked.

"The risk?" The Healer gave a sad, bitter smile. "The risk is that Elias
will see Kael not as a person, but as a puzzle to be taken apart to see
how it works. Or worse, he will see him as a weapon to be aimed. Mages
do not think like you or I. We see a boy. They see a force to be
quantified and, if possible, controlled."

They stood in silence for a long moment, the two of them, a soldier and
a healer, bound by a secret that could either save the kingdom or tear
it apart.

"I'll send a rider in the morning," Daniels said at last, his voice
heavy with resignation. He looked toward the mess hall, where Kael was
likely devouring a second bowl of stew, utterly oblivious to the
monumental forces beginning to pivot around him.

"Pray we are doing the right thing," the Healer whispered.

"I'm a soldier," Daniels grunted, turning to walk away. "I stopped
praying a long time ago."
