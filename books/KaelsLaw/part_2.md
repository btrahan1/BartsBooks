For the next few days, nothing 'out of the ordinary' happened.

Assuming, of course, that it was perfectly ordinary for a
sixteen-year-old boy to grasp the fundamental concepts of every weapon
in the royal arsenal within minutes of picking it up. The spear was not
a clumsy pole but an extension of his arm, its balance and reach
understood instinctively. The battle axe was not a brute's tool but a
lesson in physics, its weighted head used for devastating,
momentum-driven arcs that he seemed to calculate without thought.

And then there was the bow.

Daniels had tried to integrate Kael into the regular archery practice.
It was a disaster. While the other recruits struggled to send their
arrows wobbling into the general vicinity of the straw-stuffed targets
fifty yards away, Kael grew bored. So Daniels, in a moment of
exasperated genius, set up a single target for him at the far end of the
field. One hundred yards. A distance for volleys, not for marksmanship.

Kael didn't miss. He never missed. After the first day of simply
peppering the bullseye until it was a splintered hole, he had started to
play.

Daniels stood with his arms crossed, a muscle twitching in his jaw,
watching the boy's work. *Thunk.* An arrow appeared high on the left of
the target. *Thunk.* Another appeared, perfectly mirrored on the right.
*Thunk. Thunk. Thunk.* Three more, forming a jaunty, crooked smile
underneath.

The damn boy was drawing faces on the target with his arrows, for
Christ's sake!

The other recruits no longer treated Kael with simple fear. They treated
him like a religious omen. They gave him a wide berth in the mess hall.
They fell silent when he walked into the barracks. He wasn't one of
them, and his effortless, impossible perfection was a constant, glaring
reminder of their own clumsy inadequacies.

Kael, for his part, seemed oblivious. He was quiet, polite, and did
everything that was asked of him with a serene, untroubled focus. He
never boasted. He never showed off, unless one counted the archery art
as showing off, and Daniels got the distinct impression Kael was just
trying to keep his mind occupied.

The Staff Sergeant and the Healer had formed a silent, weary pact. They
would exchange long-suffering looks across the training yard whenever
Kael did something new and baffling. She would occasionally bring
Daniels a cup of a calming herbal tea, a gesture he accepted with a
grunt that she had come to interpret as profound gratitude.

Every morning, Daniels scanned the horizon for a sign of the rider he
had sent. Every evening, he was met with nothing but the setting sun. He
was a man holding a lit stick of dynamite, praying the fuse was a long
one. He wasn't training a recruit; he was babysitting a natural
disaster.

He walked down the archery line, his boots crunching on the gravel. He
stopped beside Kael, who was pulling another arrow from his quiver.

"Son," Daniels said, his voice strained. "Could you at least try to look
like you're taking this seriously?"

Kael paused and looked at the smiling face he'd made on the target, then
back at the Sergeant. "I am, Sir," he said with genuine earnestness. "I
was just wondering if I could make the nose a little more crooked on the
next one."

The sparring match was, like all of Kael's training, a complete farce.

Daniels had decided that since Kael could effortlessly disarm any single
opponent, the only way to challenge him was with numbers. So, he had
pitted Kael against three of the biggest recruits simultaneously.

It didn't help. Kael, armed with only a blunted wooden staff, was a
whirlwind of quiet, efficient motion. He wasn't fighting them; he was
conducting them. A nudge here, a trip there, using one boy's clumsy
lunge to unbalance another. The three recruits were flailing,
frustrated, and spending more time colliding with each other than
getting anywhere near Kael. It was like watching three dogs trying to
catch a butterfly.

Suddenly, in the middle of a fluid spin that sent one boy tumbling
harmlessly into the dust, Kael stopped. Dead still. He held up a hand,
not to the boys, but as if to quiet the world itself. He ignored the two
remaining opponents who skidded to a confused halt, and stared intently
towards the main entrance to the training grounds.

A man, with a long white beard, a ridiculously pointy hat, and long,
sapphire-blue robes strolled in, talking to himself.

He seemed utterly oblivious to his surroundings, his gaze fixed on some
middle distance as he ambled across the packed earth of the military
compound.

"...which, of course, means the entire premise of trans-dimensional
resonance is flawed!" the man muttered, gesturing emphatically to an
invisible colleague. "You can't apply runic sequencing to a
non-Euclidean matrix! It's academic malpractice, is what it is!"

The few recruits still lingering in the yard stopped and stared at the
bizarre apparition. This was a place of steel, sweat, and shouted
orders. A man dressed like a storybook wizard, debating theoretical
magic with himself, was so out of place it felt like a hallucination.

Daniels felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. The rider had returned
late last night with a simple message: "He is coming."

Across the yard, the Healer had also emerged from her tent, her face
pale. She gave a single, almost imperceptible shake of her head.
*Elias.*

But Kael wasn't looking at a man. He was watching a phenomenon. To his
unique senses, the air around the newcomer was... buzzing. It was a low,
powerful hum, like a thousand iron spiders spinning their silk at once.
It was a color he had never seen, a deep, crackling indigo that seemed
to bend the light around it.

The wizard, Elias, finally seemed to notice he was no longer alone. He
stopped his muttering, blinked, and looked around the yard as if
surprised to find it there. His sharp, intelligent eyes swept over the
dumbfounded recruits, past the grim-faced Sergeant and the apprehensive
Healer, and then they fell on Kael.

The buzzing intensified. The air grew thick. Elias's rambling, academic
demeanor vanished, replaced by an expression of utter, predatory focus.
He was no longer a man looking at a boy. He was a master craftsman who
had just stumbled upon a piece of raw material he had previously
believed to be purely theoretical.

"Oh," the wizard said, his voice no longer a mumble but a sharp,
resonant declaration that cut through the silence. "Well now. Isn't that
interesting."

The air, thick with crackling indigo energy, hung heavy in the training
yard. Daniels' hand was white-knuckled on his sword hilt. The Healer
looked as if she might faint. The three sparring recruits were frozen
like statues. And in the center of this maelstrom of tension, Kael
offered a simple, human observation.

"I like your hat." Kael simply smiled at the wizard.

The smile was not sarcastic or foolish. It was open, genuine, and
utterly devoid of the fear or awe that was suffocating everyone else.

The wizard, Elias, froze. The intense, predatory focus in his eyes
wavered, replaced by a flicker of profound confusion. It was as if a
master astronomer, having finally located a mythical star, had been
greeted by the star winking at him. For a long, silent moment, he just
stared. He tilted his head, his long white beard shifting on his robes.

Staff Sergeant Daniels squeezed his eyes shut and let out a low,
guttural groan. It was the sound of a man's sanity fraying one thread at
a time. *Of all the things to say...*

The Healer's hand flew to her mouth, her eyes wide with terror. She knew
of mages and their colossal egos. One did not engage a man like Elias
with flippant observations about his attire.

Elias finally broke the silence. A slow, dry smile crept across his
lips, though it did not reach his piercing eyes. "Do you?" he said, his
voice laced with a strange, analytical amusement. He tapped the brim of
the pointy hat with a long, bony finger. "It is a meticulously crafted
artifact designed to focus and channel ambient arcane energies. Its
conical structure creates a thaumaturgical vortex, optimizing somatic
amplification."

He paused, his gaze boring into Kael. "But yes. It is a rather nice
hat."

Elias took another step forward, the playful moment gone, his intensity
returning tenfold. He ignored Daniels and the Healer as if they were
furniture.

"Now," he said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that
seemed to boom in the silent yard. "Tell me. When you healed that boy...
did the energy feel warm or cold when it left your hands?"

The wizard's analytical smile froze on his face. He had asked a simple
diagnostic question, expecting a simple, brutish answer. What he got was
a cascade of revelations that seemed to fundamentally rewrite the rules
of his world.

Kael's eyes drifted off into space for a moment, his head tilted as if
listening to a distant song. He mumbled the wizard's own words back to
him, tasting them. "Ambient arcane energies..." His eyes refocused, a
flicker of understanding dawning within them. "...and then 'vortex...
amplification'..."

A slow, brilliant smile spread across Kael's own face, the smile of a
student finally grasping a difficult but elegant concept. "Oh," he said,
looking at the hat with newfound appreciation. "So it is a 'focus' of
sorts?"

Elias felt the air suck from his lungs. The boy hadn't just heard the
words; he'd dissected them, understood their function, and derived their
purpose in seconds. It was a feat of intuitive logic that would have
taken a First Circle apprentice a month of study.

But Kael wasn't finished.

"Oh, and the energy?" he continued, answering the original question with
a casualness that was almost violent in its implications. "It didn't
come from my hands. It came from my core, I think it's called." He
gestured vaguely to his own solar plexus. "And it was cold at first,
then warmed up as I channeled it."

He paused, and his expression shifted to one of genuine, fresh
discovery. He looked from the wizard's buzzing indigo aura back to his
own hands as if seeing them for the first time.

"I didn't even know that's what I was doing until we just met, you and
I."

That was the final blow. Elias, the Master of Theory, the man who had
abandoned the White Towers because his colleagues were too slow,
physically staggered back a step. His face went pale beneath his beard.

Daniels and the Healer watched, uncomprehending of the specific words
but fluent in the language of shock. The Healer put a hand to her chest,
her heart hammering. To draw from the core was the holy grail of all
power-wielders, a skill that took decades of brutal discipline to even
attempt. Kael spoke of it like a man describing his own breathing.

Elias stared at Kael, his mind, a fortress of logic and arcane law,
being torn down stone by stone. 1. The boy had intuitively understood
the function of a complex arcane artifact from a single, jargon-filled
sentence. 2. He was instinctively drawing power from his core, the
deepest and purest wellspring, a feat reserved for archmages. 3. He
could describe the specific thermal dynamics of his energy transfer, a
key indicator of a raw, untamed power source. 4. And most impossibly,
the boy's own arcane awareness had been *awakened* by simple proximity
to another mage. He was a resonant void, a walking miracle of untapped
potential that was so vast, it was learning about itself in real time.

The wizard's academic curiosity vanished, burned away by an obsessive
fire that bordered on madness. He was no longer looking at a puzzle. He
was staring into an abyss of infinite, beautiful possibility.

He took a step forward, his eyes blazing with a terrifying light. He
completely ignored the Sergeant and the Healer. There was no one else in
the world but him and the boy.

"Forget the mess hall," Elias said, his voice a low, trembling command.
"You and I are going to have a long, long talk."

If Elias had staggered before, he now looked as if he'd been physically
struck by a battering ram. His jaw, which had been hanging slightly
ajar, snapped shut with an audible click. His hand flew instinctively to
his chest, covering the amulet that had been partially hidden beneath
his beard.

Kael's torrent of innocent, devastating questions continued.

"Cool! I have questions about your robe, your staff, and the wand in
your pockets. The amulet around your neck is interesting, and doesn't
having 3 rings on the same hand cause an issue with their ambient
industries? .... Are you hungry? Our mess hall has some food, but it's
not the best."

Daniels made a sound like a dying animal. He clamped a hand over his
mouth to stifle it. *The wand in his pockets.* Plural. The boy had
X-rayed a master wizard with a casual glance.

The Healer, however, went white as her own robes. She wasn't focused on
the inventory of items; she was focused on the last, garbled question.
*Ambient industries.* It was a child's misinterpretation of "energies,"
but the core of the query was so advanced it bordered on blasphemy. To
question the harmonic resonance of a mage's chosen artifacts was to
question their very competence, and it required a perception of arcane
forces that was, frankly, impossible.

Elias's mind, a grand library of arcane knowledge, was now on fire. The
boy had, in a single breath: 1. Identified every single one of his
attuned artifacts, including a masterfully concealed hold-out wand in
his sleeve. 2. Questioned their synergy, a famously complex and volatile
field of arcanum that had driven lesser mages insane. 3. And then, with
breathtaking whiplash, invited him to dinner.

The wizard's composure, his pride, his very sense of self---it all
crumbled into dust. He was a god of knowledge being asked for a book
report by a sentient, all-knowing cloud. He opened his mouth, then
closed it. He looked at his own hand, at the three rings that had taken
him fifty years to balance, and felt a sudden, sickening doubt. Was
their resonance field slightly off?

He was saved by the final, blessedly simple question. It was the only
part of Kael's monologue his reeling mind could latch onto. It was an
anchor of simple, biological need in an ocean of cosmic insanity.

"Food," Elias croaked, his voice a dry, papery whisper. He cleared his
throat, trying to regain a sliver of his former authority and failing
miserably. "Yes. Food. Food would be... adequate."

"Great!" Kael beamed, completely oblivious to the intellectual
devastation he had just wrought. He turned, ready to lead the way. "It's
stew today. It's better than the gruel we had yesterday, but not as good
as the roasted chicken on Feast Day."

Elias the archmage, the hermit of the Tor, the master of irregularities,
took a shaky, shuffling step forward and meekly followed the
sixteen-year-old farm boy toward the promise of mediocre stew.

Daniels and the Healer stood alone in the yard, watching the two
figures---the small, unassuming boy and the grand, collapsing
wizard---walk away.

"He broke him," Daniels whispered in a tone of pure, unadulterated awe.
"He broke a wizard in less than five minutes."

The pair ate in silence in the noisy mess hall. Or rather, Kael ate,
methodically working his way through a bowl of the watery stew. Elias
merely stared into the air above his own untouched food, a statue carved
from bewilderment. His thoughts were a hurricane, a chaotic storm of
arcane theory and shattered laws of physics.

"*Innate somatic channeling... an intuitive grasp of harmonic
resonance... a self-awakening consciousness... HOW, HOW?*"

And then he heard it. It wasn't a sound. It was a thought, clear as a
bell in the center of his mental storm, and it was not his own.

...Can you hear me?...

Elias started so violently he nearly shot out of his seat. His eyes,
wide with a fresh, deeper level of shock, snapped to Kael, who was
calmly spooning more stew into his mouth.

*WHAT!* The wizard's thought was not a word but a psychic explosion, a
blast of pure, panicked astonishment that should have flattened any
lesser mind.

Kael flinched, his eyes squeezing shut for a second. He put his spoon
down. *Ouch! Too loud!* the mental voice complained, sounding more
annoyed than hurt. *But you heard me... here...*

Kael reached across the table and touched the back of the wizard's hand.
Elias felt a jolt, not of static, but of life. A warm, green glow,
invisible to Daniels but blindingly apparent to the Healer watching from
the doorway, flowed from Kael's fingertips into the wizard.

You think REALLY loud! Try now.

The Healer gasped, a sharp, choked sound of utter disbelief.

Daniels, who had been observing the silent, bizarre meal from a "safe"
distance, looked at her, his face a mask of exasperation. "What? What
now?" he hissed.

"I..." she stammered, pointing a trembling finger. "That green light...
his healing... he's not touching a wound, Sergeant. He's touching his
*mind*."

For Elias, the sensation was beyond anything he had ever known. The
chaotic tempest in his head didn't just stop; it was soothed. The raging
winds of his intellect were calmed, the crashing waves of his panic were
gentled into a placid pool. It was as if a master gardener had walked
into his hurricane and, with a simple touch, had convinced it to become
a peaceful meadow. The warm, green energy was tidying up the wreckage of
his thoughts.

Into that newfound, impossible silence, Kael's mental voice came again,
soft and clear. *Okay. That's better. Now you try. Just... think a
question.*

Elias stared at his own hand, still under Kael's touch. His terror was
being eclipsed by a boundless, scientific awe. He took a shaky mental
breath, focused his will for the first time in what felt like an
eternity, and projected a single, coherent thought.

...How are you doing this?...

Kael pulled his hand back and picked up his spoon, a slight frown on his
face as if the answer was obvious. He answered out loud.

"Doing what?"

Elias's spoon, held loosely in his trembling hand, clattered into his
bowl of stew, splashing lukewarm broth onto the rough-hewn table. He
paid it no mind. His eyes were locked on Kael, two blue points of
searing intensity in a face that had gone ashen.

"You can read my thoughts?" the wizard whispered, the words ragged.

Kael tilted his head, a look of genuine confusion on his face. He
swallowed the mouthful of stew he'd been chewing.

"No, of course not," he said, as if explaining that the sky was blue.
"You were just projecting them to everyone. No one can read someone's
mind. That would be silly."

And with that one, simple, catastrophically profound statement,
something inside Elias finally snapped.

A sound escaped his throat. It started as a dry, choked gasp, then
twisted into a single, sharp bark of a laugh. Then another. And another.
Soon, Elias, the Old Man of the Tor, the Master of Irregularities, was
leaning back on the bench, clutching his sides and roaring with a
silent, wracking laughter that produced no sound but shook his entire
body. Tears streamed from his eyes, carving paths through the grime of
his travels and getting lost in his beard.

In the doorway, Daniels stared at the spectacle, his face a granite mask
of utter defeat. He looked at the Healer, his eyes pleading for an
explanation for this new, unhinged development.

"What in the seven hells is wrong with him now?"

The Healer was pale, her hand covering her own mouth. "Sergeant," she
breathed, her voice filled with a terrible, professional awe. "He didn't
say he could read his mind. He said the wizard's thoughts are so loud he
can't *help* but hear them." She shook her head in disbelief. "He is
treating an Archmage's mental discipline... like a child shouting in a
library."

Daniels put his head in his hands and began rubbing his temples in a
slow, circular motion. He had reached his absolute limit. He was done.

Elias's silent, manic laughter finally subsided into shuddering gasps.
He wiped the tears from his eyes with the back of his sleeve and looked
at Kael with a gaze that was no longer proud or analytical. It was the
look of a devout priest who had just met his god, and his god had turned
out to be a cheerful, slightly dim-witted farm animal. Everything he had
ever known, every theory he had ever built, every law he had held
sacred, was a joke. A silly, cosmic joke.

Kael, having finished his stew, looked at the wizard's untouched bowl
and then at his own empty one. He had clearly misinterpreted the
wizard's breakdown as some form of strange, silent entertainment.

He flagged down a passing mess-hall private. "Excuse me," he said
politely. "Is there any more bread?"

A mess-hall private, looking profoundly uncomfortable, scurried over and
placed a small, coarse loaf of dark bread on the table between them.

Elias had just managed to rein in his silent hysterics, his mind a
wasteland of broken theories. He stared at the bread as if it were a
complex runic text he couldn't decipher. Kael, however, looked at it
with a practical eye.

"Can you cast a fireball?" he asked, his tone one of simple, academic
curiosity. "Or... no, that would not be good in here." He glanced around
at the wooden walls and ceiling with a thoughtful frown. "Can you...
warm the bread? I would love to see that."

The request was so earnest, so mundane, so completely and utterly
disconnected from the cosmic scale of the morning's events that it
finally broke the remaining vestiges of Elias the Archmage.

He slumped. His shoulders, which had been held with the rigid tension of
a man trying to contain a supernova in his skull, sagged in utter
defeat. He looked at the boy, then at the bread, then at his own
hands---hands that had wrestled with demons, rewritten the laws of
translocation, and shaped raw chaos into orderly spells.

All his knowledge, all his power, all his pride, had led him to this
moment. To be a human warming plate for a supernatural teenager.

From the doorway, Daniels saw the wizard's posture collapse and
instinctively braced himself for some new magical horror. The Healer
simply put a hand over her eyes, as if she couldn't bear to watch the
final, pathetic humiliation.

With a sigh that seemed to carry the weight of all the books he had ever
read, Elias lifted a single, trembling finger. He pointed it at the
loaf. There was no incantation, no flash of light, no grand gesture. A
faint, shimmering orange haze, like heat off a summer road, enveloped
the bread for a barest second and then vanished. A wisp of steam curled
up from the dark crust.

Elias slumped even further, resting his forehead on the table with a
soft *thud*. He was spent. Not his magic, but his will to live.

Kael's face lit up with pure, unadulterated delight. He leaned forward,
his nose close to the bread, and took a deep sniff.

"Oh, that's wonderful!" he beamed. He tore a piece off, blew on it
gently, and popped it into his mouth. "It's all warm and soft on the
inside now. Neat!"

He held the rest of the loaf out to the wizard's slumped form.

"Do you want some?"

Kael offered the warm bread to his new friend, holding the loaf out over
the table.

Elias didn't move. His forehead remained pressed against the cool,
splintery wood of the mess hall table, a monument to a shattered
worldview.

Seeing his offer was going unnoticed, Kael shrugged and tore off another
piece for himself. As he chewed, his eyes fell upon the wizard's mug.
The dark ale inside, long ignored, had gone to room temperature. Kael
looked at the slumped wizard, then at the mug, and a thoughtful
expression crossed his face. He seemed to come to a decision.

He placed his hands on either side of the thick ceramic mug, not quite
touching it. He focused, his brow furrowing in the now-familiar mask of
deep concentration. The air around the mug seemed to shimmer and grow
still. He drew his hands away.

A delicate, crystalline ring of frost appeared on the mug, spreading
rapidly down its sides. The dark ale inside swirled once, a tiny ice
crystal forming on its surface.

"Oh..." Kael whispered, his voice faint. His face had gone pale.
"...that was kind of hard."

Then, like a puppet with its strings cut, he slumped over, and put his
head down on the table next to the wizard's untouched bowl. Fast asleep.

The sudden silence was broken by the soft groan of wood as Elias slowly,
painfully, lifted his head. He blinked, his eyes red-rimmed and
unfocused. He saw the boy, asleep. Then his gaze fell upon his mug. He
saw the frost, the impossible, perfect rime of ice that clung to the
ceramic. He hesitantly reached out a trembling finger and touched it.
The cold was sharp, real.

In the doorway, Daniels just stared, his jaw slack. He was a man
watching a flood, a hurricane, and an earthquake happen all at once in a
single room. He had no more reactions left. He was an empty vessel of
shock.

But the Healer let out a sound, a tiny, wounded whimper.

"What is it now?" Daniels asked, his voice a flat, dead thing. "He made
a drink cold. After the rest of it, that barely registers."

"Sergeant," she whispered, her voice shaking so badly she could barely
form the words. "You don't understand. The energy of life, of healing...
it is warmth. Creation. Addition." She pointed a trembling finger at the
frosty mug. "That... is the opposite. That is stillness. Subtraction.
The absolute removal of energy. They are polar forces. Fire and Ice.
Life and Stillness."

She finally looked at him, her eyes vast pools of professional,
spiritual terror.

"One person should not contain both. It is a violation of the very laws
of nature."

Elias stood, his chair scraping loudly against the stone floor. He
backed away from the table, his eyes never leaving Kael's sleeping form.
It was not a retreat of fear, but of a man stepping back from a
masterpiece to see its full, terrifying scope. He backed all the way to
the doorway where the duo stood, his frantic energy replaced by a
chilling, profound calm.

"No," he said, his voice quiet but resonant, directly answering the
Healer's terrified whisper. "It is not. Many think so, but in the end,
it's all energy."

The Healer stared at him, aghast. "But Master Elias, the disciplines..."

"The disciplines are crutches!" Elias interrupted, his eyes blazing with
the fire of revelation. "We build walls, fences, and pathways for magic
because our minds are too small to navigate the wilderness. We
specialize because we *have* to. Fire or Ice. Life or Stillness. We
choose one path because we would be torn apart trying to walk them all."

He pointed a shaking finger back at the table. "That boy... he doesn't
see the paths. He sees the landscape. I showed him 'heat'. I applied a
specific formula to add thermal energy. He didn't learn the formula. He
grasped the *concept*. And then he just... reversed it. In his mind,
'cold' is simply the opposite of what I showed him. The same way he uses
what *you* showed him with your healing." Elias looked at her. "He saw
you 'add' life force, so he understood the principle and applied it
himself. He just 'applies' the concept and shapes it to what he wants."

A sharp, audible gasp came not from the Healer, but from Staff Sergeant
Daniels. His face, which had been a mask of stoic confusion, was now a
canvas of shocking, dawning horror. The wizard's abstract, magical
explanation had just slammed into his own concrete, physical world with
the force of a battering ram.

"The same way with weapons..." Daniels breathed, his voice hoarse.

Elias and the Healer both turned to look at him.

"They aren't different weapons to him," the Sergeant continued, his eyes
wide as he stared at Kael. "The sword, the axe, the spear... I was
trying to teach him forms, stances. But he doesn't see them. He sees the
principles beneath them." His voice grew stronger as the terrifying
truth took shape. "They are all just different extensions of ways to
apply force, redirect force, cut, pierce, blunt force... to him... it's
all the same?"

"Precisely," Elias whispered, his voice filled with a terrible, ecstatic
awe.

The three of them stood there, a triumvirate of shattered expertise, and
looked at the boy sleeping peacefully with his head on the table. He
wasn't a warrior, or a healer, or a mage. He was something more
fundamental.

He didn't learn the rules. He understood how the game was played. And in
a world governed by rules, there was no more dangerous or powerful thing
to be.

The Healer sighed, a long, weary sound that seemed to carry the weight
of the entire conversation. The two men, a soldier and a wizard, were
staring at the sleeping boy as if he were a loaded cannon with an
unpredictable fuse. She saw something else.

"But he is so.... so... innocent!" she said, her voice soft. She
gestured at Kael, who was breathing the deep, untroubled breaths of a
child. "You see a weapon. You see a paradox. I see a boy who gets tired
after he does something amazing. In an hour, he will wake up and be
hungry. Mark my words."

The three of them stood in a silent, unwilling conspiracy. Eventually,
Daniels made a decision. With a grunt, he gently hoisted Kael from the
bench, draping him over his shoulder in a fireman's carry. The boy was
surprisingly light. Without a word, they transported him from the mess
hall to the quiet solitude of the Healer's tent and laid him on a simple
cot.

For the next hour, they stood vigil, a council of the bewildered. They
spoke in low, hushed tones, debating a future they couldn't possibly
predict. What did they tell the King? How could they possibly train him?
How could they protect the world from him, and him from the world?

True to the Healer's word, almost exactly an hour later, Kael stirred.
He yawned, stretched his arms, and sat up, blinking in the dim light of
the tent. He looked at the three grave faces staring at him.

His own face broke into a small, sleepy smile. "Hello," he said.

It was too much for Elias. The wizard, who had been eerily calm since
his revelation, threw his hands into the air, his face a mask of pure,
intellectual outrage.

"How is that even FAIR!" he exclaimed, his voice cracking. He began to
pace the small tent like a caged animal. "Do you have any idea of the
thermodynamic cost of entropy reversal? To create a negative energy
state---to make something *cold*---is exponentially more difficult than
adding kinetic energy! It should have put him in a stupor for days! A
week! An hour's nap... AN HOUR'S NAP! It's an insult to the fundamental
laws of reality!"

Kael watched the wizard's frantic pacing, his head tilted in confusion.
He looked at the Sergeant, then the Healer, and then back to the
distraught mage. Finally, a thought seemed to occur to him, and he
addressed the most important issue at hand.

"Is there any of that bread left?"

That evening, very late in the evening, the three of them sat around a
rough wooden table in the Healer's tent. A single lantern cast long,
dancing shadows on the canvas walls. Kael was long asleep in the
barracks, a still point of innocence at the center of their storm. The
remnants of a simple meal---bread, cheese, and watered wine---lay
forgotten on the table. Each of them was lost in their own thoughts,
staring into the flickering flame.

Elias was the first to break the heavy silence. He had finally exhausted
his intellectual outrage and was left with a chilling, pragmatic void.

"So what do we DO with him?" he asked, his voice low. The question was
not academic. It was the most important question in the world.

Daniels, his arms crossed over his massive chest, grunted. His answer
was that of a soldier. "We train him. The King's Law is the King's Law.
We make him the greatest soldier this kingdom has ever seen. We point
him at the enemy, and we win the war."

The Healer recoiled as if struck. "He is not a sword to be aimed,
Sergeant! He is a boy! Did you see him today? He has the emotional
maturity of a child. To put him on a battlefield, to expose that... that
innocence to the horrors of war... it would be a sin beyond measure. It
would shatter him."

"Shatter him?" Elias scoffed, a bitter, hollow sound. "My dear woman,
you are not thinking on the correct scale. He would not shatter. The
*war* would shatter."

He leaned forward, the lantern light glinting in his intense eyes.
"Imagine that boy, that *mind*, faced with a charging battalion. He
wouldn't see a thousand men. He'd see a single, unified concept of
'attack'. And he would find the simplest, most elegant way to 'reverse'
it. A word, a thought, a gesture... and a thousand men would simply...
stop. Or fall down. Or forget why they were there. He wouldn't be a
soldier. He'd be a walking god of peace, or a god of absolute slaughter,
and he wouldn't know the difference between the two until it was over."

"So you agree with her," Daniels said, scowling. "We do nothing? We hide
him away? Let this... this force of nature go back to a farm and make
spider-silk bowstrings?"

"No!" Elias slammed his hand on the table, making the lantern jump.
"Doing nothing is just as dangerous! A power like that cannot be hidden.
It leaks. It attracts things. Things far older and darker than your
border skirmishes, Sergeant. Things that prey on anomalies. To leave him
untutored, unprotected... is to paint a target on this entire kingdom
for creatures you cannot imagine."

The three of them fell silent again, trapped in an impossible dilemma.
To train him for war was to risk creating a monster. To hide him was to
risk him being found by worse monsters. To do nothing was to abdicate
responsibility for the most powerful being any of them had ever known.

"There is no 'we'," the Healer said at last, her voice soft but firm.
"Not in the way we are thinking." She looked at Daniels. "You cannot
teach him to be a soldier. You can only teach him how to survive." She
turned to Elias. "And you cannot teach him magic. You can only...
explain to him what he is already doing."

She looked between the two powerful men, the masters of their respective
crafts, and delivered a simple, devastating truth.

"We cannot control him. We cannot aim him. All we can do is try to guide
him. And pray he chooses to be a good man."
