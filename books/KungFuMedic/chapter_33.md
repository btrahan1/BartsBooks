# Chapter 33: The Hub

It didn't take a master tracker to find him. Lin simply followed the sound. It was a low, rhythmic, and distinctly un-melodious humming, a sound utterly out of place in the disciplined afternoon hum of the Hub. She followed it out into the main courtyard, and her heart sank into her sandals.

There he was. Right in the MIDDLE of the courtyard. On the flagstones where the Sect's most honored masters usually held their formal addresses. And he had an audience.

Bruce was sitting cross-legged, his back ramrod straight, his eyes squeezed shut in a parody of intense concentration. His hands were resting on his knees, but instead of the traditional meditative mudra, his fingers were formed into the "rock on" devil horns gesture.

"Ahhhmmm... Ahhhmmmm..." he chanted, the sound a deep, resonant, and completely incorrect vibration.

Gathered in a semi-circle around him were at least a dozen children---Mei, the boy with the healed forehead, and all the others from the ward. A few off-duty junior warriors and curious kitchen staff lingered at the edges, watching the spectacle with expressions of pure bewilderment.

"Breathe in the good air," Bruce instructed himself, his voice a loud, instructional whisper that carried across the entire courtyard. He took a massive, noisy gulp of air. "Hold it... hold the power... Now, breathe out the bad air." He exhaled with a whoosh, like a bellows.

He then resumed his chanting. "Ahhhmmm... Ahhhmmmm..."

And the children, his loyal, adoring congregation, were mimicking him perfectly. They sat cross-legged, squeezed their eyes shut, and made little devil-horn gestures with their tiny hands.

"Ahhhmmm... Ahhhmmmm..." they chanted in their high-pitched voices, a chorus of ridiculousness.

"Breathe in the good air... breathe out the bad air..." they whispered in unison, taking big, dramatic breaths.

Lin stood at the edge of the courtyard, frozen in a state of pure, secondhand embarrassment. He had taken the sacred, ancient art of meditation, a practice of silent, internal contemplation, and turned it into a public, interactive self-help seminar. And he had a cult. A small, adorable, and very loud cult.

She covered her face with her hands, the groan escaping her lips before she could stop it.

"Oh, for Heaven's sake."

Lin was about to march over there and drag him away by his ear, but his next words stopped her cold.

"Now!" Bruce's voice boomed, silencing the chorus of tiny "Ahhmms." He opened his eyes, a look of profound, mystical wisdom on his face. "Look inside yourselves! It's all about the yin and the yang. The good and the bad. The awesome and the... not-so-awesome."

He made a slow, circular motion with his hands, a surprisingly graceful gesture.

"Find your center," he commanded, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Imagine the magic lines running all through your body. The power grid. Can you feel it? A little tingle?"

A few of the children nodded enthusiastically, their eyes still squeezed shut.

"Yes... that's it..." he continued, a genuine sense of discovery in his tone. "Now, look inside, deep, deep inside... just above your navel... See that little glowy spot? That's the power source. The main battery. The... uh... Dantian." He stumbled over the unfamiliar word, but recovered quickly. "I saw it in a book."

He closed his eyes again, his face a mask of supreme effort. "Focus on the battery! Feel its power! Now, CHANT!"

"Ahhhhmmmm..... Ahhmmmm..."

The sound started up again, louder this time, more focused. Bruce's ridiculous chant, the children's high-pitched mimicry, it all blended together in the courtyard.

And Lin just stood there, mesmerized.

He was a fool. An absolute, unequivocal, magnificent fool. But... he wasn't wrong. He was taking the most complex, esoteric concepts of their faith---the Dantian, the flow of Qi, the very nature of meditation---and translating them into a language a six-year-old could understand. "The power source." "The main battery." "The magic lines."

It was crude. It was sacrilegious. It was absurd.

And it was, to her utter astonishment, actually working. She could almost feel a palpable, if clumsy, energy beginning to coalesce in the center of the courtyard, generated by a dozen children and one very determined himbo. She stood there, rooted to the spot, completely captivated by the dumbest, most brilliant meditation lesson she had ever witnessed.
