# Chapter 44: The Magnificent

Back in the Chamber, Master Tenjin's face was a thundercloud of pure, unadulterated rage at the sheer, pedantic audacity of the man. The First Elder's lips were pressed into a thin, tight line, betraying nothing.

And Lin, unable to help herself, grinned from ear to ear. He was magnificent. He was a magnificent, infuriating, rule-bending genius.

A long, heavy silence followed Bruce's irrefutable legal defense. The mist swirled, the path stretched on, and the disembodied voice offered no rebuttal. Bruce stood his ground, arms crossed, a look of smug, triumphant certainty on his face. He had beaten the system. He had found the loophole.

In the Chamber, Master Tenjin looked like he was about to have an aneurysm. "Acceptable? First Elder, he is mocking the test! He is..."

"He is using logic, however flawed, to justify his actions," the First Elder said, a hint of something that might have been amusement in his voice. "He has defined the terms of his success. The Spirit is, if nothing else, creative."

Just as Bruce was about to declare victory and demand a cookie, the voice finally rumbled through the void again. The tone was unchanged---flat, impersonal, and utterly devoid of emotion.

"Acceptable. Proceed."

Bruce's triumphant grin widened. "See? Told ya," he said to the empty air. He then gave a jaunty, two-fingered salute to the mist, turned, and continued his walk down the Path of Sorrows, his step now filled with the confident swagger of a man who had just successfully argued with a god and won.

Satisfied with his legal and moral victory, Bruce felt a surge of energy. To celebrate, he immediately dropped to the flagstones and hammered out twenty lightning-fast pushups. He sprang back to his feet, dusting off his hands, and resumed his journey, a confident saunter in his step.

In the Chamber, Master Tenjin's eye twitched. "Why does he do that? The constant, unnecessary callisthenics?"

Lin, who had been watching with a mixture of pride and secondhand embarrassment, answered without thinking. "I have no idea," she said, her voice soft. She sighed. "It's... just what he does."

Bruce continued down the path. The grey mist swirled, and a new figure coalesced out of the haze, standing directly in his way. It was a wizened old man, his back bent with age, his face a roadmap of wrinkles. He leaned heavily on a gnarled wooden staff. As Bruce approached, the old man raised a trembling hand.

"Halt, traveler," the old man's voice was thin and reedy, but it held a strange authority. "I have a question for you. A test of your wisdom."

Bruce stopped, rocking back on his heels, his hands on his hips. "A riddle? Cool. I like riddles. Lay it on me, Gandalf."

The old man ignored the nickname. "You are in a boat, adrift on a vast and stormy sea," he began, his voice painting a grim picture. "With you are four other people: a sick, crying child; the child's desperate mother; a hardened thief, caught and bound; and a wise, holy priest. But the boat is small and taking on water. It cannot hold all five of you. To save the others, you must choose one person to cast into the sea. Who do you choose?"

The old man fixed him with a piercing, expectant gaze. "The child? The mother? The thief? Or the priest?"

Bruce listened to the entire scenario, his face shifting from mild interest to open disgust. He didn't ponder the options. He didn't weigh the moral implications. He just looked at the old man as if he'd just suggested kicking a puppy.

"That's a ridiculous question!" he blurted out, his voice ringing with pure, unadulterated offense. "That's not a test of wisdom, that's a test of 'who's the biggest jerk?' How can one person's life matter more than another's? I've seen the movie! The raft was big enough for the both of them! Jack totally could have fit if Rose had just scooted over a bit!"

The old man's wizened face remained impassive. "Nevertheless," he intoned, his voice like the rustling of dry leaves. "The rules of the scenario are absolute. The boat is sinking. You must choose."

Bruce threw his hands up in exasperation. "Fine! You want a choice? I choose myself! I jump out. I'm an excellent swimmer."

*(This was, in fact, highly debatable. He was a powerful swimmer for
about fifty feet, after which his terrible technique usually resulted in a spectacular cramp.)*

The old man shook his head slowly. "You may not choose yourself. The test demands you choose one of the other occupants."

That was the final straw. Bruce's face, which had been merely annoyed, hardened into a mask of cold fury.

"The hell I do!" he snarled, taking a half-step toward the old man, his voice a low, dangerous growl. "You think being a hero is about pointing a finger and deciding who gets to die? That's a villain's choice! You can sacrifice yourself, but you can't sacrifice someone else!"

He jabbed a finger at the old man's chest. "Unless it's in the army, or whatever, and you are in command of your men, you can't just sacrifice another human! You can only sacrifice yourself! The scenario is stupid!"

He took a deep, calming breath, his mind already having solved the "unsolvable" problem.

"The test is over," Bruce declared, his voice full of finality. "Because I already grabbed a rope from the boat, tied it around my waist, jumped off, and I am now dragging the whole damn boat to shore. Problem solved."

He glared at the illusionary old man, his moral verdict delivered.

"Now, be gone!"

The old man did not immediately vanish. There was a long, profound pause. He simply stood there, his ancient, watery eyes fixed on BruceLeeroy The Magnificent. The silence stretched, thick and heavy in the grey void. He stared, and then stared a bit longer, as if he, a mere construct of the test, was genuinely trying to process the sheer, bull-headed moral certainty of the man before him.

Then, just like the voice before, he delivered his verdict, his reedy voice flat and emotionless.

"That is... acceptable."

And with that, the wizened figure dissolved into a swirl of mist, leaving Bruce alone on the path once more.

Bruce snorted, shaking his head in disgust. "Pfft," he scoffed at the empty air. "Stupid riddle. Avoided the question with a trick answer. What's next, 'what have I got in my pocket'?" He kicked at an imaginary rock on the path and continued walking.
