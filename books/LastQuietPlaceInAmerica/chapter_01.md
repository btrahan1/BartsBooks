# Chapter 1: The man in the trunk of the Charger didn’t mind the cold. His name was Anton, and his list of earthly complaints had been permanently zeroed out three days ago by a fistful of bad coke and a weak aortic valve. I hadn’t killed him. I just… acquired him. Professional courtesy, in a way. Anton had specialized in moving scared girls across borders. He was garbage. My acquiring him was just taking out the trash on its expiration date.

Now, he was my stand-in. My ticket out.

He was a good fit. Roughly my height, same build, similar hairline before the mortician’s wax. I’d spent two days breaking his nose and jaw with a small mallet to obscure the facial features, a process I performed with the detached precision of a sculptor working on a piece he didn’t particularly like. The dental records would be a problem, but the fire would solve that. The fire solved most things.

I stood on the edge of the cliff overlooking the Pacific Coast Highway, a lonely stretch of asphalt called Devil’s Slide for good reason. The wind whipped at my jacket, cold and clean and smelling of salt. Below, the waves crashed against the rocks, a constant, churning applause for the inevitable. The plan was a checklist, and I was on the final items.

**[✓] Item 17: DNA Contamination.**
I ran a hand through my hair, pulling a few strands loose, and sprinkled them on the driver’s-side floor mat. I’d already bled a little into the seat cushion—a nick from a razor that morning, meticulously saved on a cotton ball. I’d coughed on the steering wheel. Spat on the dashboard. Michael Corrigan, the man they called ‘The Ghost,’ had to be thoroughly, biologically present for his own demise.

**[✓] Item 18: Personal Effects.**
I took out my wallet. The leather was worn smooth from a decade of sitting in my back pocket. Inside was a driver’s license with my face, three credit cards under my primary alias, and six hundred dollars in cash. I shoved it into the back pocket of Anton’s pants. I added my watch to his wrist—a heavy, practical Omega that had been my one real indulgence. It felt strange to see it on his pale, waxy skin. A tool on a mannequin.

*Why was I doing this?*

The question had been a low hum in my mind for months, a background process running behind the logistics of contracts and travel. The answer wasn’t a single event, but an accumulation. A slow erosion.

It started with the noise. Not the gunshots or the sirens. The other noise. The desperate pleading of a financier who’d embezzled from the wrong people. The wet, final gasp of a political fixer who knew too much. The arrogant sneer on a drug lord’s face, right up until the moment he realized his bodyguards weren’t coming.

For years, that noise was just part of the job. The sound of a problem being solved. But lately, I’d started to hear it when things were quiet. I’d be sitting in a sterile hotel room, cleaning a firearm, and I’d hear it. The ghosts weren’t haunting me; they were just… there. A Rolodex of final moments, spinning in the silence.

My code—"no women, no kids"—had kept me sane. It was the guardrail that separated my work from simple butchery. I was a scalpel, not a cleaver. I removed tumors from the world. That’s what I told myself, and for a long time, it was enough.

But the tumors were getting younger. Uglier. More chaotic. The targets used to be old men who’d lived their lives by a certain brutal code. Now they were kids with face tattoos and no impulse control, gangsters who’d film their own atrocities for social media clout. The work had lost its grim professionalism. It had just become… dirty.

The final straw had been a target in Miami. A pimp. Grade-A scumbag. I was on overwatch, a quarter-mile away, scope dialed in. He was walking to his car. An easy, clean shot. Then his daughter, maybe six years old, ran out of the house, crying because she’d dropped her ice cream. He stopped, knelt, and wiped her tears with his thumb. He smiled at her, a genuine, loving smile, before shooing her back inside.

I didn’t take the shot. I packed up, went home, and called in a mechanical failure. The contract went to someone else, someone who probably took the shot a day later and didn't care who was watching. But I cared. I saw a monster be a father, and the two images didn’t reconcile. The world was no longer black and white. It was a greasy, incomprehensible gray, and I was tired of living in it.

I was a ghost, and I wanted to be a person again. Even a boring one. Especially a boring one.

**[✓] Item 19: Staging.**
I dragged Anton’s body out of the trunk and levered him into the driver’s seat. His limbs were stiff. I had to break his arm to get it to bend around the steering wheel. The sound, a dull *crack*, was swallowed by the wind. I buckled the seatbelt around him. Even in a fake accident, one should observe the basics.

***

The final step. I opened the gas can and doused the backseat and Anton himself. The fumes were sharp, an acrid promise. I wedged a brick on the accelerator, put the Charger in neutral, and aimed its nose at the weakest point in the guardrail.

All that was left was to push.

I put my hands on the trunk. The metal was cold. This car, a 1970 Dodge Charger, was the only thing I’d ever really owned. I’d rebuilt the engine myself. Every wire, every piston. It was a part of me. A loud, angry, beautiful part of me. It was fitting that it would be my coffin.

I pushed.

The car rolled slowly at first, then picked up speed. It hit the guardrail with a screech of tortured metal, splintering the wooden posts and tearing through. For a breathtaking second, it hung in the air, a black silhouette against the churning gray water. Then gravity won.

It fell.

I didn’t wait to see the impact. I was already walking away, my back to the cliff, when I heard the explosion. A deep, resonant *whump* that echoed up the canyon. The fire had caught.

The work was done. Michael Corrigan was dead.

The man who walked away from the flames didn't have a name yet. That was the next item on the list. First, a bus ticket to a town so small it was barely a smudge on the map. A place where nobody had a past because they’d never left. A place where the only noise was the wind in the trees, and the only thing that ever needed fixing was a leaky faucet or a broken fence.
