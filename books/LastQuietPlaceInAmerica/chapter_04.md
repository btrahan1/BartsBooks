# Chapter 4: First Contact

***

Sleep didn’t come easily. The bed in the small room was a tactical blind spot, a coffin-shaped piece of furniture in a classic fatal funnel. Instead, I chose the living room floor. From there, I had a clear line of sight to the front door and the two main windows. My thin wool jacket was a poor substitute for a pillow, and the worn floorboards were colder and harder than any concrete ledge I’d ever used for an overwatch position. But it was defensible. The habits of a decade were a prison I had built for myself, and I didn't have the key.

Sleep, when it came, was a fractured country, a borderland between the man I was and the man I was trying to be.

At first, I was back in Bogota. The air was thick with humidity and the smell of diesel and fried plantains. I was on a rooftop, the cold steel of the rifle stock pressed against my cheek. The target, a money launderer for the cartels, was laughing on his balcony three hundred yards away, a glass of whiskey in his hand. The crosshairs were steady on his chest. My finger tightened on the trigger…

But the sound wasn't the muffled *crack* of the suppressor. It was the loud, cheerful ring of a bicycle bell. The face in the scope shifted, the features melting and reforming. It wasn't the money launderer anymore; it was one of the old men from the hardware store bench, and he was waving at me.

The scene dissolved into sunlight. I was in a backyard—*my* backyard. The sun was warm on my back. I was fixing a wooden fence, the simple, satisfying work of it calming my mind. The smell of freshly cut pine replaced the scent of cordite. A bird chirped in the oak tree. A woman—I couldn't see her face, just a silhouette in a doorway—called out, "Lunch is ready, Mike." The name sounded right. It sounded like it belonged to me. I drove another nail home with a solid, clean strike of the hammer…

And the hammer in my hand was suddenly heavy, the balance wrong. It was the butt of my Sig Sauer. The woman in the doorway was gone, replaced by a shadow with a gun. The bird's chirp became the high-pitched whine of a ricochet. My body tensed, dropping into a combat crouch, ready to—

I woke up.

Instantly. My eyes snapped open, my muscles taut. My heart was pounding a steady, professional rhythm—not panicked, but primed. Ready.

The room was dark and cold. The only sound was the furnace kicking on in the basement with a low rumble and a metallic clang. That was the noise. Not a gunshot. A furnace. In my house.

I stayed motionless for a full minute, my senses cataloging the reality of the situation. The faint orange glow from the streetlamp outside. The smell of dust and old wood. The steady, infuriating *plink… plink…* of the leaky faucet in the kitchen.

There was no threat. The war was over. I was just the only one who still had my armor on.

Slowly, I forced myself to relax. My shoulders slumped. A deep ache had settled into my back and hips from the hard floor. I pushed myself up into a sitting position, my joints protesting with a series of quiet pops and creaks. A decade of sleeping in worse places hadn't prepared me for the simple indignities of getting older on an uncarpeted floor.

I looked at the empty spot where the plaid sofa sat, then towards the dark doorway of the bedroom. A proper bed wasn't just a comfort. It was a commitment. A piece of furniture for a person who planned on waking up in the same place tomorrow.

Right. Add it to the list.
