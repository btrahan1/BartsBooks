# Chapter 6

That night, standing in the kitchen under the unforgiving glare of a new
60-watt bulb, I scraped the last remnants of the five-bean hotdish from
the corners of the Tupperware with my titanium spork. I'd made it last,
rationing it like a field medic with a dwindling supply of morphine.
When the last bite was gone, a profound and unexpected sense of loss
settled over me.

I could still feel the warmth of it in my stomach, a heavy, comforting
anchor. It was then that I felt a prickling sensation at the corner of
my eye. I reached up, my calloused fingertip coming away wet. A single
tear. It was an involuntary, baffling betrayal by my own body. I hadn't
cried when my handler was killed in Istanbul. I hadn't cried at any of
the funerals I'd watched from a distance. But a casserole had breached
my defenses.

"Damn," I whispered to the empty room. "That was good."

I washed the container and its lid meticulously, scrubbing it until it
shone, and set it on the counter to dry. It looked like a trophy.

The rest of the day was a blur of productive, grounding labor. The front
door was my first priority. The flimsy Kwikset lock was an insult to
security. I removed it, chiseled out a deeper housing, and installed the
double-cylinder deadbolt I'd bought. The heavy *thunk* of the bolt
sliding home was one of the most satisfying sounds I had ever heard. It
wasn't about keeping assassins out; it was about sealing a new life in.

Next, I addressed the walls. They were a roadmap of the house's past
life, dotted with the ghosts of picture frames and the dings of
carelessly moved furniture. I worked the spackle with the putty knife,
smoothing the chalky paste over each imperfection. The motion was
hypnotic: apply, scrape, smooth. I wasn't just fixing a wall; I was
erasing a history that wasn't mine, making the space clean. Making it
mine.

When dusk began to bleed through the windows, I surveyed my work. The
door was secure. The walls were smooth, waiting for a coat of paint. The
faucet was silent. The furnace breathed warm, clean air. It was the most
satisfying day's work I'd done in my entire life. My old job was about
creating holes in the world. This was about filling them in.

I slept on the floor again, but it felt different. It was the good kind
of tired, the ache in my muscles earned from building something up, not
tearing it down.

Tomorrow, the outside.

My new mental checklist formed as I lay in the dark, listening to the
unfamiliar sounds of a quiet neighborhood settling down for the night.

**Objective 1: Yard Work.** The lawn was a mess of dead leaves from last
fall, a tangled brown mat. A rake was required. It was an issue of
discipline and presentation. A neat yard was good camouflage.

**Objective 2: Procurement.** Mission: acquire one can of
hazelnut-flavored coffee. Secondary objective: identify the location of
the local grocery store.

**Objective 3: Reconnaissance and Diplomatic Engagement.** Conduct a
walkabout through the town. Primary purpose: return the cleaned
Tupperware containers and deliver the coffee to Eleanor Vance. Secondary
purpose: thank Brenda Gable. Tertiary, and most critical, purpose: get
the layout of the land.

I would map the town in my head. Note the alleys, the sightlines from
the church steeple, the traffic patterns on Main Street. I'd observe the
locals' routines---when the mail was delivered, when the school bus came
through, which shops opened first. A normal person would call it a
stroll. I called it establishing a baseline. You couldn't blend into an
environment until you understood its rhythms.

My last thought before sleep took me was of the mission. For the first
time, the objective wasn't to eliminate a target. It was to say thank
you. And I had a feeling it would be the more complicated operation.

Excellent. It's time for Mike to navigate the most dangerous battlefield
of all: the grocery store aisle.
