# Chapter 7

Ding!

The sound was followed by a gust of warm air that smelled faintly of
roasted chicken and floor wax. Damn, did every place in this town have a
cheerful bell? It was a security flaw, announcing your presence to
everyone within earshot.

I had found the Greenville Grocery & Sundries. It was exactly what you'd
expect: two long aisles, fluorescent lights that hummed with a low buzz,
and floors made of tired, cracked linoleum. A teenage boy was
methodically stocking cans of green beans, his movements slow and lazy.
A woman with a toddler in her cart was debating the merits of two
different brands of ketchup. This was my new intelligence-gathering hub.

First objective: the coffee. I started my patrol, moving down the first
aisle with a slow, deliberate pace. Cereal. Canned vegetables. Pasta. No
coffee. I rounded the endcap, which held a precarious pyramid of toilet
paper rolls, and started down the other side. Bread. Pickles. Chips.
Still no coffee.

It was tucked away on a small, three-shelf display between the breakfast
pastries and the dog food. And it was a nightmare of inefficiency. There
weren't one or two options. There were twenty. Dark Roast, Breakfast
Blend, French Vanilla, Colombian Supremo. And there, on the bottom
shelf, was a small section dedicated to hazelnut. There were four
different brands.

I stood there for a full minute, paralyzed by choice. Which one was the
"fancy" one Will had mentioned? Was it the one in the glossy black bag
or the one in the folksy-looking can? This was a variable I hadn't
accounted for. In my old life, you were given a weapon, and you used it.
You didn't get to choose the grain count of the ammunition.

I defaulted to the path of least resistance. One of the bags was simply
labeled "Hazelnut Crème." It sounded like something an old woman with
prize-winning petunias would drink. I took it. Objective complete.

Now, the second problem. What was I going to eat now that heaven, in the
form of a casserole, had left me high and dry?

My stomach was an empty, echoing cavern. I needed supplies. I walked the
aisles again, this time with a new mission: sustenance. I bypassed the
brightly colored boxes of processed junk food, the frozen dinners in
their sad plastic trays. I needed fuel, not filler. My basket quickly
filled with the basics: a carton of eggs, a package of thick-cut bacon,
a loaf of plain white bread, a block of sharp cheddar, potatoes, and a
thick steak from the small butcher counter in the back. It was simple,
efficient food. No complex recipes required.

As I was grabbing a gallon of milk, I saw it through the front windows
of the store, catty-corner across the street: "Greenville Diner." A
classic-looking place with a long counter and red vinyl booths visible
through the plate-glass window.

That was the answer. Cooking for one was inefficient. A diner was a
logistical solution. Predictable menu, consistent hours. A perfect place
to observe the town's social dynamics.

Ah, the Diner. I would stop there later today.

It wouldn't be lunch. It would be an operation. I'd need to do a proper
stakeout. Pick a booth in the back, one that gave me a clear view of the
entrance and the counter. Back to the wall, always. I'd observe the
staff, identify the regulars, learn the rhythm of the place. It was just
gathering data. Establishing a new baseline.

"Find everything you were lookin' for?" The cashier was a young woman,
maybe twenty, with a bright, friendly smile and a name tag that read
'SARAH'.

"Yes. Thank you," I said, placing my items on the conveyor belt. She
scanned them with a practiced ease, her chatter filling the space.

"First big grocery run? Settlin' in at the old Gable place, right? My
grandma says hi. She's Eleanor's best friend."

The information network was inescapable. It was faster than the internet
and more invasive than the NSA.

"Right," I said, handing her a fifty. "Tell her I said hello."

I walked out of the store, the bell dinging my departure, a plastic bag
in each hand. In one, my rations. In the other, a diplomatic tool
disguised as hazelnut coffee. I had a plan for the day, a series of
small, manageable missions.

First, drop the supplies at the house. Then, Operation Casserole Return.
After that, a long-term surveillance mission at the Greenville Diner. It
was a good plan. A solid plan. It was the only way I knew how to live.

As Mike is about to find out, the simplest objectives can have the most
complex variables. Here we go.
