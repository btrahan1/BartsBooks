# Chapter 15

My attempt to pivot back to the chili mission was a tactical failure. I
could see it in Kyle's eyes. He had witnessed the crack in my armor, and
now, with the gentle precision of a bomb disposal expert, he was going
to place a charge right on that fracture point.

"Right. The chili," he said again, but his focus was elsewhere. He set
Eleanor's sacred recipe card down on the counter with care. "But first
things first. We need to do some shopping tomorrow, so we need to make a
list today."

I nodded. "Okay. A list. Good. Let's do it." I was ready to fall back on
the comforting structure of logistics. A list was a plan. A plan was
control.

Kyle looked at me, then glanced over at Buddy, who was still watching my
every move from his kennel, a silent, furry sentinel.

"All right, we need to do some shopping," Kyle repeated, but his voice
had a new note in it. Not the nervous mumble from the diner, but a
quiet, clear authority. This barn was his domain, and here, he was in
charge.

He walked over to a hook on the wall and took down a well-worn red
leash. He held it out, not to me, but just holding it in the space
between us. It was an offer. A test.

"Why don't we take Buddy for a walk?" he said, his tone casual, but his
eyes were sharp, observant. "He doesn't get out much. We can talk about
the chili strategy while we walk."

It was a brilliant maneuver. He was leveraging my one moment of weakness
against me, wrapping it in the logic of the mission. It was an offer I
couldn't refuse without revealing the true nature of the conflict raging
inside me. To say no would be to admit that the dog---that a
*feeling*---had more power over me than a simple walk.

I looked at the leash. It was a simple object of leather and metal, but
it felt like a key, a contract, a chain. My entire life had been about
avoiding attachments. Attachments were liabilities. They were pressure
points the enemy could use against you. And this dog, this scruffy,
hopeful mutt, was the biggest liability I had ever faced.

My training, my instincts, every survival mechanism honed over a decade
of violence and solitude screamed at me to say no. *Walk away. Abort.
Maintain operational detachment.*

But the man who wanted to fix a leaky faucet, who had felt a tear roll
down his cheek over a casserole, who wanted to believe in a quiet
life---that man said something else.

Slowly, I reached out and took the leash from Kyle's hand. The worn
leather was warm and smooth.

"Okay," I said, the word costing me more than I could ever explain.
"Let's take him for a walk."

Kyle's smile was small, but it was triumphant. He went to the kennel and
unlatched the door. Buddy didn't bolt out. He walked out slowly, with a
quiet dignity, and trotted right over to me, sitting patiently at my
feet as I fumbled with the clasp on his collar. He looked up at me, his
gaze unwavering, as if to say, *See? I knew you would.*

I clicked the leash into place. The sound was as final as a deadbolt
sliding home.

We walked out of the shelter, the three of us, into the crisp Wisconsin
air. Buddy trotted alongside me, his gait happy and light, his nose
twitching as he took in the thousand smells of the outside world. He
never pulled on the leash. He just stayed close.

We walked down the quiet country road, the red barn shrinking behind us.
Kyle started talking about chili---about the merits of pinto versus
black beans, about the optimal simmering time---and I made the right
noises, pretending to listen.

But my mind wasn't on the chili. My mind was focused entirely on the
gentle, rhythmic tug on the leather leash in my hand, a constant, living
connection to the scruffy, unassuming creature at my side.

I was Michael Corrigan, The Ghost, a man who had walked away from a life
of death and shadows. And now I was Mike Sullivan, a man walking a dog
named Buddy down a sunlit road in the middle of nowhere. I had no idea
which one was the more dangerous job.

Hah, this is perfect. The final nail in the coffin of his anonymity.
Even the children are part of the hive mind.
