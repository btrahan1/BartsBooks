# Chapter 19: The new normal had a rhythm. It started with the gentle *thump-thump-thump* of a tail against the sofa, followed by a walk through quiet streets where my name was spoken as often as my dog’s. It ended here, in my booth at the Greenville Diner, back to the wall, a mug of black coffee steaming in my hands. It was a good rhythm. It was a quiet life.

Buddy was lying at my feet, occasionally letting out a contented sigh that ruffled the napkin I’d dropped. A few farmers were talking about crop prices at the counter. Will was arguing with John about the Green Bay Packers' offensive line. It was Tuesday morning in Greenville.

*Ding!*

The cheerful bell still sent a phantom itch between my shoulder blades, a ghost of a warning from a past life. I didn’t flinch anymore. Not on the outside.

"Coffee, black," came the demand.

A silence fell over the diner. Julian Croft slid into a booth near the front, not bothering to take off his expensive jacket. He took out his ever-present leather-bound notebook and a silver pen. He surveyed the room of silent patrons, a smirk playing on his lips, and began to take notes. He wasn't observing; he was collecting specimens.

I let out a long, slow sigh. *The rhythm was broken. This was a flaw in the system. A piece of grit in the gears that was slowly grinding the whole machine to a halt.* And if there was one thing I knew how to do, *it was fix a broken machine*.

I stood up. The scraping of my chair was the only sound in the room. I walked over to Peggy, who was standing frozen behind the counter, a look of thunder on her face.

"Peggy," I said, my voice low and calm. "Can you watch Buddy for a little while?" She just nodded, her eyes wide. I turned. "Mr. Croft. You and I are going to take a walk."

It wasn't a request. Croft looked up from his notebook, his arrogance faltering for a split second in the face of my unnerving calm.

"I'm sorry, I don't believe we've—"

"You have now," I interrupted. "If you would be so kind as to grab your notebook."

He stared for a moment, his mind trying to process the situation. He was the one in charge, the sophisticated observer. But he wasn't. I was. He hastily stood and grabbed his notebook.

"Now, Mr. Croft, start a fresh page," I said, my voice even. I gestured toward the counter. "We'll start with this diner. That there is Peggy. The finest waitress in Greenville. You'll never find one friendlier. This diner serves a breakfast special that you've never tried. You won't find it at a fancy restaurant, but it will fill you up and keep you warm on crisp, cold mornings. Like today." I looked at Croft, then pointed a single finger at his notepad. "You might want to write that down."

He blinked, then, almost involuntarily, he started to write.

I walked out of the diner. After a second's hesitation, Croft followed, the bell dinging his departure. Behind us, the diner crowd was completely, utterly stunned.

We walked down Main Street. I didn't set a fast pace. It was a tour, not a march. People stopped what they were doing, watching our strange procession. I pointed to Henderson's Hardware. "That's Will's place. He can tell you the history of every screw and nail in there. His son, Robert, can fix anything with an engine. They'll extend you credit even if they know you can't pay it back right away."

Croft was furiously taking notes, his smirk long gone, replaced by a look of intense, confused concentration.

We stopped in front of the animal shelter. The distant, happy sound of barking filled the air.

"Do you know what a no-kill shelter is, Mr. Croft?" I asked.

He nodded quickly, a nervous tic in his jaw.

"This is where I got Buddy. You know, the 'livestock'?"

Croft gulped, his face paling slightly.

"A man named Kyle works here. He took it over from his Mom after she passed. He pours every cent he has into this place. No animal has ever died in here, Mr. Croft. Not a single one." I waited a beat. "Not from neglect, anyway."

He just stared at the red barn, its cheerful paint a stark contrast to the cold dread that was dawning on his face. I nodded toward his notepad. He started writing again, his hand shaking slightly.

We wound up, in the end, on the manicured lawn in front of the Inn where he was staying. His shiny black car was parked in the spot reserved for guests, angled slightly over the line.

"And this is the town Inn," I said. "Where you were staying."

His head snapped up. "Were?"

"Yes, were," I said, my voice still quiet. "Go check out. I'll wait here by your car."

There was no room for argument in my tone. It was a simple statement of fact. He looked at me, at my calm, unreadable expression, and he understood. He turned and walked quickly into the Inn.

Fifteen minutes later, a flustered Julian Croft came down the steps, dragging two expensive-looking suitcases. He loaded everything into his car without a word. I hadn't moved.

"Mr. Croft," I said as he closed the trunk. "This is a comfortable little town. There is not a single 'simpleton' here. Just good, honest folks who will give you the shirt off their back if you need it." Again, I nodded to the notepad resting on his passenger seat. "When your book comes out, I hope you have the decency to give it a better title." I looked him in the eye. "Have a nice trip back home."

I then nodded towards the open road. The final, unspoken dismissal.

Croft got in his car, started the engine, and backed out. As he drove slowly down Main Street, something amazing happened. People started coming out of the shops. Will, Peggy, Brenda, the farmers, the mailman. They lined the street, and as he passed, they waved at him with big, friendly, unforgiving smiles.

He turned the corner and was gone.

The town was silent for a long moment. Then, they turned their attention to me. They gathered around, a slow, silent circle of familiar faces.

Then, one person started clapping. It was Will. Then Peggy joined in. Then Kyle, who must have heard the commotion from the shelter. Within seconds, the entire town was applauding. It wasn't a wild cheer. It was a steady, rhythmic, powerful sound of gratitude and acceptance. It went on and on, a wave of sound that washed over me.

I just stood there, my hands in my pockets, a ghost who had accidentally become a guardian. I was their champion. It was the last thing on earth I had ever wanted to be.
