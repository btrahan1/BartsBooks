# Chapter 5: Hell to Pay

As he moved, a cold, perfect clarity settled over Steven. The world
became a simple, binary equation. There was his family, and there was
everything else. He had spent his entire life building a wall around
them, a wall made of his own muscle, sweat, and endless, grinding labor.
He had willingly made himself the foundation, the beast of burden, the
silent guardian. In return, he asked for nothing and expected less. He
had only one rule.

Don\'t mess with his family.

Mock him. He had been mocked his entire life for his size, for his quiet
nature, for the worn state of his clothes. It was meaningless noise,
like the buzzing of flies. Spit on him. It would wash off. Call him
names. Words were just air; they had no substance against the solid
reality of his obligations. None of that mattered. He had endured it all
with a placidity that many mistook for stupidity. *It was, in fact, an
iron discipline, a conscious choice to ignore the inconsequential.*

But this was not inconsequential. Somebody had crossed a line that they
had not even known existed. Somebody had mistaken the gentle giant for a
harmless one.

Somebody had made a mistake. And that somebody had hell to pay.

His boots made no sound on the cobblestones, his footsteps swallowed by
the stunned silence of the crowd. Behind him, he could hear Commander
Vance shouting his name, an order to halt, but it was a distant,
irrelevant sound from a world he had already left behind. His universe
was now the fifty feet between himself and the tavern door.

He filled the doorway, his massive frame eclipsing the noon sun and
casting the entrance into sudden, deep shadow. The raucous laughter he
had heard moments before was gone, replaced by a tense, waiting quiet.
Every eye in the tavern was fixed on the terrifying silhouette that had
just appeared.

His eyes adjusted instantly, scanning the dim, smoky room. He saw it all
in a single, sweeping glance. A table near the hearth. Three young men,
dressed in the fine clothes of merchants' sons. One of them, a
swaggering youth with a cruel smirk and reddish hair, was wiping his
hand on his trousers. At their feet was a shattered tankard and a puddle
of ale. And standing beside the table, her body trembling, was Sarah.
Her hand was pressed to her cheek, where a raw, red mark was already
beginning to bloom against her pale skin. Tears streamed silently down
her face.

The red-haired youth, emboldened by ale and the sycophantic grins of his
friends, was the first to speak. \"Well, look what the cow dragged in.
Come to fetch your sister, clodhopper? She was clumsy.\"

Steven did not look at him. He did not acknowledge his existence. His
gaze was fixed entirely on Sarah. He moved past the table, his presence
a cold wave of dread that made the other two boys shrink back in their
chairs. He stopped in front of his sister, his sheer size shielding her
from the rest of the room.

His voice, when he spoke, was impossibly gentle, a stark contrast to the
murderous energy pouring off him. \"Sarah. Look at me.\"

She lowered her hand and looked up, her blue eyes swimming with tears
and fear.

\"Did he do this to you?\" he asked, his voice still quiet, still calm.

She could only manage a single, hiccuping nod.

That was all he needed. He gave her shoulder a soft, reassuring squeeze.
\"Go outside,\" he whispered. \"Go find Mother in the crowd.\"

She hesitated for only a second before scurrying away, vanishing through
the doorway into the bright, safe light of the square.

Only then did Steven turn.

He turned slowly, the motion deliberate and final, to face the
red-haired boy, who was now looking slightly less confident. The smirk
was faltering as he finally seemed to comprehend the sheer,
unadulterated menace standing before him.

\"You should not have done that,\" Steven said. His voice was no longer
gentle. It was as flat and cold as a winter grave.

And in the dead silence of the tavern, Steven raised his hand.
