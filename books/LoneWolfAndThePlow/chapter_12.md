# Chapter 12: The Work That Remains

The quiet of their small farmhouse that evening was a taut, fragile
thing, easily broken. Sarah was a nervous wreck, pacing the worn wooden
floor of the main room while their mother sat silently by the hearth,
her knitting needles frozen in her lap.

When Steven came in from washing at the well, Sarah rushed to him, her
eyes wide with a fear that pained him more than any sword thrust could.

\"Why, Steven? Why did you do that?\" Her words tumbled out, a frantic
cascade of fear and regret. \"He was just a foolish boy, full of ale! It
was nothing! You could have been killed! You challenged the Commander
himself!\"

Steven simply listened, his stillness a rock in the river of her
anxiety. *To her, the incident was a terrifying anomaly, a hornet\'s
nest he had kicked for no good reason. But to Steven, family was
everything. It was the beginning and the end of his law. The foundation
upon which his entire world was built. An attack on that foundation, no
matter how small, was an existential threat that had to be met.*

He reached across the small wooden table and placed his large, calloused
hand over hers. It covered her hand completely, a warm, solid weight. He
gave her the smile that had always been her anchor, a smile that was
calm, reassuring, and absolute.

\"It was not nothing, Sarah,\" he said, his voice soft but firm. \"It
was everything.\"

He let the simple truth of that statement settle in the room before
turning to his mother, who gave him a single, worried nod of
understanding. She knew him better than anyone. She knew the unshakeable
core of him.

With nothing more to be said, he turned and went back outside. The sun
had long since set, leaving the sky a bruised purple that bled into a
deep, star-dusted indigo. He went not to his bed, but back to his
fields.

Low light was of no worry to him. His feet knew the furrows, his hands
knew the feel of a stubborn stone buried in the dark earth. There was
still work to be done. He had lost time in town, and the seasons did not
wait for the squabbles of men. The rhythmic, demanding labor centered
him, pulling the last vestiges of adrenaline from his system and
replacing it with a familiar, grounding ache.

The cool night air washed over him as he worked, clearing his head. *He
was not angry anymore. He was simply... ready. He had made a statement
in the square, and in the morning, the powers he had challenged would
come to his home for an answer. He had invited them, and he would be
prepared. The work he did now, under the silent watch of the stars, was
not just for the harvest. It was for them. He was a farmer, on his own
land, and tomorrow his guests would learn what it meant to stand on his
ground.*
