# Chapter 3: Old Ghosts

San Antonio was too loud.

Even after just an hour, the city felt like it was screaming at him. The River Walk, the tourists, the traffic on I-35—it was a sensory assault. Pedro needed space. He needed to hear his own thoughts without them being drowned out by sirens and reggaeton blasting from passing trucks.

He spent four dollars of his stash on a local bus ticket and rode it north, out past the Loop, where the concrete sprawl began to fray into the Texas Hill Country.

Here, the air was easier to breathe. The businesses were more spread out: lumber yards, taco trucks parked in gravel lots, and metal sheds baking in the sun. This was working-man's land.

He walked along the access road, ignoring the sweat trickling down his back. He had a destination in mind. Not a specific place, but a *kind* of place.

He saw it about a mile past the last strip mall. *Sal's Auto & Tire*.

It wasn't pretty. A cinderblock building painted a peeling white, surrounded by a chain-link fence that leaned tiredly to the left. A stack of used tires formed a rubber pyramid near the entrance.

Pedro stopped. The smell hit him first. Oil. Acetone. Old rubber.

It was the smell of his childhood. It was the smell of his father's hands. And, for the last five years, it had been the smell of his biggest mistake.

*Things I don't want: To touch a steering wheel.*

He hesitated at the gate. Working here meant being around the temptation. It meant being around the very thing that locked him up. But it was also the only thing he knew how to do well. He couldn't code computers. He couldn't wait tables. He could make an engine sing.

"Just wrenching," he muttered to himself. "Under the hood. No test drives."

He adjusted his bag and walked into the open bay door.

A man was under a Ford F-150 on the lift, wiping his hands on a rag that was blacker than the oil pan. He was older, thick around the middle, with gray stubble and eyes that looked like they'd seen every scam in the book.

"Help you?" the man grunted, not stepping away from the truck.

"I'm looking for work," Pedro said. He didn't smile. He didn't try to charm. "I can fix that timing belt you're looking at, and I can swap a transmission in four hours flat."

The man paused. He looked Pedro up and down—the prison haircut, the cheap clothes, the plastic bag.

"You got papers?"

"I got a release form," Pedro said steadily. "Got out this morning."

The man laughed, a short, sharp bark. "Honest, at least. What were you in for?"

Pedro felt his heart hammer against his ribs. This was it. The moment of truth.

"Grand Theft Auto."

The shop went silent. The only sound was the compressor humming in the corner. The man stared at him, rag frozen in mid-wipe.

"And you want to work in a car shop," the man said flatly.

"I want to fix them," Pedro said. "I don't want to drive them. I don't even want the keys. You park 'em, I fix 'em. I just need a paycheck, boss. I need a steak, and I need a room. I'll sweep the floors if I have to, but I'm better with a wrench."

The man studied him for a long, agonizing minute. Then he tossed the greasy rag onto the workbench.

"Name's Sal," he said. "The broom's in the corner. You sweep the bays today. We'll see about a wrench tomorrow."

Pedro felt forty pounds lighter. He nodded, dropping his bag by the wall.

"Thank you, Sal."
