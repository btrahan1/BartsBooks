# Chapter 12: Gravity

They didn't just drive back to the shop. They led a parade.

By the time the convoy parked, half the town was there. It wasn't just a car meet anymore; it was a block party. Someone had brought a grill. Someone else had brought a cooler of sodas. The music was loud, but it wasn't aggressive—it was celebratory.

The three trophies sat on the counter in Sal's office, gleaming under the fluorescent lights, but nobody was looking at them. They were looking at the cars.

Pedro stood near the office door, sipping a root beer. Next to him stood Sal. On his other side was Franklin Summers, who had loosened his tie but kept his jacket on.

And next to Franklin stood Sheriff Miller.

The Sheriff wasn't there to make arrests. He was leaning against the wall, eating a rib, watching the chaos with a bemused expression.

"You're blocking traffic, Sal," the Sheriff said mildy.

"It's Saturday night, Miller," Sal grunted. "Nobody's going anywhere important."

Suddenly, the vibe shifted.

Two cars roared into the edge of the lot. A Mustang and a Camaro, both loud, both ragged, both revving their engines aggressively. They were outsiders. Northside plates.

The doors opened and six guys piled out. They were posturing, looking for a race, looking for trouble. They saw the trophies. They saw the clean shirts of The Fleet. They saw an invitation to knock someone down.

Sal stiffened. The Sheriff wiped his hands, ready to step in. Pedro started to move forward.

But then, he stopped.

Leo was already there.

Leo walked out from the crowd. He didn't have a weapon. He didn't have his fists up. He walked with his hands in his pockets, his "Fleet" shirt pressed and clean.

He stood between the crowd and the newcomers.

"Yo!" the Mustang driver shouted. "We heard you southside boys think you're fast. Who wants to run for pinks?"

The crowd went silent.

Leo looked at the Mustang. He looked at the driver. He didn't sneer. He just looked... disappointed.

"We don't run for pinks," Leo said calmly. "We build."

"You scared?" the Camaro driver laughed.

"No," Leo said. He gestured to the Charger, then to the Impala, then to the families eating BBQ behind him. "We just have too much to lose to waste time with children."

The insult hung in the air. But before the Northside guys could react, Leo stepped closer.

"If you want to race," Leo said, his voice dropping to that low, dangerous register he had learned from Pedro, "take it to the track on Friday. If you want to learn how to build a car that doesn't sound like a lawnmower, come back on Tuesday. We're open."

He held the driver's gaze.

"But tonight? Tonight is for family. So cut your engines or cut your losses."

The Mustang driver looked at Leo. Then he looked at Rico, who had stepped up silently behind Leo. Then he looked at Tiny. Then he looked at the wall of grey shirts.

It wasn't a fight. It was a dismissal.

The Northside guys muttered something, got back in their cars, and reversed out. They peeled away, tires screeching.

Leo watched them go, then turned back to the party. "Sorry about the noise, folks!"

The crowd cheered.

Back at the office, the four men watched it all.

The Sheriff let out a low whistle. "I'll be damned. Leo? The kid who I arrested three times last year for fighting?"

"He's got gravity now," Pedro said softly.

Franklin Summers smiled. "It appears the investment is yielding returns."

Sal looked at Pedro. He looked at the party. He looked at the Sheriff.

"You're out of a job, Miller," Sal said. "We got this."

The Sheriff laughed. Franklin chuckled.

Pedro just grinned, watching Leo shake hands with a neighbor. He wasn't the leader anymore. He was just a member of The Fleet.

And that meant he was free.
