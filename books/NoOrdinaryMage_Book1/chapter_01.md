# Chapter 1: The Birthday System

The morning sun of Tobin’s twelfth birthday didn’t rise so much as it bruised the horizon, a dull, aching orange that bled through the gaps in his wooden shutters. But to Tobin, it was the most beautiful sight he’d ever seen. It was the color of destiny.

He didn’t climb out of bed. He catapulted.

In the center of his vision, shimmering like sun-dappled water against the familiar clutter of his small room, was the Interface. It had arrived at the stroke of dawn, a silent, translucent gift that every child in the kingdom of Oakhaven awaited with a mixture of terror and hope. To most, it was a ledger of their limitations—a formal declaration that they would spend their lives behind a plow or a shop counter. To Tobin, it was a ticket out.

"Stats," he whispered, his voice cracking with the first hint of puberty.

The shimmering veil hardened into text, floating in the air with a faint hum that only he could hear.

**[Status: Tobin]**
**[Age: 12]**
**[Level: 0]**

**[Attributes]**
**Strength:** 10
**Constitution:** 10
**Agility:** 10
**Wisdom:** 10
**Charisma:** 10
**Intelligence:** 12

"YES!"

Tobin pumped a fist into the air, the motion nearly knocking over a stack of his favorite books on ley-line theory and the secondary properties of mana-infused ink. Ten was the baseline—the "Average Commoner" mark. It was the score of a man who could haul a sack of grain or count his copper pieces without too much trouble. But that twelve—that glorious, shining twelve in Intelligence—was the golden key.

In Oakhaven, twelve was the threshold. It was the magical number that scholars spoke of in hushed, reverent tones. Twelve meant the mana-channels in the brain were wide enough to handle the flow of raw arcane energy without burning out the neural pathways. Twelve meant he wasn't just another boy with a quick wit; he was a potential Mage.

He could already see it: the silver-threaded robes of a Second-Circle Initiate, the staff of white oak capped with a pulsing sapphire, and the way the guards at the village gate would dip their spear-tips in respect when he walked past. He wouldn't be hunched over a desk for the rest of his life, smelling of old parchment and the bitter, metallic scent of gall ink. He was going to move the world, not just record its minor transactions.

"Come on down, Tobin! Breakfast is ready!"

His father’s voice rumbled up through the floorboards, carrying the familiar scent of toasted oats and the sharp, medicinal tang of the special birthday tea—a brew meant to "clarify the mind" for the System's arrival. It was a tradition in Oakhaven for parents to spend weeks of wages on that tea, hoping for a single-point boost that almost never came.

Tobin scrambled into his breeches and a tunic that was starting to feel a little tight across the shoulders. He paused at the small, polished bronze mirror hanging by his door. He looked back at himself: messy brown hair, a face that still had a bit of childhood roundness, and eyes that were perhaps a little too wide with excitement. Physically, he was nothing special—he wasn't the tallest boy in the village, nor the strongest. But as he looked at that floating '12', he felt like a giant among boys.

"Coming, Dad!" he yelled, practically sliding down the narrow stairs, his boots thumping rhythmically against the worn oak steps.

The ground floor of their house was also the shop. Tobin’s father, Alaric, was the village’s primary scrivener, a man of quiet patience and ink-stained legacy. The shop was a space dominated by the tools of preservation: floor-to-ceiling shelves packed with scrolls, jars of variously colored inks—from the cheap lampblack used for tax lists to the expensive vermillion reserved for temple records—and two heavy, slanted desks that had seen decades of labor.

Alaric was already at the table, his fingers permanently stained with a faint violet hue from the clerical ink he’d been using for the late-night property deeds. He looked up, a warm smile crinkling the corners of his eyes.

"Happy birthday, son," Alaric said, sliding a bowl of steaming porridge toward Tobin's seat. "Did the System greet you well?"

Tobin practically vibrated in his chair, his spoon clattering against the ceramic bowl. "It did, Dad. Intelligence Twelve. Exactly twelve! I’m... I’m going to be a Mage."

The smile on Alaric’s face faltered, just for a fraction of a second, before returning with a slightly more forced brightness. He knew that look. He’d seen it in the eyes of every boy who ever dreamed of leaving the ink-stained life behind for the glimmering towers of the capital.

"Twelve, eh? That's... that's a fine number, Tobin. A very fine number." Alaric reached across the table, his rough, ink-stained hand covering Tobin’s smaller one. "But remember what we discussed. A Mage’s life isn't just about what's in the head. It's a heavy burden to carry, and the world doesn't always make way for those with high numbers."

"I can handle it," Tobin insisted, his mind already miles away, imagining the complex sigils he’d soon be tracing in the air. "Once I get to the Academy, I'll learn the shielding charms. I won't even need to be strong if I can just stop the world with a word. I’ve been reading the journals of Magus Kaelen—he said that physical strength is just a crude substitute for a truly focused mind."

Alaric sighed, the sound lost in the steam from his tea. "Magus Kaelen lived in a tower guarded by three hundred soldiers, son. Eat your breakfast. We have a lot of work today. The Lord’s estate sent over three crates of old land deeds—parchment so brittle it flakes if you sneeze near it. They need to be transcribed before the festival tonight."

Tobin groaned, his excitement hitting the cold wall of daily labor. The mundane reality of the scrivener’s shop felt like a cage today. He was a creature of the stars, trapped in a room of dead trees and dusty shadows.

The morning passed in a slow, rhythmic scratching of quills. Tobin worked at his own small desk near the window, his fingers aching from the repetitive motions required to replicate the ornate, looping script of the previous century. The land deeds were tedious—endless lists of boundaries, stone markers, and generations of families who had long since turned to dust. 

"Boundary markers at the hollow oak," Tobin muttered to himself, his quill dipping into the inkwell with mechanical precision. "Three paces west to the stream. Property of Miller’s son, Third House."

His mind, however, was a storm of arcane theory. He kept visualizing the '12' in his status window, treating it like a lighthouse in the fog of his chores. He thought about the Academy entrance exams—the logic puzzles, the mana-sensing trials, the history and geography of the Empire. He was sure he could ace them all. He was smarter than the other kids. He saw patterns they didn't. He understood the *why* of things.

"Dad," Tobin said, pausing to stretch his cramped hand. "Do you think Master Gregory will see my stats at the festival tonight?"

Alaric didn't look up from his own work, his quill continuing its steady *skritch-skritch*. "Master Gregory sees everything he needs to see, Tobin. But he’s not looking for numbers. He’s looking for people who can finish a job."

"But a twelve!" Tobin pressed. "That’s rare. Even in the capital, they say only one in fifty reaches twelve."

"And of those one in fifty," Alaric said, finally setting his quill down and looking at his son, "how many do you think actually finish their first year of training? Magic isn't a gift, Tobin. It's a discipline. It’s like this ink—if you don't keep it contained, it just makes a mess. If you don't have the right vessel, the ink is useless."

Tobin went back to his work, but his father’s words felt like a splash of cold water. He didn't want to hear about vessels or containers. He wanted to hear about power. He wanted to hear that he was special. 

About midday, the village bells rang for the noon break. Usually, this was the highlight of Tobin's day—thirty minutes to run to the village square, talk to the other kids, and maybe see if any travelers had arrived at the inn with stories of the outside world. But today, the shop was his focus. He wanted to get through the deeds so he could spend the afternoon practicing his focus exercises.

He poured himself another cup of the "mind-clarifying" tea, which tasted like boiled grass and copper. He stared at the steam, trying to see if he could make it swirl in a specific pattern using only his will. He imagined the mana flowing from his core, through his arms, and out his fingertips. 

*Swirl,* he commanded in his mind. *Rotate.*

The steam continued to rise in a chaotic, natural plume, indifferent to his mental commands.

"It takes time," he whispered to himself. "I just need the right focus. Once the Academy unlocks my mana-channels, I'll be able to do it effortlessly."

By mid-afternoon, the shop was quiet, filled only with the smell of old parchment and the low, afternoon light filtering through the dusty windowpanes. Tobin had finished the first crate of deeds. His back ached, and his eyes felt gritty from staring at the fine script, but he felt a sense of accomplishment. He was one step closer to his goal.

He caught his reflection in a jar of dark obsidian ink. He looked... average. A boy in a dusty shop. But he knew the verdad. He knew the numbers hidden behind his eyes. He was a mage in waiting. He was Tobin of Oakhaven, potentially the greatest mind the village had produced in a generation.

As he reached for the next scroll, a new sound cut through the silence of the shop. It wasn't the jangle of the door bell—not yet. It was a rhythmic, heavy thumping from the street outside. The sound of boots that hit the ground with purpose.

Tobin looked at the door, his heart skipping a beat. Was this it? Was the world finally coming to find him?

He straightened his tunic, wiped his ink-stained fingers on his breeches, and waited. He didn't know yet that the world wasn't coming to find him with a scroll and a robe. It was coming with a fist.
