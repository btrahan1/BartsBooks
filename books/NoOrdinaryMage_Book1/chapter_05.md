# Chapter 5: The Day of Testing

The village square of Oakhaven, usually a space for quiet bartering and the slow creak of ox-carts, had been transformed into a riot of color and sound. Banners of deep indigo and sun-bright yellow—the colors of the Crown—fluttered from the eaves of the surrounding buildings, snapping like whips in the brisk autumn breeze. The air was thick with the scent of roasted meat, spiced cider, and the faint, ozone-heavy tang that always seemed to accompany the presence of the Testing Stone.

The Festival of the System was a yearly tether for Oakhaven, a moment where the mundane reality of village life touched the grand, crystalline order of the Empire. It was a day of celebration for everyone—a holiday from the fields, the forges, and for Tobin, the endless mounds of brittle land deeds. But for those like Tobin, who had touched their twelfth year, it was something else entirely. It was the Day of Testing. It was the moment the potential whispered by their Status windows became the cold, hard steel of a permanent reality.

Tobin stood amongst the other children, a group of twenty-four boys and girls huddled near the northern edge of the square. They were a patch of nervous energy in the midst of the merriment. Some were deathly pale, their eyes fixed on the shrouded pedestal in the center of the square. Others were vibrating with a frantic, desperate bravado, boasting about the strength they felt in their limbs or the wisdom they’d supposedly gained over the summer.

Tobin, for his part, was deathly quiet. He felt the weight of his father’s eyes on him from the crowd—Alaric was standing near the front of the scrivener's guild delegation, his violet-stained hands clasped tightly in front of him. Tobin didn't look at him. He didn't look at the other children. He looked only at the pedestal, and in his mind, he prayed.

*Mage. Please, System, let me be a Mage. Let that twelve mean something.*

He was painfully aware of the "Adventurer" classes. They were the stories he’d grown up on—the Warriors who stood like mountains, the Rogues who danced through shadows, the Healers who mended the world. And of course, the Mages. To Tobin, a Mage wasn't just a class; it was the only way to be more than a footnote in someone else's ledger.

The alternative was a word that felt like a death sentence: Commoner.

In the reality of the Empire, the "Adventurer" classes were rare. They were the military, the elite, the chosen few destined to face the monsters of the Wilds or the intrigues of the capital. The vast majority of the children in the square today would walk away with the Commoner designation. It was a broad, utilitarian category that simply meant you were a functional part of the kingdom’s machinery. It meant you would be a Scrivener like his father, or a Blacksmith, or a Baker, or a Weaver.

The possibilities were endless, as Alaric often said. But to Tobin, they were endlessly boring. A life of ink and parchment felt like a slow-motion rot. He didn't want to be a functional part of a machine; he wanted to be the lightning that powered it.

"Look!" a boy named Elian whispered, pointing toward the southern gate. "He’s here."

The chatter of the crowd died down as if a giant hand had been laid over the village. A small procession was moving through the market street, led by four guards in polished breastplates. Their spear-tips caught the sun, glinting with a sharp, disciplined light. But they were merely the heralds.

Behind them rode a man on a horse so white it looked carved from salt. He wore armor of brushed silver, etched with runes that seemed to shimmer with a light of their own, and a cloak of deep velvet scarlet that trailed behind him like a wake of blood. This was Sir Gregory—or "Master Gregory," as the messenger girl had called him—the Warden of the South Road and the Empire’s official representative for the Oakhaven Testing.

He didn't look like the Mages Tobin had seen in the wood-cut illustrations. He didn't have a long beard or a pointed hat. He had the hard, weathered face of a man who had spent decades in the wind, and his eyes, as he scanned the crowd, had a weight to them that made people instinctively step back. He possessed a stillness even greater than the messenger girl’s—a quietude that suggested he was the center of the world, and everything else was just moving around him.

Sir Gregory dismounted with a grace that spoke of high Agility, his boots hitting the cobblestones with a heavy, purposeful thrum. He walked toward the shrouded pedestal, the crowd parting before him like water.

The village elder, a man whose back was bowed by eighty years of history, stepped forward with a trembling bow. "Warden Gregory. Oakhaven is honored. The children are ready."

Gregory didn't nod. He merely stepped up to the small wooden stage that had been erected around the pedestal. He looked out over the twenty-four children, his gaze lingering on each of them for a fraction of a second. When those eyes met Tobin’s, the boy felt a jolt of electricity down his spine. It wasn't a sensing of mana, not yet. It was the presence of a man who lived outside the maps.

"Twelve years," Gregory began. His voice wasn't loud, but it carried to every corner of the square, amplified by some subtle artifice or sheer force of character. "Twelve years you have lived in the shadow of Oakhaven. You have played in the streams, you have helped with the harvests, and you have dreamed. Today, those dreams meet the Law."

He reached out and gripped the edge of the heavy velvet cloth covering the pedestal. With a single, fluid motion, he ripped it away.

The Testing Stone was a jagged spike of obsidian-dark crystal, about two feet tall, pulsing with a faint, rhythmic violet light that matched the beat of Tobin’s own heart. It sat on a base of worked silver, and as the air touched it, the temperature in the square seemed to drop ten degrees.

"The System does not judge merit," Gregory continued, his voice cold and precise. "It judges potential. It looks at the clay of your spirit and decides what vessel you are capable of becoming. Some of you will be the shields of the Empire. Some will be its knives. Some will be its hands."

He paused, his hand resting near the pulsing crystal. "And most of you will be its heart. The Commoners who keep the fires burning and the wheels turning. Do not mistake the 'Common' for the 'Small'. Without the heart, the hands have nothing to hold, and the shield has nothing to protect."

Tobin felt a surge of frustration. It was the same talk his father gave him. It was the "important cog" speech. He didn't want to be the heart. He didn't want to be the background noise of the world.

"But," Gregory said, and for the first time, a hint of something real—perhaps a memory of his own testing—softened his voice. "The System is also a choice. It offers a path. Once you touch the Stone, that path is yours to walk. It will give you the tools. It is up to you to find the strength to lift them."

He stepped back, his hand gesturing toward the first boy in the line, a trembling weaver’s son named Thomas. "One by one. Step forward. Touch the Stone. Receive your destiny."

Tobin watched as Thomas walked forward, his boots clicking hollowly on the wooden stage. The boy’s hand shook as he reached out. The moment his skin touched the obsidian, the violet light flared into a brilliant, blinding white. A sound like a struck bell echoed through the square.

Thomas’s eyes rolled back in his head for a second, then snapped open. He looked at his own hands, then at the status window only he could see. A slow, relieved smile spread across his face, followed by a look of quiet resignation.

"Commoner - Weaver," the Village Elder announced, reading the secondary display on the silver base.

A cheer went up from the crowd—Thomas would stay in Oakhaven. His parents embraced. The machine had found another gear.

But Tobin wasn't cheering. He was counting. He was watching the line dwindle, watching as child after child touched the stone. Commoner. Commoner. Commoner. A girl named Sela was named a Healer, and the crowd roared; her parents wept with the joy of a lottery win. A boy named Jon became a Warrior, his chest puffing out as he realized he’d never have to plow a field again.

And then, it was Tobin’s turn.

He felt the eyes of the village on him. He felt the weight of his father’s "twelve" and the memory of the punch in his jaw. He walked up the steps, the wood creaking under his weight. He felt small. He felt average. But as he looked at the pulsing violet heart of the Testing Stone, his internal monologue was a frantic, white-hot scream.

*Mage. Mage. Mage. Give me the stars. Anything but the ink.*

He reached out his hand, his fingers inches from the cold, dark crystal. Sir Gregory was watching him, his expression unreadable, his arms crossed over his silver breastplate.

Tobin closed his eyes, took a ragged breath that tasted of ozone and spiced cider, and pressed his palm against the Stone.
