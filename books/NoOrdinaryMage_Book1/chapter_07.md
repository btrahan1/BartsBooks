# Chapter 7: Goodbyes

The village square of Oakhaven, which had been a riot of color and music only an hour before, felt like a hollow shell. The banners still whipped in the wind, their bright dyes mocking the sudden, heavy silence that had settled over the crowd. The Festival was over, but not in the way anyone had expected. The air no longer tasted of spiced cider and roasted meat; it tasted of ash and the lingering, bitter ozone of the dormant Testing Stone.

Tobin sat on the edge of the wooden stage, his boots dangling over the side. He felt remarkably small. The "Interdicted" label, though invisible to most, felt like a physical weight on his shoulders—a yoke of his own making. He looked at his hands, still ink-stained, still average. He didn't feel like a hero. He felt like a boy who had just set fire to his own house to see which way the smoke went.

Below him, the village was in a state of muted chaos. Parents were leadings their children away, some whispering in hushed, terrified tones about "the boy who refused the blessing." The village elder was huddled with the guild masters, their faces grim as they discussed the legal ramifications of an Interdicted youth living within the town limits.

But the most painful sight was Alaric.

His father stood near the front of the square, his shoulders slumped as if the very air had become too heavy to breathe. He looked distraught—not angry, which would have been easier for Tobin to handle, but utterly, heartbreakingly lost. The violet ink on his fingers seemed darker than usual, a permanent reminder of the life he’d painstakingly recorded for his son, a life that Tobin had just thrown into the dirt.

Tobin watched as Sir Gregory stepped off the stage. The Warden’s silver armor didn't clank; it hummed with that same predator’s grace he’d shown earlier. He walked toward Alaric, his scarlet cloak trailing behind him like a lingering threat.

Alaric didn't move. He didn't even seem to notice the Warden’s approach until Gregory was standing directly in front of him. Tobin held his breath, expecting a decree of exile or a lecture on the Empire’s laws.

"Master Alaric," Gregory began. His voice was no longer the cold, public instrument of the Testing. It was softer, carrying a resonant depth that seemed to bypass the ears and go straight to the bone.

Alaric looked up, his eyes glassy and red-rimmed. "He... he refused, Warden. My boy. He had a twelve. He could have been safe. He could have had a tower." He reached out, his hands trembling as if trying to grasp a thread that had already vanished. "Fret not for the legalities, Warden. I'll... I'll find a way to make him touch it again. He was just confused. The pressure—"

"Fret not, Master Alaric," Gregory interrupted, his hand—clad in a glove of fine, white leather—resting briefly on Alaric’s shoulder. It was a gesture of surprising warmth from a man who looked like he was carved from mountain ice. "We will take good care of him."

Alaric blinked, the words taking a moment to penetrate the fog of his distress. "Take care of him? You... you are taking him?"

"He has chosen a road that does not lead to Oakhaven," Gregory said. "And as Warden of the South Road, it is my duty to see that those who choose the Unspoken Path are not left to wander and wither. It will be hard, Master Alaric. Harder than anything young Tobin has ever faced. The world hits those on the path much harder than it hits those in the towers."

"But why?" Alaric asked, his voice cracking. "He wanted to be a mage! Every book he ever read, every day in the shop—he dreamed of nothing else. Why would he discard the very thing he loves?"

Gregory looked up at Tobin, who was watching from the stage. The Warden’s eyes were unreadable, but there was a flicker of something that might have been respect. 

"And he will be a mage, eventually," Gregory said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "If he is willing to work for it. If he is willing to build the foundation that the Academy ignores. He will not be the kind of mage you see in the wood-cuts, Alaric. He will not be a man who hides behind a three-syllable shield and hopes the world is polite enough to wait."

He smiled—a thin, dangerous line that didn't reach his eyes. "He will be No Ordinary Mage. He will be a NOM."

Alaric stared at him, the word finally making sense. No Ordinary Mage. NOM. Not an absence of magic, but a different kind of presence. 

"Is that... is that what she is?" Alaric asked, gesturing toward the shadows where the girl was still waiting.

"She is a student of the path," Gregory said. "And if Tobin survives the first few months, he might eventually earn the right to walk beside her. But for now, he needs his things. The South Road waits for no one."

Gregory turned and looked at Tobin. "Ten minutes, boy. Pack what is essential. Leave the books. They are maps of a dead world. From now on, you learn from the terrain."

Tobin scrambled off the stage, his heart hammering in his chest. He ran toward his father, almost colliding with him. For a long, silent moment, the two of them just stood there in the emptying square—the Scrivener and the Interdicted.

"Dad, I—" Tobin started, but Alaric pulled him into a crushing embrace. 

"I don't understand it, Tobin," Alaric whispered into his hair, his voice thick with tears. "I don't understand any of it. But if the Warden says you’ll be a mage... if he says there’s a path for you... then you go. You go and you don't look back at this dusty shop."

He pulled away, his hands framing Tobin’s face. He looked at the bruise on his son's jaw, then at the '12' that was still glowing in Tobin's peripheral vision. "Just... don't let them break the heart of you, son. The hands can be replaced, and the head can be filled with numbers. but the heart is all you have."

Tobin nodded, his own throat feeling like it was clogged with parchment dust. He ran back to the shop, his boots thumping on the cobblestones for what he knew would be the last time. 

The shop felt different. The smell of ink and old land deeds, which had felt like a cage only days ago, now felt like a comfort he didn't deserve. He moved through the rooms with a frantic energy, grabbing a sturdy leather pack and stuffing it with a few changes of hard-wearing clothes, a sturdy knife his father had given him for his tenth birthday, and a small, leather-bound notebook with blank pages.

He paused at his desk. His favorite book—*The Principles of Arcane Resonance*—lay there, its cover worn and familiar. He reached for it, then remembered Gregory’s words. 

*Leave the books.*

He pulled his hand back. He looked at the ink-stains on the wood, the records of his childhood labor. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, polished silver coin—a birthday gift from his father that he’d intended to spend at the festival. He laid it in the center of the desk, a silent promise to return it with interest.

When he stepped back out into the square, Sir Gregory and the girl were already mounted. Gregory was on his salt-white horse, and the girl sat atop a sturdy, mountain pony that looked as rugged and tireless as she did. 

"Tobin!" Alaric called, standing by the gate. He held out a small, waxed-wrapped bundle. "For the road. Some of your mother's special oatcakes. And... and this."

He handed Tobin a small, silver-capped inkwell—the finest the shop possessed. its glass was dark and heavy, the ink inside a deep, midnight blue. "You might not be a scrivener anymore, but a man should always have a way to leave his mark."

Tobin took the bundle, his vision blurring. "Thanks, Dad. I'll... I'll write. somehow."

Gregory looked down at Tobin. "Are you ready, ghost?"

Tobin looked at his father one last time, then at the village elder who was still pretending he didn't exist, and finally at the girl. She looked at him, her expression as flat as ever, but she shifted her pony slightly to make room for him to walk beside her.

"I'm ready," Tobin said.

They moved out of the square, the clip-clop of the hooves echoing off the silent buildings. The villagers watched from their doorways, their faces a mix of pity and fear. They saw a boy who had broken the law, a boy who had chosen the dust over the silver.

As they reached the southern gate, the sun began to dip below the horizon, bathing Oakhaven in a final, golden glow. Tobin looked back once, seeing his father’s lone figure standing in the gateway, a small, dark shape against the light.

Then, they turned a corner, and the village was gone.

The South Road stretched out before them, a ribbon of red clay winding toward the jagged peaks of the mountains. The air was colder here, sharper, and filled with the scent of pine and adventure.

Tobin walked beside the girl's pony, his pack heavy on his shoulders and his jaw still aching. He didn't have a class designation. He didn't have a mana-pool. He didn't have a map.

But as the first stars began to pierce through the indigo sky, he realized that for the first time in his life, he wasn't reading about the world. He was walking on it.

He was Tobin of Oakhaven. He was Interdicted. He was a ghost on the South Road.

And he was going to be No Ordinary Mage.
