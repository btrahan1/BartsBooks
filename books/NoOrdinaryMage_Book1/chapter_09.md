# Chapter 9: The First Lesson

The sun had finally surrendered to the jagged teeth of the Ironwood Ridge, leaving a bruise-colored twilight that bled into the valleys of the South Road. The air, which had been merely brisk while they were moving, turned sharply cold the moment the Salt-white horse and the mountain pony came to a halt in a small, rock-strewn clearing. 

Tobin stood by the roadside, his legs trembling from the miles of unassisted walking. His breath came in small, ghostly plumes, and the bruise on his jaw throbbed with a cold, rhythmic ache. He looked at the clearing—a patch of grey stone and stunted, scrubby brush—and felt a wave of exhaustion that threatened to pull him toward the dirt.

Sir Gregory dismounted with that same effortless grace, his boots making a soft, crunching sound on the frozen gravel. He didn't look tired. He didn't even look like the cold affected him. He simply stood there, a silver silhouette against the deepening gloom, and looked at Tobin.

"We break for camp here," Gregory stated. "And here, your first lesson begins."

Tobin straightened his back, trying to ignore the protest from his aching shoulders. "A lesson in what, Sir? Shielding? Mana-sensing?"

Gregory didn't answer immediately. He began to unbuckle the straps of his saddle, his movements precise and economical. "If this were a common group," he said, his voice carrying clearly in the thin mountain air, "the roles would be clearly defined by the map. A Mage would wait until the branches and twigs were brought before them, standing in the center of the camp and conserving their mana. When the pile was ready, they would snap their fingers, cast a simple *Ignition* spell, and the fire would roar to life. Efficient. Low-effort. Predictable."

Tobin nodded. That’s how it was in the books. The Mage was the pinnacle, the one who provided the final, magical touch to the mundane efforts of others.

"A Rogue," Gregory continued, "would be out there now, scouring the forest floor. They would use their System-assisted Agility and Luck to find the driest fuel, all while avoiding the dangerous plants and the small, hungry predators that prowl the Ironwood. They wouldn't even break a sweat; the System would guide their feet and sharpen their eyes."

He pulled the heavy leather saddle from his horse and set it on a flat stone. "A Warrior might help drag in a large log, his Strength bonus allowing him to move weight that would crush a normal man's spine. He would place it on the fire once it was burning, providing the heavy fuel that keeps the night at bay. And if anyone tripped or cut themselves in the dark, a Healer would be there to mend the scratches or wounds with a word and a flash of white light."

Tobin listened, his mind trying to reconcile this organized, assisted world with the rugged reality of the clearing. It sounded so easy. So logical.

"The System provides the harmony," Gregory said, turning to face Tobin. "It ensures that each piece of the machine does its part so the whole can function with minimum friction. But a Nom trainee, like yourself, will be able to see to all of this, alone."

Tobin froze. The word felt like a physical blow, colder than the wind. "Alone? You mean... I have to find the wood? And drag the logs? And light the fire? Without a class?"

Sir Gregory let out a short, sharp laugh that sounded like dry leaves skittering over stone. "Of course you won't be able to do it at first! You have the physique of a scrivener's apprentice and the survival skills of a house-cat."

He gestured toward the shadows near the horses, where the girl was already busy unhitching her own gear. "Elana will help you. Though, 'help' might be a strong word. She will 'guide' you. Learn well, Tobin. The South Road is a harsh teacher, and it doesn't give extra credit for effort."

Elana. The name finally had a sound. 

The girl stepped into the faint light reflected from the grey stone. She had already removed her travel-cloak, revealing the rugged, practical layers beneath. She looked at Tobin, her expression as flat and unreadable as ever. She didn't say a word; she simply turned and walked toward the edge of the clearing, where the scrubby brush gave way to the dark, twisted shapes of the Ironwood trees.

"Follow her," Gregory commanded, his voice already receding into the business of tending to his horse. "The night is coming, and a ghost without a fire is just a cold corpse."

Tobin scrambled to follow Elana, his boots clattering over the rocks. He felt a sudden, frantic surge of anxiety. He’d never even built a fire in his father’s hearth without Alaric’s supervision, let alone gathered fuel in a mountain forest at night.

"Elana!" he called out, his voice sounding small against the vastness of the ridge. "Wait! What do I look for first? Is there a specific type of wood? I read that Ironwood burns slow but is hard to ignite—"

Elana stopped at the very edge of the tree-line. She didn't look back at him. She didn't even seem to hear him. She just pointed a finger toward a cluster of dead, grey-barked shrubs about twenty paces away.

"Deadfall," she said. Her voice was as compact as her strike. "Dry. Not rotten. Move."

Tobin hurried to the shrubs. They looked like twisted skeletons, their branches brittle and sharp. He reached out to grab a handful of twigs, but as his fingers closed around them, a sharp pain shot through his hand. 

"Ow!" He pulled back, seeing a thin line of blood already forming on his palm. A thorn, long and curved like a cat’s claw, had sliced right through the skin.

He looked around for the Healer he knew wasn't there. He looked for the System-assisted Agility that would have guided his hand away from the danger. There was nothing. Just the cold wind, the darkening sky, and the stinging pain in his hand.

He looked at Elana. She was already twenty paces further into the trees, her movements silent and fluid. She was picking up branches with a speed that felt like magic, but Tobin knew now that it was just raw, unassisted skill. She wasn't avoiding the thorns; she was moving in the spaces between them.

Tobin gritted his teeth, wiped his bloody hand on his breeches, and went back to the shrubs. He moved slower now, his mind trying to do the work that the System usually handled. *Look at the branch. Trace the thorns. Find the grip. Pull.*

It was exhausting. It required a level of focus that made his head ache. Every branch was a puzzle. Every step on the uneven forest floor was a gamble with his ankles. He realized now what Gregory meant by "assistance." The System was a filter that removed the friction of the world. Without it, the world was nothing *but* friction.

By the time he had a small armful of twigs and dry limbs, his hands were covered in scratches and his breath was coming in ragged gasps. He dragged himself back to the center of the clearing, where Gregory was already laying out his bedroll. 

Tobin dumped the wood onto the stone. It looked like a pathetic pile, barely enough to boil a pot of tea, let alone keep a man warm through a mountain night.

"Not enough," Elana’s voice drifted from the darkness. She appeared a moment later, carrying a massive, dead log over one shoulder. She looked like she was carrying a bag of feathers, her Strength of fourteen working in perfect harmony with her body. 

She dropped the log near Tobin’s small pile with a heavy *thud* that seemed to rattle his bones. 

"More," she said, looking at him for a heartbeat before turning back to the woods.

Tobin sighed, a plume of steam escaping his lips. He looked at his shaking hands, then at the dark, unforgiving shape of the Ironwood Ridge. He thought about the "twice-hit." He thought about the "alone."

He realized that the first lesson wasn't about fire. It was about the cost of being No Ordinary Mage. It was about the fact that every single thing he’d taken for granted—the warmth of a hearth, the safety of a shop, the assistance of a map—was a luxury he could no longer afford.

He turned back toward the woods, his boots heavy and his jaw throbbing. He didn't have a spell for light. He didn't have a bonus for strength. He didn't have a stencil for survival.

But as he reached for the next branch, his fingers avoiding the thorns by mere millimeters, he felt a strange, flickering spark of something new. It wasn't mana. It wasn't the System.

It was the beginning of his own power. Unassisted. Raw. And entirely his.

He gathered more wood. He hauled larger branches. He ignored the stinging of the scratches and the cold in his marrow. He was Tobin of Oakhaven, the Interdicted. And if he had to become a Rogue, a Warrior, and a Mage all in one night just to survive, then that was exactly what he would do.

He worked until the moon rose, a cold, silver eye watching from the peaks. And as he finally sat down by the small, sputtering fire he’d managed to build—not with a snap of his fingers, but with flint, steel, and a dozen failed attempts—he realized that the fire didn't just feel warm.

It felt earned.
