# Chapter 13: The Strength of the Generalist

The ascent toward the high peaks of the Ironwood Ridge was a relentless grind of verticality. The air was thinning now, each breath a conscious effort of the lungs against the vacuum of the high altitude. Tobin walked in the shadow of the Salt-white horse, his boots finding the rhythmic 'thrum' of the earth that Sir Gregory seemed to navigate by. 

His stat sheet—the silent, observational ledger in his mind—was his only consolation for the sheer physical toll of the climb. He was balanced. 11/11/11/10/12/10. It was a neat, clean block of numbers, a foundation etched in red clay and mountain grit. 

But as the path narrowed further, pressing them against a wall of obsidian-dark ironwood, a thought that had been gnawing at him since the previous campfire finally found its way to the surface. It was a cold, sharp worry, more persistent than the wind.

"Sir Gregory?" Tobin asked, his voice sounding hollow in the thin air. 

The Warden didn't slow his horse, but he inclined his head slightly, acknowledging the boy's presence. "Speak, ghost."

"Something you said earlier is bothering me a bit," Tobin started, his 'Intelligence 12' brain trying to frame the question without sounding ungrateful for the path he’d chosen. "You mentioned that an elite warrior might have a strength of forty, but be lower in other stats because of the siphon. And you said a mage gets stencils for spells that allow them to bypass the arithmetic of mana-resonance."

Gregory waited, the rhythmic clip-clop of the hooves the only response.

"So," Tobin took a deep breath, "does that mean a Warrior will always be a better 'warrior' than those on our path? I mean, forty is a huge number. I’m an eleven. And a Mage... will they always be a better 'mage' than us too? If we don't have the siphoned power or the assistance... will we just be weaker than any other class in their own field? Are we just second-rate versions of four different things?"

Tobin followed along, deep in thought. He didn't speak for some time, the weight of the realization making his pack feel even heavier. He’d left Oakhaven to be more than a footnote, but the logic of forty versus eleven seemed to suggest he’d just chosen a path of permanent average-ness.

Sir Gregory finally pulled the Salt-white horse to a halt. They were at a small, flat outcropping that overlooked the valley they’d crossed two days ago. From here, Oakhaven was nothing more than a faint smudge of smoke on the horizon.

Gregory dismounted, his silver armor shimmering with a faint, internal light that seemed to pulse in time with the mountain’s heartbeat. He walked to the edge of the outcropping and looked out at the world.

"A fair question, Tobin," Gregory said, his voice carrying that same resonant depth that bypassed the ears. "And on paper—on the map the Empire provides—you are absolutely correct. A Warrior with Strength forty will hit harder than you. A Mage with targeted Intelligence will cast faster than you. In a controlled, specialized context, they are the superior tools."

He turned, his eyes locking with Tobin’s. "But tell me, ghost. What happens to the Warrior when the ground isn't flat? What happens when the enemy isn't standing in front of him, but is moving faster than his unassisted Agility of ten can track? What happens when he needs to cross a ravine, or recognize a trap, or... hide?"

Tobin thought about the elite warriors in his father’s shop. They were magnificent, but they moved like stone statues. 

"The specialized class is a tower, Tobin," Gregory continued, gesturing toward the horizon. "It is built high on a very narrow plot of land. It is visible, impressive, and within its specific domain, it is unshakeable. But a tower has a single point of failure. If you break the foundation, if you change the environment so that Strength forty no longer matters... the tower falls."

He walked back toward the horses, his boots crunching on the scree. "The elite Warrior with a Strength of forty and an Agility of ten is a man who has surrendered his ability to be a whole person for the sake of being a better hammer. If the world stops being a nail, he is useless. Worse than useless—he is a liability. He cannot adapt. He cannot redirect. He can only hit. And if he misses... he has nothing else to fall back on."

Elana, who had been listening from her pony, let out a short, sharp snort. "I’ve seen it," she said. "A 'Master' Warrior who couldn't climb a three-foot ledge because his Agility was so low the System couldn't 'assist' the lift. He just stood there, waiting for a Rogue to rope him up. Pathetic."

"That is the cost of the siphon," Gregory said. "You are building a foundation that covers the entire territory. You will never be as 'strong' as the man who has siphoned his entire life into his biceps. You will never be as 'fast' as the man who has siphoned his soul into his hamstrings. But you will be something they can never be: Resilient."

He leaned in, his voice dropping to a whisper. "Because your Strength of eleven is supported by an Agility of eleven, a Constitution of eleven, and an Intelligence of twelve. Every part of you is in harmony with the rest. When you hit, you don't just hit with your arm; you hit with your whole being. When you move, you don't just move with your legs; you move with your mind. Your power is integrated. It is unassisted, which means it is yours."

"So... we're not weaker," Tobin said, a flicker of understanding warming his chest. "We’re just... consistent?"

"We are the territory, Tobin," Gregory said, mounting his horse again. "The specialist is a specialist only in a world that stays still. A NOM is a master of the world as it actually is—messy, unpredictable, and unforgiving. You won't out-lift the specialized Warrior in a contest of strength. But you will stay standing long after he has collapsed because he forgot to breathe, or because he didn't see the hole in the ground, or because he couldn't think of a plan beyond 'hit it again'."

He looked down at Tobin. "A Mage with a stencil is a man who knows a thousand ways to light a fire. A NOM is a person who understands the fire itself. Who do you think survives when the mana-stencil flickers? Who do you think finds the breakthrough when the old spells fail?"

Tobin gripped the straps of his pack. He thought about the 40 Str/10 Agi warrior. It sounded like a nightmare now—a man trapped in his own lopsided body, a tool that could only do one thing well. 

"I'd rather be the guy who knows how to draw the lines," Tobin said, his voice gaining a new, hard certainty.

"Good," Gregory said. "Because the Ironwood Ascent is not a nail. And no amount of Strength will help you if your Perception misses the shift in the stone."

They moved forward again, the path turning into a series of jagged, vertical steps. Tobin climbed, his 11 Strength working in tandem with his 11 Agility and his 11 Constitution. He felt the harmony Gregory had described—a solid, un-siphoned unity that made him feel bigger than the sum of his mediocre numbers.

He wasn't a hammer. He wasn't a stencil. 

He wanted to be the one who was still standing after the towers fell.

The sun hit the peaks of the ridge, turning the obsidian bark of the Ironwood into a sea of liquid fire. Tobin didn't stop to admire the view. He focused on the next step, the next breath, and the solid, unassisted reality of his own growing foundation.

Tobin hugged the side of the stone, his boots finding the rhythmic 'thrum' of the earth. He was No Ordinary Mage, not because of a title, but because he was starting to see that 'Ordinary' was just another word for 'fragile.'

He pulled himself up the next grey stone step, a long, long way from his father's shop, but for the first time, he felt like he was standing on his own two feet.
