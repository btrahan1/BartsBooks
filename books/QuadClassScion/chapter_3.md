**Chapter 3: The Unwitting Mentor**

The seventh sun set, painting the sky in hues of orange and bruised
purple. Every muscle in Gabriel\'s body screamed. It was a new kind of
pain, a deep, satisfying ache that was entirely different from the
gnawing emptiness of hunger he\'d known his whole life. In just one
week, the rhythm of the farm had become his own: waking in the pre-dawn
chill, hauling buckets of water from the well, mucking out the animal
stalls, and then spending the rest of the day in the fields, clearing
rocks and turning the stubborn soil.

He had eaten more in seven days than he had in the previous month.
Silas\'s stews were simple but thick with vegetables, and Gabriel
devoured every meal, feeling the food turn directly into fuel. He could
already feel the change. His tunic felt a little tighter across his
shoulders, and the spade that felt impossibly heavy on the first day now
felt merely difficult.

As he finished washing the supper dishes, Silas grunted from his chair
on the cottage porch. \"Boy. Come here.\"

Gabriel\'s stomach tightened. The week was up. He dried his hands and
walked out to stand before the old man, his posture straight, his
expression carefully neutral.

Silas didn\'t look at him, staring out instead at the fields Gabriel had
begun to clear. \"The goat\'s stall is cleaner than it\'s been in a
year,\" he said, his voice a low rumble. \"I saw you bracing the fence
posts in the west field before they had a chance to snap. The last two
boys I had would have waited for them to break first.\"

He finally turned his pale eyes to Gabriel. \"You work harder than any
two of them combined. But you also work smarter. You don\'t just see the
job in front of you; you see the one after it.\"

Gabriel remained silent, his heart thumping.

\"The deal was for one week,\" Silas said, his face unreadable in the
twilight. \"I\'m a man of my word. You\'re free to go.\"

The knot of dread tightened. \"But,\" Silas continued, a little more
gruffly, \"if you want to stay\... the loft is yours. Three meals a day.
And two coppers a week. It ain\'t much, but it\'s more than you had.\"

A wave of relief so powerful it almost buckled his knees washed over
Gabriel. \"I\'ll stay,\" he said, his voice a bit hoarse. \"Thank you.\"

\"Don\'t thank me,\" the old man grumbled, turning his gaze back to the
fields. \"You earned it. The System rewards effort, boy. Not just the
strength of your back, but the sharpness of your mind. Most fools forget
that.\"

Gabriel nodded, a genuine, unforced smile touching his lips. He walked
back towards the barn, two copper pieces in his pocket feeling heavier
than gold. Lying back in the sweet-smelling hay, he closed his eyes. The
dream of the underwater cavern was still vivid, but it was no longer a
desperate hope. Now, it was a destination.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out the two coppers. He closed his
fist around one. *This one is for the grind.* Then he looked at the
second coin. His mind went back to Graywall, to the pinched faces of the
younger children. A single copper was a pitiful amount, but the gesture
changed everything for *him*. He tucked the coin into a small tear in
the lining of his bundle. *One for Graywall,* he vowed. *Someday, it
will be more.*

With that promise made, his mind drifted to the old farmer\'s
words. *The sharpness of your mind.* He replayed the past week. Silas
splitting wood---never a wasted motion. Silas mending a harness---his
gnarled fingers moving with the dexterity of a master craftsman. The
farm wasn\'t failing because Silas was a bad farmer. It was failing
because he was one old man doing the work of five. The man himself was a
model of efficiency.

A new dimension of his training plan slotted into place. The farm was no
longer just a place to build his body. It was a classroom. Silas was no
longer just his employer. He was an unwitting mentor, a puzzle to be
solved. He fell asleep not with a smile, but with the focused calm of a
student who has just discovered the entrance to a secret library.
