## Chapter 1: The Sunbeam T-20

The shop smelled of ozone, 3-in-One oil, and the faint, sweet memory of singed dust. It was a precise smell. A clean smell.

Arthur Penhaligon adjusted the magnification loop on his headset and leaned over the stainless steel operating table.

"Subject is a Sunbeam T-20," Arthur murmured to the empty room. His voice was clipped, a remnant of Cambridge and the Yard. "1954 vintage. Radiant Control. No levers. No buttons. A masterpiece of mid-century automated breakfast engineering."

He picked up a set of non-marring forceps.

"Patient presented with total respiratory failure. Smoke inhalation reported by the witness—one Mrs. Higgins—followed by a catastrophic seizure of the carriage mechanism."

Arthur treated the chrome casing with the reverence usually reserved for a fresh corpse at a crime scene. He wore nitrile gloves. Not to protect himself from the toaster, but to protect the toaster from the oils of his skin. The T-20 was a mirror; it reflected Arthur’s face—sharp-nosed, grey-templed, eyes narrowed in forensic scrutiny—back at him, distorted only by the curve of the metal.

"Beginning exploratory incision," he whispered.

With a jeweler’s screwdriver, he removed the base plate. He didn't just scatter the screws on the workbench; he placed them into a velvet-lined sorting tray, organized by length and thread pitch. Chaos was the enemy. Entropy was the suspect. Arthur was the law.

He lifted the housing. The crime scene was revealed.

Arthur sighed, a long exhale through his nose. It was exactly as he had profiled.

"Blunt force trauma," he noted. "Complicated by thermal fusion."

Inside the toaster’s delicate ribcage, wedged tight against the central bi-metal sensor, was a shapeless, bubbling mass of green plastic. It had once been a soldier. A brave infantryman, perhaps, sent on a suicide mission into the glowing trenches of the breakfast service by Mrs. Higgins’ grandson.

Arthur reached in with the forceps. He applied gentle, steady pressure. The plastic had bonded to the heating element—a difficult extraction. If he pulled too hard, the element would snap. If he heated it, the fumes would be toxic.

"Careful now," Arthur whispered. "Don't struggle."

He applied a single drop of solvent to the edge of the plastic lesion. He waited exactly thirty seconds. Then, with a tactical twist of his wrist, he pried the intruder loose.

It came away with a microscopic *snap*.

Arthur held the object up to the light of the magnifying lamp. It was barely recognizable, a casualty of war melted down to his boots and rifle.

"Cause of death confirmed," Arthur said, dropping the green lump into an evidence baggie. "Foreign object intrusion resulting in thermal paralysis."

He looked back at the toaster. The mechanism was clogged, the contacts carbonized, but the heart... the heart was still viable.

"We can save him," Arthur decided.

He loved the machines. People lied. People covered up their crimes. People claimed they "didn't know how the vase broke" or that the laptop "just stopped working" despite the obvious residue of sugary tea on the motherboard. But machines? Machines were honest. If a gear stripped, it was because of stress. If a wire frayed, it was friction. Cause and effect. Pure. Linear.

Fixable.

The bell above the shop door chimed—a brass naval bell, tuned to a perfect G-sharp.

Arthur didn't jump. He carefully covered the exposed toaster with a lint-free cloth, removed his headset, and turned.

Mrs. Higgins stood in the doorway, clutching her handbag. She looked at the shop interior, her eyes darting nervously. Penhaligon’s Restoration didn't look like a repair shop. It looked like a laboratory. The floors were black and white checkered tile, scrubbed spotless. The walls were lined with tools hanging in silhouettes.

And there, behind the counter, hung The Sign. Hand-lettered in gold leaf on black slate:

> **PENHALIGON’S RESTORATION**
>
> **Standard Rate:** $25.00 / Hour.
> **Minimum Charge:** 1 Hour.
> **Average Repair:** 4 Hours.
> **Current Success Rate:** 97.6%
>
> *If I cannot repair it, there is no charge.*
>
> *If you cannot pay, or are unwilling to pay, compensation may be rendered in:*
> *A) Equal time spent in labor of Proprietor's choosing.*
> *B) Sufficient baked goods (subject to quality inspection).*

"Mr. Penhaligon?" Mrs. Higgins asked, her voice trembling slightly. " Is it... is it terminal?"

Arthur walked to the counter, peeling off his gloves with a snap.

"It was touch and go, Mrs. Higgins," Arthur said gravely. "The intruder was deeply embedded. A Green Beret, I believe. Or perhaps a plastic Paratrooper who missed his drop zone."

Mrs. Higgins flushed pink. "That boy. I told him the kitchen is not a battlefield."

"On the contrary, madam. The kitchen is the most dangerous room in the house." Arthur retrieved the evidence baggie and placed it on the counter. "I have removed the tumor. The heating element requires debridement, and the bi-metal strip needs recalibrating. But the prognosis is excellent."

"Oh, thank goodness. They don't make them like that anymore."

"No," Arthur agreed, looking back at the shrouded form on his table. "They do not. Modern toasters are plastic coffins for bread. This is an instrument."

He tapped the ledger on the counter.

"I estimate two hours of surgical time. That will be fifty dollars."

Mrs. Higgins hesitated. She opened her purse, frowned, and looked up at the sign. She looked back at Arthur.

"I... I only have thirty dollars on me, Mr. Penhaligon. And my pension doesn't come in until Tuesday."

Arthur’s expression didn't change. He looked at the money, then at the sign, then at Mrs. Higgins. He valued his time. He valued his skill. But he also valued the mechanism of the community.

"The sign is binding, Mrs. Higgins," Arthur said stiffly. "Thirty dollars will cover the first hour and partial materials."

"And the rest?"

Arthur leaned forward, his eyes landing on the canvas tote bag hanging from her shoulder. He detected a scent. Vanilla. Butter. High-quality dark chocolate.

"I detect," Arthur said, his nose twitching once, "that you are carrying fresh macaroons. Coconut?"

"Chocolate-dipped," she whispered.

Arthur picked up a pen and opened his intake log. He wrote: *Case #402. Sunbeam T-20. Status: Resuscitated.*

"Half a dozen should settle the account," Arthur said. "Provided the texture is correct."

"They're still warm," Mrs. Higgins offered.

"Acceptable," Arthur said. "Leave them on the counter. The patient will be discharged at 14:00 hours. Good day, madam."

As she bustled out, leaving the cookies and the silence behind, Arthur picked up a macaroon. He inspected it for structural integrity, nodded, and took a bite.

Perfect.

He turned back to the workbench. The world outside was a mess of broken laws and broken people, but in here, for fifty dollars and a cookie, he could make things right.

"Right then," Arthur said to the toaster. "Let's get you polished."
