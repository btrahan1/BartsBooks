## Chapter 10: The Hotel

The hotel room in Zurich was less a room and more a sovereign state of luxury. The view looked out over the Limmat River, the lights of the Old Town reflecting on the water like scattered coins. Arthur was currently disassembling the room’s Nespresso machine on the desk, convinced that the pump pressure was 0.5 bars too low.

The door chime sounded.

Mr. Vance entered. He was followed by a man in a dark suit who moved with the silent efficiency of a well-trained shadow. The man carried a garment bag stamped with the logo of a Savile Row tailor. He hung the bag in the cedar closet, nodded once to Vance, and evaporated back into the hallway.

Arthur wiped a smudge of coffee grounds from his thumb and stood up.

Vance was grinning. It was his "showtime" grin.

"Good evening, Arthur. Please tell me you haven't voided the warranty on the coffee maker."

"The warranty was void the moment they calibrated the water temperature to 88 degrees instead of 93," Arthur replied, picking up a tiny screwdriver.

Vance chuckled and gestured to the closet.

Arthur looked at the garment bag, then back at Vance.

"So," Arthur said, "you have chosen one of the items I repaired, and wish to show it off tonight. I will be your guest, and I assume that is a very nice tuxedo in that garment bag."

"Midnight blue," Vance confirmed. "Peak lapels. Just like mine. We must present a united front."

"I could guess," Arthur said, tapping the screwdriver against his chin. "But I don't have enough data. I finished three projects this week. The 18th-century automaton of the writing boy? The Fabergé egg with the seized carriage mechanism? Or the vintage Leica camera that had been dropped in a lake?"

He studied Vance's face for a micro-expression.

"The gala tonight is at the Kunsthaus, isn't it? An art museum."

"It is," Vance said.

"Then the camera is too small for a theatrical reveal," Arthur deduced. "And the Fabergé egg, while impressive, is a static display. You want movement. You want gasps."

"So, which of the items will you... 'demonstrate'?" Arthur asked.

Vance walked over to the mini-bar and poured two sparkling waters. He handed one to Arthur.

"Neither," Vance said. "We are bringing the Jaquet-Droz."

Arthur froze. The glass stopped halfway to his lips.

"The Writer?" Arthur whispered. "The Automaton?"

"The very one," Vance said. "The one you spent the last four nights regulating because the ink flow was inconsistent on the downstroke of the letter 'P'."

Arthur’s mind flashed back to the workbench. The Jaquet-Droz automaton was a masterpiece of 18th-century robotics. A small mechanical boy, capable of dipping a quill in ink and writing a programmable sentence. When Arthur had opened it, he found the cam-stack—the machine's brain—had been coated in a century of dried oil. It had taken him forty hours just to clean the cams without altering their profile.

"But Mr. Vance," Arthur said, "that machine is incredibly sensitive to vibration. Transporting it to a gala..."

"Is a risk," Vance finished. "Which is why you are coming. To ensure he behaves."

Vance leaned in, his eyes sparkling.

"Tonight, Arthur, the crème de la crème of European society will be there. And at 9:00 PM, I will unveil the boy. I have programmed him to write a specific sentence. A sentence that will serve as... an invitation."

"An invitation to what?"

"To the future of my little enterprise," Vance said cryptically. "Now, get dressed. The car is downstairs. And Arthur?"

"Yes?"

"Make sure you bring your toolkit. The hidden one. The one that fits in a tuxedo pocket."

Arthur looked at the garment bag. He looked at the disassembled coffee machine. He looked at Vance.

"I always carry the pocket kit," Arthur said. "One never knows when a salad fork might need straightening."

He walked to the closet.

"But if that boy spills ink on my tuxedo," Arthur warned, "I am billing you for the dry cleaning. At $135 an hour."

Vance laughed. "Deal."
