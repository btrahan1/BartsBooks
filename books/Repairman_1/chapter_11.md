## Chapter 11: The Setup

The ballroom of the Kunsthaus was a cavern of marble and gold leaf, currently echoing with the frantic hush of caterers setting tables. In the center of the room, on a raised dais draped in velvet, stood a glass case.

Inside the case sat The Writer.

The mechanical boy looked serene. His painted eyes stared at the blank sheet of parchment before him. His right hand, holding a goose-quill pen, hovered just above the inkwell.

Arthur stood next to the open case, his tuxedo jacket discarded on a nearby chair, his shirtsleeves rolled up to the elbow. He was wearing his magnification loupe.

"Cam stack seven," Arthur murmured to himself. "Pressure coefficient on the quill tip... adjusted to 0.04 grams."

He used a pair of non-magnetic tweezers to make a microscopic adjustment to a spring near the automaton’s shoulder. It was the spring responsible for the dipping motion. Too loose, and the pen wouldn't pick up enough ink. Too tight, and the quill would smash into the bottom of the well.

He verified the ink viscosity. He checked the paper alignment. He ensured the drive wheel was fully wound but not over-torqued.

Arthur exhaled, a long, steady breath. He stepped back. He removed the loupe. He rolled down his sleeves and fastened his cufflinks—gold gears, naturally.

"Patient salvaged and calibrated," Arthur announced to the empty air.

"Most excellent."

Vance stepped out from the shadows of a marble pillar. He looked impeccable, holding a glass of scotch.

"Is he ready for the performance?" Vance asked.

"He is," Arthur said, putting on his jacket and adjusting the lapels. "I have programmed the cam stack for the phrase you requested. Though I must say, it is an unusual choice."

Vance walked up to the glass case. He looked at the mechanical boy with pride.

"It is a statement of intent, Arthur. The people arriving tonight think that the old world is dead. They think craftsmanship is a relic. They think everything can be replaced by an app or a 3D printer."

Vance turned to Arthur.

"Tonight, a two-hundred-year-old boy is going to prove them wrong."

Vance checked his watch.

"Guests arrive in forty-five minutes. The reveal is at dessert. Until then, Arthur, you must mingle."

Arthur stiffened. "Mingle? Mr. Vance, I fix machines. Machines do not make small talk about the weather or the state of the bond market."

"Think of it as forensic analysis," Vance suggested, sipping his scotch. "Listen to them. Identify the ones who are broken. The ones who are bored. The ones who are looking for something... real."

He patted Arthur on the shoulder.

"Besides, you look the part. You look like a man who knows secrets. Just nod mysteriously and sip your drink. It works for me."

Arthur sighed. "Very well. But if anyone spills red wine on a rug, I cannot be held responsible for my urge to spot-clean it immediately."

"I would expect nothing less," Vance laughed. "Now, come. Let us toast to the machine."

They stood before the silent boy.

"To the mechanism," Arthur said quietly.

"To the magic," Vance replied.

They clinked glasses. The boy sat motionless, his pen poised, waiting for the command to write the future.
