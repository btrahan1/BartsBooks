## Chapter 2: Resuscitation

The afternoon light slanted through the front window, catching the dust motes dancing in the air—though there were significantly fewer dust motes in Penhaligon’s Restoration than in the rest of the universe. Arthur had swept at 3:00 PM. It was now 4:45 PM.

When the bell chimed G-sharp, Arthur was already waiting.

The Sunbeam T-20 sat on the counter, no longer shrouded. It gleamed with an intensity that threatened to blind passersby. The chrome wasn't just clean; it was optically corrected. Arthur had buffed it until the surface tension looked like liquid mercury.

Mrs. Higgins stepped inside, blinking against the shine.

"Oh my," she breathed. "Is that... is that mine?"

"Identity confirmed via serial number," Arthur said, looking up from a clipboard. "Though I admit, the reconstructive surgery has been transformative. It looked ten years older when it arrived. Stress does terrible things to the complexion."

He tapped the toaster’s side with a gloved finger.

"Standard procedure involved a full teardown. I removed the casing, degreased the chassis, and polished the contacts with a mild abrasive. The melted infantryman has been extracted and placed in a sealed evidence bag for your records."

He slid a small plastic bag across the counter. Inside was the green blob, looking like a piece of modern art representing regret.

Mrs. Higgins chuckled nervously. "I don't think I need to keep that, Mr. Penhaligon."

"I insist," Arthur said, his face deadpan. "It serves as a deterrent. Place it on the counter. When your grandson asks what it is, tell him it is the remains of the last soldier who attempted to breach the perimeter. Psychological warfare is often the most effective method of household management."

Mrs. Higgins laughed, a warm, genuine sound. "You sound like you’ve had experience with unruly children."

"I have experience with unruly criminals, madam. The psychology is remarkably similar. Impulsive. Sticky fingers. Prone to lying about their whereabouts." Arthur picked up a piece of paper. "Now, regarding the functionality."

He reached behind the counter and produced a single slice of white bread. He held it by the corners, inspecting it for flaws.

"I conducted a ballistic test firing at 14:30. Observe."

Arthur dropped the bread into the slot. He did not press a lever. He did not push a button. The Sunbeam T-20, true to its engineering genius, sensed the weight of the bread. The carriage silently, elegantly, lowered the slice into the glowing belly of the machine.

Mrs. Higgins clasped her hands. "I always loved that part. My husband, rest his soul, used to say it was like a magic trick."

Arthur’s expression softened. The clinically detached mask slipped, just a fraction. He stopped looking at the machine and looked at her.

"It is not magic, Mrs. Higgins. It is thermal expansion. The heat expands the wire, which trips the mechanism. It is physics." He paused, adjusting his cuffs. "But... reliable physics is the closest thing we have to magic in this chaotic world. Your husband had an appreciation for quality."

"He bought it for our first anniversary," she said softly. "It’s been on that counter for sixty years. It felt... wrong, seeing the empty space today."

Arthur nodded. He understood that empty space. He knew that when you removed an object from a crime scene, you left a void in the narrative. And when you removed an object from a home, you left a void in the memory.

"The empty space is filled now," Arthur said gently.

A soft *click* echoed from the machine. The toast rose silently, golden-brown, perfect.

"The Maillard reaction," Arthur announced, pointing to the toast. "Uniform caramelization. No burning. Center remains soft. This is a text-book specimen. The patient is fully rehabilitated."

Mrs. Higgins reached out and touched the cool chrome of the side panel. "Thank you, Arthur. Truly. I was afraid I’d have to buy one of those new plastic things."

"Perish the thought," Arthur shuddered. "Planned obsolescence is a crime against humanity. I am merely the defense attorney."

She opened her purse to retrieve her wallet, but Arthur raised a hand.

"The account is settled, Mrs. Higgins."

"But the extra time? The polishing?"

Arthur picked up a small saucer from behind the counter. On it sat a single crumb—the last remnant of a coconut macaroon.

"The macaroons were of exceptional structural integrity," Arthur said solemnly. "The chocolate-to-coconut ratio was within the ninety-ninth percentile of ideal parameters. In forensic terms, the bribe was sufficient to corrupt the official."

He picked up the toaster with both hands and passed it over the counter like he was handing her a folded flag.

"Take it home. And tell your grandson that if he inserts another army man, I shall have to charge him the full hourly rate. And I collect from allowance jars."

Mrs. Higgins beamed, tucking the toaster under her arm. "I will tell him. Good evening, Mr. Penhaligon."

"Good evening, Mrs. Higgins."

She left, the bell chiming its G-sharp farewell.

Arthur watched her go. He watched her walk down the street, clutching her shiny, resurrected memory. He waited until she turned the corner.

Only then did he permit himself a very small, very private smile.

He turned back to the empty shop. He picked up the evidence bag containing the melted soldier, walked over to the trash bin, and hovered his hand over it.

He hesitated.

He walked back to his shelf of spare parts—jars of screws, springs, and gears. He placed the baggie with the green blob on the shelf, right next to a jar labeled *'Unidentified Foreign Objects.'*

"Case closed," he whispered.

He reached for the broom. He had seen a speck of dust near the door.
