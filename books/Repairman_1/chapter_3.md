## Chapter 3: The Osterizer and the Music Box

The bell chimed—a cheerful G-sharp that Arthur was beginning to find increasingly intrusive.

Arthur did not look up immediately. He was deep inside the abdominal cavity of a 1974 Osterizer blender. The diagnosis was grim: a leaking gasket had led to a catastrophic lubricant hemorrhage. The motor was drowning in its own fluids.

"I am currently performing a transfusion, Ms. Freeman," Arthur said, his voice muffled by his surgical mask. "The patient is critical. Unless you are here to donate 30-weight non-detergent oil, I must ask you to observe the sterile field."

"Oh, hush, Arthur. Put the blender out of its misery."

Ms. Freeman—Clara to everyone else in town, but stubbornly 'Ms. Freeman' to Arthur—breezed toward the counter. She was a whirlwind of paisley scarves and the scent of old paper and lavender wax. She owned "The Time Capsule" next door. She had an uncanny ability to spot a diamond in a dumpster, but give her a screwdriver, and she became a threat to national security. Arthur had once seen her try to open a pocket watch with a butter knife; he still woke up in a cold sweat thinking about it.

She placed an object on the counter with a thud that made Arthur wince.

"Careful," he hissed, snapping his head up. "That is a countertop, not a drum."

"It’s sturdy. Unlike this." She tapped the wooden box she’d brought in. "I picked it up at an estate sale in Ridley. The grandkids were going to toss it in the goodwill pile."

Arthur pulled down his magnification loupe and looked.

His irritation evaporated, replaced instantly by the cold, clear focus of the analyst.

It was a cylinder music box. Swiss, almost certainly. Late 19th century. The case was rosewood with intricate floral marquetry—tulipwood and mother-of-pearl inlay. It was filthy, covered in a century of grime and tobacco smoke, but the craftsmanship was undeniable.

"Paillard?" Arthur asked, leaning in without touching it. "Or perhaps Mermod Frères?"

"No markings on the outside," Ms. Freeman said. "But look at the grain, Arthur. It’s gorgeous."

"The exterior is merely the skin," Arthur muttered, finally peeling off his oil-stained gloves. "The truth lies in the viscera."

He walked to the sink, scrubbed his hands for twenty seconds, dried them, and donned a fresh pair of white cotton archival gloves. He approached the box like it was a suspicious package found on a subway platform.

"What is the complaint?" he asked.

"Dead silence," she said. "I wound the key—gently, I promise!—but nothing happened. I thought about opening the glass cover to nudge the cylinder, but..."

"But you remembered the 'Grandfather Clock Incident of 2019' and decided to spare this innocent device from your reign of terror?"

Ms. Freeman smirked. "I prefer to think of it as outsourcing to a specialist."

Arthur carefully unlatched the lid. The hinges groaned—dry, rusted. He winced sympathetically. He lifted the lid.

Inside, behind a protective glass pane, lay the brass cylinder and the steel comb. It was a mechanism of breathtaking precision, currently in a state of severe neglect. The brass was tarnished black. The governor fan was coated in fuzz.

Arthur lowered his loupe and leaned in until his nose almost touched the glass.

"Observe," he whispered, pointing with a non-magnetic probe. "The mainspring barrel is fully tensioned. You wound it, but the energy has nowhere to go. It is effectively paralyzed."

"Is it broken?"

"It is seized. The grease has solidified. It’s suffering from arteriosclerosis of the gear train. Basically, its arteries are clogged with sixty-year-old whale oil."

He shifted his gaze to the comb—the row of tuned steel teeth that plucked the notes.

"And here..." Arthur’s voice dropped to a grave whisper. "Oh, dear."

"What? Is it bad?"

"Tooth number forty-two and forty-three," Arthur recited. "Fractured. Missing in action." He peered closer, tilting his head. "I see shrapnel. The tips of the teeth are lodged in the cylinder pins. Someone wound it while the damper was misaligned. It was a massacre."

He stood up, retracting the loupe. He looked at Ms. Freeman with a mixture of judgment and pity.

"This is a Level 4 restoration, Ms. Freeman. We are looking at a full disassembly. Chemical bath for the brass. Re-dampering. And I shall have to graft new steel onto the broken teeth and file them to pitch. Absolute pitch."

"Can you do it?" she asked, though she already knew the answer.

"It is a Paillard 'Sublime Harmonie'," Arthur said, his voice reverent. "To leave it in this state would be a felony."

"Excellent. I knew you couldn't resist." She patted the counter. "I need it by the 15th. I have a buyer flying in from Chicago who collects Swiss mechanics."

Arthur stiffened. "The 15th? That gives me ten days. I cannot rush the healing process, Ms. Freeman. The new lead weights on the bass teeth require twenty-four hours just to cure."

"Ten days, Arthur. Or I take it to that guy in the city who uses... what was it?"

"WD-40," Arthur shuddered, the color draining from his face. "The man is a butcher. A savage."

"Then I guess you'd better get started." She smiled, sweet as arsenic. "Put it on my tab?"

Arthur sighed. He reached for a fresh intake tag.

"Case #403. Paillard Cylinder Box. Status: Comatose. Cause of injury: Neglect and time." He tied the tag to the handle. "Your tab is becoming substantial, Ms. Freeman. I believe you owe me approximately four hundred years of labor or a very specific antique magnifying glass."

"I'm working on the glass," she said, turning to the door. "Oh, and Arthur? Don't look so grim. It's a music box, not a murder weapon."

Arthur watched her waltz out, the bell chiming behind her. He turned back to the silent, broken box.

"Every broken object is a crime scene," he murmured to the room. "And this..."

He gently ran a gloved finger over the rosewood lid.

"...this is a hostage situation."
