## Chapter 4: The Ultimatum

Arthur had moved the Paillard to the "Long-Term Care Unit"—a heavy oak table in the corner reserved for comas, full reconstructions, and crimes against mechanical humanity.

He adjusted the dust sheet over it. He had done the math. He always did the math.

Arthur had cross-referenced the serial number and the inlay pattern with the Swiss Heritage Database and three auction house archives. In its current state—silent, seized, and filthy—it was worth perhaps three thousand dollars to a brave speculator. But once Arthur was finished? Once he had re-tempered the steel teeth, chemically cleaned the brass to a soft gold, and regulated the governor fan to spin with the silence of a holding breath?

Ten thousand. Conservative estimate.

He looked at his clipboard. He estimated thirty hours of labor.
At his fixed rate of $25 an hour, the bill would come to $750.

Arthur tapped his pen against his lips. $750 for him. $7,000 profit for her.

He wasn't a greedy man. He lived simply. His overheads were low (he owned the building), and his pleasures were few (Earl Grey tea, the smell of soldering flux, and order). But he possessed a keen sense of justice. And there was a disparity in this sentencing that bothered him.

"Speak of the devil," he murmured to the empty shop.

The bell chimed G-sharp.

Ms. Freeman breezed in. She was carrying two cups of coffee from the bakery down the street—a transparent attempt at bribery.

"Morning, Arthur! I brought you a 'Red Eye'. The barista said it keeps you sharp."

"I am already sharp, Ms. Freeman. I am a scalpel." Arthur didn't move from behind the counter. He didn't accept the coffee yet. He simply laid his hands flat on the ledger.

"Well, you look... intense," she said, setting the cup down on a coaster she pulled from her pocket (she knew better than to put a paper cup directly on his zinc counter). "Did you look at the patient?"

"I have completed the preliminary forensic audit," Arthur said. "Please, sit."

He gestured to the stool on the customer side of the counter. It was bolted to the floor. Arthur didn't like furniture that wandered.

Clara sat, looking slightly wary. "This sounds serious. Is it terminal?"

"On the contrary. The prognosis is lucrative." Arthur opened a manila folder and spun it around to face her. "I have researched the comparables. Once I perform the necessary grafts on the comb and flush the arterial plaque from the mainspring, this device will likely fetch north of ten thousand dollars at the Chicago auction."

Clara’s eyes widened. She leaned in, a conspiratorial grin spreading across her face. "Ten grand? Arthur, you’re a magician."

"I am a mechanic," he corrected sharply. "Now, tell me. What did you pay for it?"

Clara hesitated. She stirred her coffee. "Does it matter?"

"Motive always matters."

"Twelve dollars," she admitted. "It was in a box with some old Tupperware."

Arthur made a small noise in his throat. A 'hmph' of vindication.

"So," Arthur said, lacing his fingers together. "You invested twelve dollars. You stand to gain nine thousand, nine hundred, and eighty-eight. Meanwhile, my fee—calculated at thirty hours of surgical labor—will be seven hundred and fifty dollars."

"That's your rate, isn't it?" Clara asked innocently. "It's on the sign."

"It is," Arthur agreed. "However, I have noticed a pattern, Ms. Freeman. Your 'tab' is currently occupying three pages of my ledger. You treat this establishment like a line of credit."

"I always pay up! Eventually."

"Eventually is not a fiscal quarter." Arthur stood up straight. "This is a capital case, Ms. Freeman. I cannot keep this patient on life support for two weeks on a promise and a cup of caffeinated bean water."

He pointed a long, steady finger at the sign behind him.

"The Ultimatum," he announced.

Clara blinked. "The what?"

"For this project, the 'Tab' is suspended. Due to the significant discrepancy between my labor and your profit margin, I am enforcing the strict letter of the law."

Arthur picked up the clipboard and read from his notes.

"Condition One: A retainer of three hundred dollars is required before I remove a single screw. This covers materials—specifically the high-carbon steel for the tooth grafts—and the initial ten hours of labor."

Clara opened her mouth to object, but Arthur raised a hand.

"Condition Two: The balance of four hundred and fifty dollars is due upon discharge. Not 'after the auction.' Not 'when the buyer writes the check.' But the moment I hand you the key."

"Arthur, I don't have seven hundred dollars liquid right now! That's why I need to sell the box!"

Arthur’s eyes narrowed. He looked over her shoulder at the sign.

"Then we refer to Clause B," Arthur said calmly.

He walked around the counter and tapped the gold lettering on the blackboard.

*If client can not pay... trade equal time in a task of his choosing.*

"Thirty hours, Ms. Freeman," Arthur said softly. "Thirty hours of labor."

Clara looked horrified. " doing what? I can't fix things. I break things. You said so yourself, I have the dexterity of a drunken crab."

"I do not need a mechanic," Arthur said. "I need a Forensic Cleaner."

He gestured to the floor. To the high shelves where the dust dared not settle but sometimes did. To the back room where hundreds of screws needed to be sorted by thread pitch and head type.

"If you cannot provide the capital," Arthur said, "then you shall provide the elbow grease. You will sort the fasteners. You will polish the brass intake railing. You will scrub the grout in the entryway with a toothbrush until it is white enough to surrender."

He leaned back against the counter, crossing his arms.

"So, the choice is yours. A check for the retainer today... or you report for duty at 0800 hours tomorrow. Dress for a crime scene."

Clara looked at Arthur. Then at the music box under the sheet. Then at her manicured nails. Then back at Arthur.

She sighed, defeated.

"I'll write the check for the retainer," she grumbled. "But I have to sell a Victorian vanity table first. Give me till Friday?"

Arthur considered this. He looked at the coffee she had brought him. He took a sip. It was, admittedly, excellent.

"Friday," Arthur conceded. "But the music box stays in the morgue until the check clears."

"You're a tyrant, Arthur Penhaligon."

"I am a restorationist, Ms. Freeman. I bring order to chaos." He offered her a very slight, very dry smile. "And your finances are chaos."
