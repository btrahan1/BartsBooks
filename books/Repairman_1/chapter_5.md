## Chapter 5: The Retainer

The bell chimed—G-sharp, punctual as ever—at 10:00 AM sharp on Friday.

Ms. Freeman didn't breeze in this time; she walked with purpose. She marched to the zinc counter and slapped a folded piece of paper onto the surface.

"Retainer," she announced. "Plus the balance. Post-dated for the pickup date, just in case you decide to hold the hostage for ransom."

Arthur picked up the check by the corner, as if preserving fingerprints. He inspected the signature, the amount, and the date. He nodded once, a sharp dip of the chin, and slid it into the lockbox beneath the register.

"Funds verified," Arthur said. "Administrative protocols satisfied."

"So," Clara said, leaning against the counter, looking toward the shrouded shape on the Long-Term Care table. "You will start today?"

Arthur paused. He adjusted his cuffs. He walked out from behind the counter, moving with that silent, gliding gait he used when approaching volatile chemicals. He stopped beside the table and rested his hand on the dust sheet.

"I had trust in you, Ms. Freeman," Arthur said quietly.

Clara blinked. "You... you started?"

"I could not leave the patient in critical condition while we haggled over bureaucracy. The corrosion on the governor fan was advancing. I performed an emergency intervention on Tuesday."

He looked at her, his expression unreadable behind his wire-rimmed glasses.

"Would you like to hear it?"

Clara straightened up. "Is it fixed?"

"It is by no means complete," Arthur warned, raising a finger. "Not if you want the full value for it. The damper felts are still uneven, and the lubrication hasn't fully seated in the mainspring barrel. She is essentially in physical therapy."

He gripped the corner of the cloth.

"But," he softened, "she will sing for you for a few moments, if you’d like to hear."

"I would," Clara whispered.

With a flourish—controlled, precise, not theatrical—Arthur whipped the cloth away.

The Paillard gleamed. It wasn't finished, Arthur knew—he could see the microscopic dullness on the control lever that still needed rouging—but to the untrained eye, the rosewood and brass were radiant.

Arthur donned a single white cotton glove. He reached for the winding key.

*Click.*
*Click.*

Just two turns. He didn't dare stress the mainspring yet.

He hovered his finger over the start lever. He looked at Clara, then flipped the switch.

The cylinder began to rotate. The pins, tiny as poppy seeds, caught the steel teeth of the comb.

The sound was instantaneous and crystalline. It wasn't just music; it was a physical vibration in the air. The notes of *The Blue Danube* spilled out, fragile and hauntingly clear. It sounded like rain on a tin roof, arranged by a mathematician.

It played for ten seconds.

Arthur watched the governor fan spin, a blur of silver. He watched the comb ripple.

Then, he saw Ms. Freeman.

Her hand was over her mouth. Her eyes were wet, swimming with tears that spilled over onto her cheeks. She wasn't looking at the box as inventory anymore. She was hearing a hundred years of silence breaking.

Arthur immediately flipped the lever.

The music stopped abruptly. The fan spun down. Silence rushed back into the shop, louder than before.

Arthur quickly, almost hurriedly, threw the cloth back over the machine. He smoothed it down, hiding the magic, restoring the clinical atmosphere. He didn't like tears. Tears were salt water. Salt water caused rust.

He turned to face her, clasping his hands behind his back to hide the fact that he was also, in his own way, moved.

"It is functional," Arthur said, his voice stiff. "But still in distress. You heard the slight hesitation on the D-sharp? The governor is fighting the friction."

Clara wiped her eyes, laughing a little through the sniffle. "It was beautiful, Arthur. I didn't hear any hesitation."

"I did," Arthur said. "And the machine did."

He walked back behind the safety of his counter.

"She will be ready, as agreed, a week from today. Do not be late, Ms. Freeman. I do not run a daycare center."

"I'll be here," she said, her voice thick with gratitude. "Thank you, Arthur."

"It is merely a transaction," Arthur lied, opening a ledger so he wouldn't have to look at her emotional face. "Good day, madam."

But as the door closed and the bell chimed, Arthur didn't look at the ledger. He looked at the covered table.

"D-sharp was a little flat," he whispered to the empty room. "We'll fix that on Monday."
