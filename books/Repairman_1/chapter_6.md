## Chapter 6: The Discharge

The week had passed in a blur of mundane triage—a vacuum cleaner with a hairball obstruction in the primary artery, a blender with a stripped drive coupling, and a grandfather clock that had simply lost the will to tick. Arthur treated them all with professional courtesy, but his soul remained in the Long-Term Care Unit.

On Thursday night, at 11:45 PM, Arthur applied the final coat of micro-crystalline wax to the rosewood case. He buffed it with a lambswool pad until the wood glowed with a deep, inner fire. He checked the governor fan one last time. It spun with the silence of a holding breath.

"Patient fully recovered," Arthur said simply to the empty shop. He draped the cloth over it, not to hide it, but to let it rest.

On Friday morning, the G-sharp chime sounded at 8:58 AM. Arthur hadn’t even unlocked the bolt, but Clara Freeman was peering through the glass, her face pressed against the window like a child at a candy store.

Arthur unlocked the door.

"Is she ready?" Clara asked, breathless, stepping inside without waiting for an invitation.

"She is discharged," Arthur said. He walked to the table. "I have regulated the speed to exactly 58 seconds per revolution. The D-sharp has been grafted and tempered. The dampers are whisper-quiet."

He pulled the cloth away.

Clara gasped. The grime of the last century was gone. The brass cylinder shone like gold bullion. The steel comb looked lethal and precise. The wood grain swirled like trapped smoke.

"Wow," Clara whispered, reaching out but stopping inches from the surface. "Arthur, it looks... it looks new."

"It looks correct," Arthur corrected gently. "It looks as it did the day it left Sainte-Croix in 1890."

*CLANG-a-lang.*

The door was shoved open with enough force to make the naval bell rattle on its mount.

Arthur’s spine stiffened. He knew that heavy-footed entry. He knew the smell of cheap cologne and polyester that followed it.

Mr. Hemmingway stood in the doorway. He was a large man who wore suits that were too shiny and ties that were too wide. He owned "Buy-4-Less," a warehouse monstrosity down the road that smelled of off-gassing plastic and despair.

"Penhaligon!" Hemmingway boomed. "I need you to look at the HVAC unit on my roof. Thing's rattling like a banshee."

"I am a restorationist, Mr. Hemmingway," Arthur said, his voice dropping ten degrees. "Not a janitor. Call a contractor."

Hemmingway waved a dismissive hand and lumbered toward the counter. His eyes landed on the Paillard music box. He squinted at it, not with appreciation, but with the dull calculation of a man who measured the world in profit margins.

"What's this gizmo?" Hemmingway sneered. He reached out a thick finger to poke the glass lid.

Arthur intercepted the motion. He didn't touch Hemmingway—he wouldn't want to contaminate his gloves—but he placed a screwdriver firmly on the counter between Hemmingway's hand and the box. A border wall.

"Do not touch the patient," Arthur said.

Hemmingway snorted. "Touchy, touchy. It's just a music box. I got a pallet of these in last week for the Christmas rush. Plastic snowflakes, plays 'Silent Night' when you push a button."

He looked at Clara, grinning broadly.

"I sell those for $49.99," he announced loudly. "He probably charged you an arm and a leg to fix that one! You got ripped off, lady. You could've bought ten of mine for whatever he charged you."

Clara bristled, her face flushing red. "This isn't—"

"Mr. Hemmingway," Arthur interrupted. His voice was very quiet. It was the quiet of a bomb squad technician cutting the red wire.

Arthur walked around the counter. He stood in front of the Paillard, shielding it from the Big Box owner's gaze.

"You sell units," Arthur said. "Units composed of injection-molded polystyrene and a microchip that costs three cents to manufacture in a factory where the sun never shines. Your 'music' is a digital file played through a speaker the size of a fingernail. It has no depth. It has no resonance. It has no soul. It is landfill in waiting."

Hemmingway rolled his eyes. "It's the same function, Penhaligon. It makes noise."

"A scream and a sonnet both make noise, Mr. Hemmingway. The distinction is in the structure."

Arthur turned to the Paillard.

"This," Arthur said, "is a mechanical orchestra. It contains three thousand pins, placed by hand under a microscope. The comb is tempered steel, tuned by a master who has been dead for a hundred years. It does not run on batteries. It runs on tension and release. It breathes."

Arthur looked at Clara. "May I?"

Clara nodded, glaring at Hemmingway.

Arthur wound the key. *Click. Click. Click.* The sound was heavy, satisfying. The sound of potential energy.

He flipped the brass lever.

The shop filled with sound. It wasn't the tinny, electronic beep of a greeting card. It was rich, layered, and complex. The bass notes hummed in the wood of the table. The high notes chimed like bells. The mechanism whirred softly, a heartbeat beneath the melody.

For a moment, the tactical cozy vibe of the shop expanded. The smell of oil and wood seemed to get stronger.

Hemmingway blinked. He opened his mouth to speak, then closed it. He shifted his weight. The sound was... annoying. It was annoying because it sounded expensive. It sounded like something he couldn't drop-ship.

The music stopped. The silence that followed was heavy.

"Your $49.99 units are designed to break in six months so the customer must return to buy another," Arthur said, turning his cold gaze back to Hemmingway. "This machine has outlived the Empire that created it. It will outlive me. And it will certainly outlive your warehouse."

Arthur picked up a lint roller and symbolically cleaned the air in front of Hemmingway.

"Now. Unless you have a pre-1960s appliance requiring forensic reconstruction, I must ask you to leave. You are disturbing the dust."

Hemmingway scoffed, adjusting his cheap tie. "You're a weirdo, Penhaligon. A total weirdo. I'm taking my business to the city."

"Please do," Arthur said. "And take your chaos with you."

Hemmingway turned and stomped out. The bell jangled angrily behind him.

Arthur waited until the vibrations ceased. He looked at Clara. She was smiling, a fierce, proud smile.

"He's an idiot," Clara said.

"He is a malfunction," Arthur corrected. "Cause of failure: total lack of imagination."

He patted the music box gently.

"Now, Ms. Freeman. Let us box this up before the air contamination sets in. I believe you have an auction to win."
