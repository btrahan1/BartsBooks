## Chapter 7: Attachment

Arthur was in the middle of a delicate operation—re-hairing a Victorian hygrometer with a single strand of human hair (donated, unwillingly, by his own head)—when the bell chimed.

It wasn't a happy chime. It was a slow, reluctant *G-sharp*.

Ms. Freeman walked in. She didn't march, and she didn't breeze. she drifted. She looked like a woman who had just left a puppy at a shelter. She leaned against the counter, staring at the empty space on the Long-Term Care table where the Paillard had rested.

Arthur lowered his tweezers. He swiveled his stool.

"The auction was yesterday," Arthur stated. "Report."

Clara sighed, a long, deflating sound. "Fifteen thousand, Arthur. I got fifteen thousand dollars for it."

Arthur raised a singular eyebrow. "Fifteen? That is... statistically significant. That is fifty percent above the high-end projection. You have achieved a windfall, Ms. Freeman."

"I suppose," she murmured, picking at a loose thread on her cardigan.

Arthur frowned. He stood up and walked to the counter. He studied her face with the intensity he usually reserved for stripped screw heads.

"The objective was achieved," Arthur said. "The asset was liquidated for maximum return. Then why the sad face?"

Clara looked up, her eyes shimmering.

"I... I just hated to let it go," she confessed. "It felt like selling a family member. A very quiet, very expensive family member."

"Attachment is a common side effect of the restoration process," Arthur said clinically, though his voice was soft. "We invest labor. We invest time. We leave a piece of ourselves in the mechanism."

"But it went to a good home, Arthur!" Clara blurted out, seemingly trying to convince herself. "The buyer... he was an older gentleman. Swiss. He didn't look at the provenance papers. He didn't look at the certificate of authenticity."

She took a shaky breath.

"He looked at it with reverence. He tore the ten-thousand-dollar check up right then and there—the amount we agreed on—and wrote me another one! He handed it to me without looking at me, Arthur! He couldn't take his eyes off of it."

Arthur nodded slowly. "He was listening with his eyes. He saw the tension in the spring. He saw the alignment of the governor."

"And then," Clara whispered, "he wound it three times. Just three. And let it play. He closed his eyes and just... stood there. I thought he was going to write me another check! I thought he was going to start crying."

"And?"

"And I bid him farewell and fled!" Clara threw her hands up. "I took the check and ran. I felt like I was abandoning a child at a boarding school."

Arthur leaned against the counter. A sense of relief washed over him. The Paillard wasn't sitting on a shelf in a hedge fund manager's office as a paperweight. It wasn't in a damp basement.

"Ms. Freeman," Arthur said, waiting for her to look at him. "You did not abandon it. You placed it in protective custody."

"You think?"

"The buyer knew to wind it only three times," Arthur pointed out. "He knew not to stress the mainspring after transport. He respected the mechanism. The patient is in competent hands. Case closed."

Clara sniffled, wiping her nose with a tissue she pulled from her sleeve. "I guess you're right. It just... the shop feels quieter today."

Arthur reached under the counter. He pulled out his invoice log.

"The silence is merely the absence of chaos," Arthur said, reverting to his professional demeanor. "Now. Regarding the final settlement."

Clara fumbled in her purse. "Right. The balance. Four hundred and fifty dollars."

She slapped a stack of crisp bills onto the counter. "I cashed the check. I figured you'd prefer cash. No paper trail."

Arthur counted the money. Precise. Crisp. He opened the register and placed the bills in the clamp.

"Account settled," Arthur said.

He hesitated. He looked at Clara, who still looked forlorn.

Arthur reached under the counter again. He produced a small, brown paper bag. It was grease-stained.

"I... acquired these earlier," Arthur said stiffly. "From the bakery. They are almond croissants. They were slightly over-baked, which gives them a structural rigidity I find pleasing. However, I cannot consume two."

He slid the bag across the zinc counter.

"Consider it a commission bonus."

Clara stared at the bag, then up at Arthur. A genuine smile broke through the melancholy.

"You're a softie, Arthur Penhaligon."

"I am tactical," Arthur corrected, turning back to his work table. "Sugar assists in shock recovery. Eat your croissant, Ms. Freeman. We have work to do. I believe you mentioned a shattered porcelain doll that looks like it crawled out of a nightmare?"

Clara laughed, taking a bite of the croissant. Flakes of pastry fell onto the pristine floor.

Arthur watched the crumbs fall. He twitched. But he didn't reach for the broom. Not yet.
