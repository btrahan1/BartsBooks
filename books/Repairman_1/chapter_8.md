## Chapter 8: Julian Vance

The bell chimed.

It was not the sharp, assertive *ding* of the postman, nor the hesitant rattle of a guilt-ridden customer. It was a fluid, controlled sound. The door had been opened with a specific velocity—fast enough to engage the striker, slow enough to dampen the reverb. It was a frequency Arthur had not cataloged before.

Arthur looked up from his microscope. Clara froze, a flake of almond pastry hovering halfway to her mouth.

A man stood in the doorway. He was in his mid-fifties, with silver hair swept back in a style that cost more than Arthur’s van. But the most arresting detail was his attire. He was wearing a tuxedo. Not a rental—Arthur could spot the polyester sheen of a rental from three blocks away. This was bespoke. Midnight blue wool, satin peak lapels, a shirt of Egyptian cotton so white it seemed to absorb the ambient light.

He looked entirely out of place in a small-town repair shop, yet he stood with the ease of a man who owned the ground beneath his feet.

The man didn't approach the counter immediately. He stood by the door, his eyes scanning the room. He didn't look at the prices on the sign. He didn't look at the shiny toaster on the display shelf.

He looked at the tools on the wall. He looked at the organization of the screw jars. And finally, his gaze drifted to the "Long-Term Care" table—specifically, to the empty rectangle of clean wood where the music box had sat for two weeks.

He nodded, a microscopic movement.

Arthur set down his tweezers. He stripped off his gloves, one finger at a time. The deduction was elementary. The timing. The attire (likely en route to a gala or the opera, diverted by an obsession). The gaze that sought the ghost of the object.

Arthur stood and smoothed his apron.

"Good afternoon, sir," Arthur said, his voice cutting through the silence. "I trust you are enjoying the Paillard?"

Clara choked on her croissant. She looked from Arthur to the man, her eyes wide.

The man in the tuxedo stopped. He turned his head slowly to face Arthur. He didn't look surprised that Arthur knew; he looked relieved. He studied Arthur—the grease on the apron, the magnification loupe still perched on his forehead, the eyes that were currently dissecting the stitch count of the man's lapels.

The stranger smiled. It was a genuine smile, warm and appreciative.

"I am definitely in the right place," the man said. His voice was a rich baritone, carrying a faint, unplaceable European accent.

He stepped forward, extending a hand that was manicured but showed the faint callus of a violin player—or perhaps a conductor—on the index finger.

"The damping," the man said, ignoring pleasantries. "The damper on the lowest bass tooth. You used felt, didn't you? But not modern synthetic felt."

"Merino wool," Arthur replied, coming around the counter to shake the hand. "Pressed high-density. Sourced from a supplier who restores piano hammers for Steinways. Synthetic felt is too harsh. It clips the resonance."

The man closed his eyes and exhaled, a sound of pure contentment.

"I knew it," the man whispered. "I told my driver, 'No machine did this. No factory refurbished this.' I could hear the fingerprints."

He opened his eyes and looked at Arthur with intense focus.

"My name is Julian Vance. And I believe, Mr. Penhaligon, that we have much to discuss."

Clara, still clutching her paper bag, looked back and forth between them. "I... I should probably go."

"Stay, Ms. Freeman," Arthur said, never breaking eye contact with Vance. "Mr. Vance isn't here for a refund."

"Refund?" Vance laughed softly. "Good heavens, no. I'm here because I have a warehouse in Zurich full of automata that have been silent for fifty years." He gestured to the shop, to the order, to the sanity of it all. "And I believe I've just found their conductor."

Arthur felt a very rare, very strange sensation in his chest. It wasn't heartburn.

It was anticipation.

"Zurich is a long commute, Mr. Vance," Arthur said evenly. "And my rate is twenty-five dollars an hour. Firm."

Vance smiled, glancing at the sign behind the counter.

"We can discuss the logistics," Vance said. "But first... do you have any tea? I missed my lunch reservation to find this place."

Arthur turned to the kettle.

"Earl Grey," Arthur said. "Steeped for exactly four minutes."

"Perfect," Vance said.

Arthur flipped the switch on the kettle. The game was afoot.

The kettle began to whistle—a low, rising F-natural that clashed terribly with the G-sharp of the door chime as it sounded again.

Arthur silenced the kettle with a flick of his wrist, but he didn't turn to the door immediately. He watched the reflection in the polished side of the kettle.

A man had entered. He was in his forties, built like a fire hydrant, wearing gray coveralls that were pressed sharp enough to cut paper. A leather tool roll was tucked under one arm. He didn't wander. He didn't gawk. He stepped onto the checkered tile, wiped his boots on the mat with precise, deliberate strokes, and stood at parade rest.

"Ah, there you are, Stevens," Vance said, not turning around, as if he had tracked the man's arrival by the displacement of air pressure in the room. "Please look around and get a feel for the shop."

Stevens nodded once. "Sir."

He didn't move his feet, but his eyes swept the room. He looked at the sorting trays. He looked at the hanging tools. He looked at the absence of dust on the skirting boards. He didn't look at the objects; he looked at the *system*.

Arthur poured the boiling water into the teapot. He watched the steam rise, swirling in the quiet shop. He didn't startle. He didn't ask who this intruder was. The data points were already connecting in his mind, forming a picture of a meticulously planned siege.

Arthur turned, placing the teapot on the counter on a cork trivet.

"You are that confident that I will agree," Arthur said, his eyes sliding from the stoic Stevens back to the tuxedoed Vance. "And so you brought the answer to my first excuse of not being able to abandon my shop for a few months."

Vance picked up a macaroon from the bag Clara had left, inspecting it with amusement.

"I am a collector, Arthur," Vance said smoothly. "And the first rule of collecting is to secure the logistics before you acquire the asset. Stevens here was the head of maintenance for the Royal Clock Museum before budget cuts rendered him... available. He is one of the few men I would trust to dust your shelves, let alone wind your clocks."

Arthur looked at Stevens. "Stevens."

"Mr. Penhaligon," Stevens replied. His voice was gravel, but polite gravel.

"The Sunbeam T-20 on the display shelf," Arthur said, gesturing with his chin. "Thoughts?"

Stevens shifted his gaze to the toaster. He didn't walk over to it. He just looked.

"Notorious for the bi-metal expansion screw drifting out of spec," Stevens said flatly. "And if you don't clean the crumb tray daily, the carbon buildup changes the radiant heat profile, fooling the sensor. Fine piece of kit, but needy."

Arthur’s expression didn't change, but a very small muscle in his jaw relaxed. The assessment was accurate. Crucially, Stevens had identified the *liability* of the machine, not just its beauty. He understood the crime scene.

Arthur looked back at Vance. The man in the tuxedo was smiling over the rim of his teacup. He had checkmated Arthur before the game had even officially begun. He had removed the obstacle of duty.

Arthur sighed, a sound that was half-annoyance, half-admiration. He picked up his own cup of tea.

"Well played, Mr. Vance," Arthur murmured, raising his cup in a toast. "Well played."

"Now, first things first."

Vance reached into the inner pocket of his tuxedo—a pocket that likely contained a silk handkerchief and a ticket to an opera box—and withdrew a thick, cream-colored envelope. He slid it across the zinc counter. It moved with the heavy friction of high-quality linen paper.

Arthur stared at it. He didn't touch it. He treated the envelope not as a gift, but as a variable in an equation.

His eyes narrowed slightly behind his glasses. The gears in his mind, usually reserved for gear ratios and voltage drops, began to spin on a new problem.

*Zurich. A large collection. Initial assessment and triage phase.*

"Four months," Arthur murmured, his gaze unfocused.

*Sixteen weeks. Standard billable hours, deducting the mandatory thirty-minute unbilled lunch break and two fifteen-minute tea intervals, results in a thirty-seven-point-five hour work week. Precision is key. One does not bill for tea time.*

*Sixteen weeks times thirty-seven point five hours equals six hundred hours exactly.*

*Six hundred hours times the posted rate of twenty-five dollars.*

Arthur looked up. The corner of his mouth twitched—the microscopic equivalent of a grin. He was enjoying this. It was a test of calibration.

"$15,000, I presume?" Arthur asked dryly.

Vance’s smile widened, stretching into a look of genuine delight. He clapped his hands together once, a sharp sound that made Stevens blink in the corner.

"That’s just the base pay, but exactly right, Arthur, exactly right!" Vance chuckled, shaking his head. "I was told you were fastidious, but your reputation does not do you justice. Most men would have rounded up to forty hours."

"I do not round, Mr. Vance," Arthur said, finally picking up the envelope. "Rounding is a slippery slope that ends in chaos and wobbly tables."

"Of course," Vance agreed. "The envelope also contains a travel stipend, a per diem for lodging—though I have a guest house prepared on the estate, should you choose to use it—and a discretionary fund for tools. Stevens will handle your local overhead here. His salary is already covered."

Arthur weighed the envelope in his hand. It was heavy. It felt like opportunity. It felt like a very large, very complex puzzle waiting to be solved.

He looked over at Stevens, who was currently inspecting the bristles of a broom with professional interest.

"Stevens," Arthur called out.

"Sir?" Stevens snapped to attention.

"The Shop vacuum," Arthur said. "The filter must be tapped out against the rear brick wall exactly three times after every use. Not two. Not four. Three."

"Understood," Stevens said, nodding. "To dislodge the particulate without damaging the pleats. Standard procedure."

Arthur turned back to Vance. He tucked the envelope into his apron pocket, right next to his favorite micrometer.

"When do we leave?"

Vance checked a pocket watch—a Patek Philippe, Arthur noted, likely a 1920s cushion case.

"The plane is fueling at the county airstrip. We leave in an hour."

Arthur untied his apron. He folded it into a perfect square. He placed it on the counter.

"I need to pack my tweezers," Arthur said. "And my tea."
