## Chapter 9: The Flight to Zurich

The private jet was a Gulfstream G650, but to Arthur, it was simply a pressurized tube with excellent sound dampening. The cabin smelled of conditioned leather and fresh orchids. Arthur sat in a captain's chair that swiveled with suspicious ease, sipping a cup of tea that was, admittedly, superior to his own shop blend.

Across the burl-wood table, Julian Vance watched him. The smile hadn't left his face since takeoff. It was the look of a big-game hunter who had just netted a snow leopard.

Arthur set his cup down in the saucer with a soft *clink*.

"There's more, isn't there?" Arthur asked.

Vance didn't speak. He just grinned—a wide, Cheshire Cat expression—and gestured with an open hand for Arthur to proceed. *Show your work,* the gesture said.

Arthur leaned back, steepeling his fingers. He closed his eyes for a moment, shutting out the luxury, focusing on the mechanism of the situation. Why him? Why the elaborate extraction? Stevens. The jet. The immediate payment.

Arthur was skilled, yes. He was perhaps one of the best forensic reconstructionists in the field. But he was not unique. There were old men in dusty workshops in Vienna and Kyoto who could do what he did. Perhaps not as quickly, perhaps not with the same crime-scene flair, but the result would be the same.

Vance didn't just want a mechanic. He wanted *The Repairman*.

"You have friends," Arthur said slowly, his eyes opening to pin Vance to his seat. "Very rich friends."

Vance nodded once.

"They have expensive stuff," Arthur continued, the gears whirring. "Lots of expensive stuff. Collections. Vaults. Things that haven't seen the light of day since the Treaty of Versailles."

"Go on," Vance whispered.

"But rich people—people at your altitude—don't trade in currency. Currency is vulgar. It's infinite." Arthur tapped the table. "They trade in favors. Access. Exclusivity."

Vance was practically vibrating with delight. He looked like a child watching a magician guess his card.

"So," Arthur deduced, "you want me to fix some of the items in your warehouse. The flashiest ones. The most hopeless cases. Which you will then show off to your friends at dinner parties."

"Demonstrations," Vance corrected softly.

"They will be amazed by the restoration," Arthur went on, ignoring him. "They will ask who performed the miracle. And you will tell them you have a man. An exclusive man. A man who cannot be hired, only... borrowed."

Vance was smiling so wide Arthur thought he might pull a muscle. He took a sip of champagne to hide it, but his eyes danced.

"They have nice things," Arthur said, his voice dropping as he closed in on the truth. "Things that are too large, or too fragile, or too secret to be shipped to a shop. Things in their homes. Castles. Penthouses."

Arthur leaned forward.

"I assume the first one is a Grand Piano, am I right?"

Vance choked on his champagne. He set the glass down hurriedly, coughing, wiping his mouth with a linen napkin. He looked at Arthur with something approaching awe.

"How?" Vance wheezed. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Deduction," Arthur said simply. "You have the callus of a musician on your left index finger, but you have no corresponding mark on your neck from a violin chin rest. So, you play a stringed instrument that sits on the floor. Cello, perhaps. But your suit... the right shoulder has a faint, almost invisible wear pattern consistent with the repeated motion of a heavy arm extension. Conducting? No. Piano."

Arthur pointed to Vance's hands.

"But more importantly, your friends. You mentioned Zurich. The humidity in the Swiss valleys is notorious. A Steinway or a Bösendorfer kept in a climate-controlled vault is fine. But one kept in a drafty chateau? The soundboard cracks. The action swells. It becomes a crime scene."

Vance leaned back, laughing. A full, belly laugh that shook the table.

"It is a Pleyel," Vance admitted, wiping his eyes. "Double grand. 1898. It belongs to a... let's call him a Duke. It hasn't held a tune since the Cold War. He has fired five technicians. He says it is haunted."

"It is not haunted," Arthur said, reaching for a macaroon from the silver platter. "It is merely neglected. The pin block has likely delaminated."

"And if you fix it?" Vance asked, his eyes gleaming. "If you make it sing?"

"Then the Duke owes you a favor," Arthur concluded. "A very large favor."

"Arthur," Vance said, raising his glass again. "I think this is going to be the beginning of a beautiful friendship."

Arthur took a bite of the macaroon. It was fresh. The texture was perfect.

"Perhaps," Arthur said. "But my rate for house calls is thirty-five dollars an hour. Plus tea."

Arthur paused, the macaroon halfway to his mouth. He blinked.

"I beg your pardon?"

"No," Vance said simply. He swirled the golden liquid in his glass. "Your house call rate is not thirty-five dollars an hour."

He leaned forward, his face serious for the first time since they had boarded.

"Your house call rate is one hundred and thirty-five dollars an hour. No exceptions."

Arthur lowered the macaroon. He frowned. This violated the logic of the market. It violated the Sign.

"That is... extortionate," Arthur said. "The labor value does not increase simply because the geography changes. A screw is a screw, whether it is in a toaster in Ohio or a piano in Zurich."

"To you, yes," Vance corrected. "But not to them."

Vance gestured out the window at the clouds below, encompassing the world of the ultra-wealthy.

"The people I deal with wouldn't even deign to pay less. It would be unseemly. To them, price is a proxy for quality. If I tell the Duke that I have flown in a specialist from America who charges thirty-five dollars an hour, he will send you to fix the servants' microwave."

Vance chuckled dryly.

"They pay the plumber thirty-five dollars an hour. For you? The man who resurrects the dead? The man who treats rust like a murder suspect? It must be one hundred and thirty-five. Anything less, and they won't trust you."

He looked at Arthur, waiting for the logic to click.

"It is a filter, Arthur. High price filters out the noise. It ensures that when you speak, they listen. When you say 'do not touch,' they keep their hands in their pockets."

Arthur processed this. He didn't like it. It felt messy. It felt inflated. But... looking at it forensically, Vance was describing a psychological mechanism. The price tag was not for the labor; it was a calibration tool for the client's behavior.

If they paid a premium, they would value the repair. If they valued the repair, they would maintain the object.

High cost equals high compliance.

Arthur nodded slowly.

"A behavioral tariff," Arthur murmured. "I see."

"Exactly," Vance beamed. "Is that acceptable?"

Arthur took a sip of his tea. He did the math. $135 an hour. Assuming a standard restoration took forty hours... the numbers became astronomical. But he could use the excess capital. He could buy better tools. He could buy that laser welder he had been eyeing for three years. He could... perhaps... start a scholarship for young restorationists.

"It is acceptable," Arthur agreed. "On one condition."

"Name it."

"The surplus," Arthur said. "The difference between my standard rate and this... inflated rate. It must be diverted."

"Diverted where?"

"To a blind trust," Arthur said. "Dedicated to the preservation of endangered mechanical arts. Watchmaking schools. Blacksmithing apprenticeships. The restoration of public clocks."

Vance stared at him. Then, he smiled—a softer, more genuine smile than before.

"Done," Vance said. "You are a strange man, Arthur Penhaligon."

"I am a repairman," Arthur said, finishing his macaroon. "Now. Tell me about the Pleyel's damper rail. Is it felt or leather?"
