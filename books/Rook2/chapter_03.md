# Chapter 3: The Plateau

The transition was not a violent lurch or a dizzying fall. It was a
single, smooth, silent step from one reality to another. One moment, the
cold, sterile air of the hangar filled his lungs. The next, he was
breathing an atmosphere that was thin, cool, and tasted of dust and
something ancient, like the air in a long-sealed tomb.

As Rook stepped fully through the Portal, it shimmered and vanished
behind him, leaving him standing alone under a strange, bruised-purple
sky.

He was... disappointed.

He hadn't known what to expect. Alien jungles? Crystalline cities? A
psychic dreamscape of pure chaos? He had been prepared for anything.

But this... this wasn't it.

He stood on a high, rocky plateau overlooking a vast, desolate
landscape. Jagged, black mountains clawed at the purple twilight, their
peaks sharp and cruel. A dry, rust-colored desert stretched to the
horizon, its surface broken by clusters of strange, geometric rock
formations and the occasional, skeletal remains of what might have been
a forest, aeons ago. A single, pale, and oversized moon hung in the sky,
casting long, stark shadows.

There was no sound. Not the hum of a generator. Not the distant call of
an animal. Not even the whisper of wind. The silence was absolute, a
perfect, profound emptiness that made the silence of his death row cell
seem like a noisy marketplace.

This wasn't a new hell. It was an old one.

It was pretty much like the mountains and deserts he was intimately,
brutally familiar with from his deployments. The stark, unforgiving
terrain of northern Afghanistan. The red, dusty wastes of a forgotten
corner of Syria. The strategic calculus of high ground, cover, and
concealment was exactly the same. The sun was the wrong color and the
moon was too big, but the fundamentals of survival were identical.

It was not the least bit interesting.

He dropped into a low crouch, his eyes scanning the terrain with the
practiced, automatic efficiency of a lifelong soldier. He was not a man
awestruck by a new world. He was a predator, dropped into a new hunting
ground, and his first, and only, instinct was to assess the terrain and
identify the threats.

He saw no movement. He heard no sound. He felt no immediate psychic
pressure.

It was just him, the rocks, the dust, and the silence.

A profound, weary, and deeply familiar sense of disappointment washed
over him. He had been promised a one-way ticket off the third rock, a
final, desperate escape.

He had a terrible, sinking feeling that he had just stepped right back
onto it.

Rook remained crouched, his senses extending, peeling back the layers of
this new, disappointing reality. The silence was the first layer. The
stark, familiar terrain was the second. But beneath them, there was a
third.

And then he heard it.

It wasn't a sound. It was a feeling. A vibration at the edge of his
perception, a hum that was both in the air and inside his own skull.

The "buzz."

He hadn't noticed it initially, lost in the disappointment of the
familiar landscape. But now that he focused, it was there. An old,
unwelcome, and deeply intimate friend. It was the background static of
his own broken mind, the constant, low-grade hum of his PTSD. The
tinnitus of the soul.

But it was different.

He closed his eyes, tilting his head, analyzing the sensation with the
precision of a bomb disposal expert examining a new device. On Earth,
the buzz had been a chaotic, jagged thing. It was a cacophony. It was
the sound of bullets whizzing past his ear, the deafening *crump* of
mortar explosions, the rhythmic, gut-shaking *wump-wump-wump* of
helicopter blades. It was the screams of his men. It was the wet, final
sigh of the men he had killed. It was the sound of war, a constant,
shrieking orchestra playing a symphony of his trauma, day and night.

Here... the buzz was different.

The shrieking harmonics were gone. The violent, chaotic spikes of sound
were absent. The terrible, familiar orchestra had packed up and left.

All that remained was the baseline. The pure, clean, fundamental
frequency of his own internal damage.

It was like... the quiet, pleasant buzz you get after a couple of beers,
sitting alone on your porch on a quiet summer evening. A low, gentle,
almost peaceful hum.

It was not the frantic, blitzed, everything-is-screaming feeling you get
from mixing beer, and wine, and cheap shots on New Year's Eve in a
crowded, noisy bar, when you were just desperately trying to forget the
past.

Rook's eyes snapped open, a look of profound, world-altering revelation
on his face.

The Grey. The psychic parasites that fed on emotion. They weren't here.
But their presence, or the presence of this world, was doing something
to him. It was acting like a filter. A damper. It was stripping away the
external echoes of his trauma, the ghosts of the battlefield that
haunted him, and leaving only the quiet, manageable hum of his own core
being.

He stood up, no longer a soldier assessing terrain, but a scientist who
had just stumbled upon a miracle. He took a deep breath. For the first
time in a decade, the air in his own mind felt... clean. Quiet.

He wasn't disappointed anymore. He was fascinated. And for a man who had
given up on hope, fascination was a far more powerful, and far more
dangerous, emotion. This wasn't just another rock. This was something
new. Something that might just be worth exploring.
