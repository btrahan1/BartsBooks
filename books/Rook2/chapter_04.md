# Chapter 4: The Signal

Back in the subterranean hangar, the atmosphere was thick with a tense,
brittle silence. The mission clock on the main holographic display
glowed, marking the seconds, then the minutes, since Rook had vanished
into the portal.

Every sixty seconds, a small, green light would flash: *INCOMING
TRANSMISSION*. And every sixty seconds, the same result would fill the
screen: *DATA CORRUPTED. SIGNAL UNINTELLIGIBLE.* It was the same
chaotic, useless static they had received from every previous mission.

Director Rostova stood with her arms crossed, her knuckles white, her
gaze fixed on the screen. "Anything?" she snapped, the question directed
at the bank of technicians furiously working at their consoles.

"Negative, Director," the lead technician replied, his voice strained.
"It's the same Grey interference. We're getting his
bio-telemetry---heart rate is stable, brain activity is... well, it's
active---but the comms channel is a complete wash. Just noise."

Dr. Carter paced behind them, his face pale. "It's already started.
They're already in his head. We sent him in completely unprepared..."

Elias Vance stood apart from them, perfectly still, his hands clasped
behind his back. He was the only one in the room who wasn't showing any
outward signs of stress. He just watched the screen, and he waited.

The five-minute mark passed. The six-minute mark.

INCOMING TRANSMISSION.

"Are we getting a signal?" Eva asked, the question a raw nerve of
frustration.

The technicians were clicking away, their fingers flying across their
keyboards. One of them stopped, pulling off his headset, a look of pure,
baffled disbelief on his face.

"Uhm... yes, Director," he said, his voice hesitant. "But... something
is strange."

Rostova was at his shoulder in an instant. "What is it?"

"Well... just as we suspected, every sixty seconds, we get a
transmission," the technician explained, pointing at a clean, scrolling
line of code on his screen. "But... it's... clear? There's no
interference. No static. It's a perfect, clean, encrypted data-burst.
It's exactly what we have always wanted, and have never, ever gotten
before."

He looked up at her, his expression a mixture of triumph and utter
confusion. "I don't understand. Why is his signal a clean feed? Why
aren't the Grey interfering with his comms?" He looked at the other
technicians, who were all shaking their heads in bewilderment. "Why is
that?"

Rostova stared at the clean, beautiful, impossible line of code on the
screen. It was a miracle. A complete and total violation of everything
they knew about the other side.

And from his quiet spot by the containment ring, Vance just smiled.

He knew why.

The Grey fed on chaos. On the frantic, noisy signals of a mind in
distress. Rook's mind, for the first time in a decade, wasn't screaming.
It was quiet. He wasn't a lighthouse, broadcasting a frantic, delicious
beam of fear into the darkness.

He was a black hole. Silent. Empty. And the Grey, the predators of the
psychic storm, had absolutely no idea he was even there.

The lead technician's voice rose with excitement, cutting through the
hangar's tense atmosphere. "Director! We're not just getting a clean
signal! We are getting a map!"

He pointed to the main holographic display. The chaotic, useless static
that had filled the screen for months flickered and resolved into a new,
breathtaking image. It was a three-dimensional, topographical map,
rendered in stunning, razor-sharp detail. It showed the high plateau,
the jagged mountain range, the rust-colored desert. It was a perfect,
real-time cartographical survey of Rook's immediate surroundings.

"Impossible," Rostova breathed, stepping closer to the display. "The
recon drones could never maintain a mapping laser for more than a few
seconds before the Grey corrupted the signal. How are we getting a map?
And look at the detail!"

She leaned in, her eyes tracing the lines of the terrain. The map wasn't
just showing geography. It was updating with atmospheric data,
mineralogical scans, and faint, shimmering lines that indicated
potential sources of water or radiation. It was the most comprehensive
piece of intelligence they had ever received from the other side.

Vance watched, his small smile never wavering. Rook wasn't using a
mapping laser. He was simply *looking*. His hyper-aware,
pattern-recognizing mind was absorbing the entire landscape, and the
comms unit, slaved to his bio-telemetry, was translating his perfect,
eidetic observations directly into data. He wasn't mapping the world; he
*was* the map.

"He's giving us a full environmental workup," the technician said in
awe. "At this rate, we'll have the entire quadrant mapped in a few
hours."

Rostova's mind, a cold, pragmatic engine, kicked back into gear. The
shock and awe were gone, replaced by the immediate, driving need to
exploit this unprecedented opportunity.

"Try sending an update," she commanded, her voice sharp and
authoritative once more. "I want to see if we can communicate both ways.
If his signal is clean, maybe ours can get through too."

"What should we send, Director?" a technician asked.

"Send him the last known coordinates of the survivor's enclave,
Fortitude. Mission objective update." She then turned to the lead
technician, her eyes gleaming with a new, aggressive strategy. "And
change the transmission variable on his comms. I want a data-burst every
thirty seconds, not every sixty. Double the intel flow."

She barked the final, decisive order.

"Do it now!"

The technicians scrambled to obey, their fingers flying across their
consoles. A data-burst was compiled---a tight, encrypted packet
containing the coordinates for Fortitude---and sent hurtling through the
shimmering veil of the portal. On the main display, a timer began to
count down from thirty seconds.

Vance just smiled, a private, knowing expression. He watched Rostova,
the brilliant director, trying to manipulate the technology, trying to
control the data flow. She was a master of the machine, but she didn't
understand the ghost running it.

*It's not the signal,* Vance thought, a deep, academic amusement running
through him. *It's not the program. It's Rook.*

He knew how the bio-comms worked. They translated the wearer's sensory
input and cognitive patterns into data. For a normal, panicked soldier,
that meant a chaotic, useless flood of fear and confusion. But for
Rook...

*The way HE organizes data. The pattern recognition. The quantization
and organization of information.* Rook's mind wasn't just observing; it
was actively, instinctively, and with superhuman efficiency, processing
the world into a coherent, transmittable format.

*He's basically memorizing what he sees... his way... in the form of a
perfect, three-dimensional map.* Vance mused. *Poor bastard. He's
helping us, and he probably doesn't even know it. The comms unit is just
a passive instrument, translating his thoughts. He doesn't even know we
have a way to track him.*

And then Vance's smile faltered for a fraction of a second. A new, more
chilling, and far more likely thought occurred to him. He replayed the
moment in his mind: Rook, sitting on the bed, absorbing the twenty-page
report in sixty seconds. The name of the comms unit, its model number,
its power source, its data-burst frequency---it was all in there, a
single, minor line of technical specifications.

Wait...

Vance's amusement was suddenly replaced by a profound, and deeply
respectful, sense of being outmaneuvered.

...of course he knows. Who was I kidding?

Rook knew exactly what the comms unit was doing. He knew they were
getting his signal. He knew they were seeing what he was seeing. He
wasn't just *passively* transmitting a map. He was *actively,
deliberately, and with painstaking precision*, building it for them. He
was holding up his end of a bargain that had never even been spoken.

He was giving them the intelligence they needed to monitor the mission.

And he was doing it because it was the most efficient way to be left the
hell alone.

Vance's smile returned, but it was different now. It was no longer the
smile of a puppet master watching his plan unfold. It was the smile of a
grandmaster who has just realized the pawn he's been playing with is, in
fact, another king.

The thirty-second timer on the main display reached zero. A green light
flashed: *INCOMING TRANSMISSION*.

"Clean feed, Director," the lead technician reported, a note of routine
entering his voice. "New mapping data received."

"And our outbound signal?" Rostova demanded. "Any acknowledgment?"

"Negative, Director. No return receipt. It's still just a one-way---"

The technician stopped mid-sentence. He stared at his console, his eyes
widening. "Wait..."

"What is it, corporal?" Rostova snapped.

"The transmission variable," the technician stammered, pointing at a
line of code that was now flashing a bright, impossible red. "I... I
didn't even do anything. It changed by itself."

"Changed to what?"

"It's transmitting... wait, that's not possible," he breathed, his voice
a mixture of awe and terror. "It's transmitting in real-time."

The entire bank of technicians froze. Real-time transmission across the
dimensional barrier was a theoretical impossibility. The energy cost,
the data compression required... it was beyond anything they had ever
conceived.

"He's overriding the protocols," Vance murmured, his voice a low,
fascinated whisper. "He's not just using the comms unit. He's rewriting
its source code on the fly."

"Oh shit..." the lead technician whispered, his face pale. "He's not
just sending data. Director... he's speaking to us."

Rostova was at his side in an instant. "On screen! Now!"

"It's not a voice signal, ma'am," the tech explained, his hands flying
across the keyboard. "It's a direct-thought-to-text transmission. No
encryption. It's... addressed to Dr. Carter."

He looked up at her, a silent question in his eyes.

"Put it on the main speakers," Rostova commanded, her voice tight. "The
whole room. Now."
