# Chapter 6: The Survivors


The hangar became a new kind of command center. Rostova, Vance, and
Carter stood before the main holographic display, a silent, stunned
triumvirate. The technicians, who a few hours ago had been in control of
the flow of information, were now reduced to the role of frantic
scribes, desperately trying to log and analyze the sporadic, brilliant,
and completely unpredictable bursts of data that Rook deigned to send
them.

Every five minutes, exactly, a single, green dot would pulse on the
bio-telemetry screen. *Heartbeat: Stable.* It was a metronome, a
countdown, a constant, quiet reminder that he was alive, he was in
control, and he was working.

For two hours, they received nothing else. Twenty-four heartbeats.
Twenty-four silent indicators that the most dangerous man they had ever
known was moving, unseen and unheard, through a world that had consumed
everyone else.

Rostova was a coiled spring of frustration. "What is he doing? Where is
he going? Why isn't he sending any mapping data?"

"He's hunting," Vance said quietly, his eyes fixed on the pulsing green
dot. "He has the coordinates for Fortitude. He's moving toward them. But
he's not taking the direct route. He's... flowing. Following the path of
least resistance through the psychic environment he's mapping in his
head."

Just as the twenty-fifth pulse was due, the main screen flickered again.
A new text-to-voice transmission began, Rook's synthetic, deadpan voice
once more filling the hangar.

"**FIRST SURVIVOR TEAM FOUND,**" the voice stated, with no preamble.

A wave of relief and excitement washed through the room. They found
them!

"**ALL EIGHT MEMBERS ALIVE,**" the voice continued. "**ALL IN DECENT
PHYSICAL CONDITION. MINOR MALNUTRITION AND DEHYDRATION. NO IMMEDIATE
LIFE-THREATENING INJURIES.**"

Dr. Carter let out a sob of pure, unadulterated joy. "He did it! He
found them! They're alive!"

But the message wasn't finished. There was a slight pause, and then the
final, chilling, and all-too-familiar diagnosis came through, delivered
with the cold, detached clarity of a man who knew the symptoms better
than anyone.

"**ALL EIGHT MEMBERS ALSO SHOWING EXTREME SIGNS OF ADVANCED PARANOIA,
HALLUCINATIONS, AND COMPLEX POST-TRAUMATIC STRESS. THEY ARE
COMBAT-INEFFECTIVE. THEY ARE A LIABILITY.**"

There was another pause, longer this time. Everyone in the hangar held
their breath, waiting for the final, terrible part of the report. What
would a man like Rook, a man who saw the world in terms of threats and
assets, do with eight liabilities?

The next transmission was not a report. It was a request. And it was not
addressed to Rostova, or to Vance.

"**CARTER. I NEED A CRASH COURSE. FAST. HOW DO YOU TALK A MAN OFF A
PSYCHIC LEDGE WHEN HE THINKS THE LEDGE IS REAL?**"

Dr. Carter stared at the words on the screen, his heart pounding. It
wasn't a request for a sedative protocol or a restraint technique. It
was a request for *therapy*. A desperate, real-time, in-the-field,
"how-to" on psychological intervention. Rook, the man who had rejected
his help with a sneer, was now asking for the one tool Carter was
uniquely qualified to provide.

"Get me on the comms," Carter said to Vance, his voice shaking with a
sudden, fierce urgency. "Now."

Vance nodded to the lead technician. "Open a channel for Dr. Carter.
Text-to-voice. Go."

Carter grabbed the microphone, his mind racing. He had seconds to
distill a decade of clinical experience into a single, actionable piece
of advice for a man who was not a therapist, in an environment he
couldn't possibly imagine. How do you teach empathy to a nihilist?

"Rook," Carter began, his voice clear and steady, pouring all his
professional authority into the words. "This is Carter. Listen to me.
You can't reason with them. Their reality is not your reality right now.
The Grey have them trapped in a feedback loop of their own worst
memories."

He paused, letting the thirty-second delay tick by, picturing Rook
crouched in the alien dust, surrounded by eight broken soldiers.

"They don't need a soldier," Carter continued, his voice resonating with
a passionate, desperate sincerity. "They need an anchor. They need one,
single, simple, and irrefutably true thing to hold onto in the storm of
their own minds. Something real. Something that cuts through the
hallucinations."

He was trying to think, what would work? What could Rook possibly use?
He didn't have medication. He didn't have equipment. He only had what
was in the file, the file Rook already knew by heart...

And then it hit him. The answer was in the psych profiles.

"An anchor!" Carter's voice rose with the force of his revelation. "He
needs an anchor! It has to be something personal! Something from their
lives before this hell! A name, a place, a memory!" He was almost
shouting into the mic now, hoping the intensity of his words would carry
across the void. "Like your sister, Rook! Her name was your anchor! It's
in their files! Whoever the ranking officer is, you find them, you read
their file in the packet I know you have memorized!"

He leaned into the microphone, his final instruction a plea, a prayer,
and a clinical command all in one.

"Find their Eleanor, Rook! Find their anchor and hold on! It's the only
way to pull them back!"

The hangar waited in an agony of silence. Carter's message had been
sent. Now there was nothing to do but watch the five-minute heartbeat
pulse on the screen and pray. One pulse. Two. Three. Each green dot was
a sign that Rook was still alive, but it gave them no information about
the eight broken soldiers he was with.

Rostova paced like a caged wolf. Vance stood, as always, a still, grey
statue of quiet observation. Carter stared at the comms panel, his hands
clasped so tightly his knuckles were white. He had just given
therapeutic advice to a man with an immeasurable IQ and zero empathy. It
was like handing a scalpel to a butcher and hoping he'd perform
microsurgery.

Almost exactly one hour later, the speakers crackled to life again. It
was Rook's synthetic, text-to-voice transmission, as deadpan and devoid
of emotion as ever.

"**NOT BAD, CARTER,**" the voice stated. "**NOT BAD.**"

A wave of dizzying relief washed over the doctor.

"**EIGHT ANCHORS LATER,**" the transmission continued, "**YOUR SURVIVORS
ARE NON-CATATONIC. THEY HAVE STOPPED TRYING TO EAT THE ROCKS AND SHOOT
AT THEIR OWN SHADOWS. I WOULD CLASSIFY THEM AS MERELY** '**HIGHLY
DYSFUNCTIONAL**' **NOW, AS OPPOSED TO** '**ACTIVELY SUICIDAL**'**.**"

Carter let out a shaky breath. He had done it. Or rather, Rook had. In
the space of an hour, a man with no training had successfully performed
eight simultaneous psychological interventions in the most hostile
therapeutic environment imaginable. The thought was both terrifying and
magnificent.

"**THEY ARE STILL IN BAD SHAPE,**" the voice continued, a cold dose of
reality. "**UNFIT FOR DUTY. UNFIT FOR SURVIVAL OUT HERE. I GAVE THEM THE
SAFE-ROUTE MAP I CREATED ON MY WAY IN. IT IS PROGRAMMED INTO THEIR
NAV-UNITS. IT WILL GUIDE THEM AROUND THE WORST OF THE GREY'S NESTING
GROUNDS.**"

Rostova stepped forward. "Can you escort them? Riley, your orders are
to---"

She was cut off by the next line of the transmission, as if Rook had
anticipated her command and was preemptively rejecting it.

"**I AM NOT ESCORTING THEM. THEY ARE A LIABILITY AND WILL COMPROMISE MY
STEALTH PROFILE. THEY ARE ON THEIR OWN.**"

Before Rostova could explode, the final part of the message came
through, a piece of cold, logistical data.

"**THEIR NAV-UNITS ARE ON LOW-POWER, PASSIVE MODE. YOU WON'T BE ABLE TO
PICK UP THEIR SIGNAL UNTIL THEY ARE APPROXIMATELY ONE HUNDRED METERS
FROM THE PORTAL'S EVENT HORIZON. BE READY.**"

There was a pause, and then the final, chillingly precise calculation.

"**IN ABOUT... TWO HOURS.**"

The transmission ended. The hangar was silent once more, save for the
steady, five-minute pulse of Rook's lonely heartbeat, already moving
away from the survivors' last known location, heading deeper into the
grey, silent world. He had saved them. And he had abandoned them. All in
the same, ruthlessly efficient, and perfectly logical breath.

The next two hours were the longest of Eva Rostova's life. They were a
slow-motion torment of watching a single, green dot pulse its lonely
rhythm on one screen, while staring at another, completely blank screen,
waiting for eight more dots to appear. It was an exercise in pure,
helpless faith in the calculations of a man she had called an imbecile.

She had teams of special forces operators suited up and on standby, a
medical unit prepped for mass casualties, and a line of open
communication to the Joint Chiefs. The entire multi-trillion-dollar
operation was on a knife's edge, waiting for a blip on a screen.

And then, after an eternity that the mission clock insisted was only one
hour and fifty-eight minutes, it happened.

"Contact!" a technician yelled. "Multiple signals, bearing zero-zero-one
from the gate aperture! One hundred meters and closing!"

On the blank screen, eight new dots appeared, faint at first, then
growing stronger as they stumbled out of the signal-dampening effect of
the portal. They were moving slowly, erratically, but they were moving.

Rostova was about to bark an order to her tac-team---"Breach team, on my
mark!"---when she was stopped by a new, unexpected movement.

Elias Vance, the quiet, grey analyst, was striding toward the
ready-room, already shrugging off his suit jacket.

"What in the hell are you doing, Vance!?" Rostova demanded, her voice
sharp with disbelief.

Vance didn't stop. He began strapping on the lightweight, flexible armor
of a field agent. "I'm going to get them," he said, his voice as calm
and matter-of-fact as if he were going to get a cup of coffee.

"That's a job for the operators!" she shot back. "You're a non-combatant
analyst!"

"And they are eight psychologically shattered soldiers who have just
spent two hours walking through a psychic minefield," Vance countered,
his voice never rising. He picked up a helmet, a specialized piece of
gear with a fine, silver mesh integrated into the visor. "The first face
they see needs to be a calm, welcoming one, not a squad of faceless
operators pointing rifles at them. Give me the Faraday Cage helmet. It
will shield my own mind from the residual Grey resonance. I can last for
ten minutes, max. That's more than enough time."

He turned to face her, his expression a mask of quiet, unshakeable
resolve. "I am bringing our men home, Director."

He then did something she had never seen him do. He asserted his
authority.

"And yes," he said, his voice dropping to a low, non-negotiable tone, "I
am taking the credit. Officially, I will have heroically breached the
portal alone and guided the lost soldiers back to safety. Rook doesn't
exist, remember?" He held her gaze, a silent, powerful challenge.

Rostova stared at him, at this quiet, unassuming man who had just
revealed a core of pure, cold steel. He wasn't just managing the mission
anymore. He was managing the *narrative*. He was protecting his asset,
protecting the secret, and in his own quiet, brilliant way, winning the
political game.

Vance turned to leave, then paused, as if a final, minor administrative
detail had just occurred to him. He looked over his shoulder at her.

"He doesn't want the credit anyway," he said. "But... we are doubling
his sister's endowment. Effective immediately. That part is
non-negotiable."

Without waiting for her reply, Elias Vance, the borderline genius, the
man who had set this entire, insane plan in motion, strode toward the
shimmering, silver veil to go play the hero.
