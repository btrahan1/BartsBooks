# Chapter 4: The Color of Bruises

The settlement on the horizon resolved itself in Jin-woo's vision not as
a collection of buildings, but as a sprawling, complex bruise on the
landscape. The tranquil greens and blues of the forest gave way to a
chaotic jumble of muted, unhappy Hues. He saw the dull, persistent grey
of fear, the muddy brown of toil and poverty, and over it all, a greasy,
arrogant slick of crimson. It was the color of a festering wound, the
signature of an infection.

The whispering road became a street, and the echoes of the past were
drowned out by the noise of the present. But it was not the bustling
sound of a healthy town. The Scribe's Insight perceived the emotional
texture beneath the surface noise: the anxious beat of a merchant's
heart as he guarded his wares, the quiet despair of a woman staring at a
handful of coins, the simmering resentment of a young man clenching his
fists in a darkened alley. This place was not living; it was surviving.

The vibrant violet trail of the child's grief led him to the heart of
the town, to a small, dusty square. He saw the source immediately. A
small family---a man, a woman, and a little girl with tear-streaked
cheeks---stood before a woodcarver's stall. The ground around them was
littered with shattered carvings---splintered animals and broken
figures. The violet Hue of their sorrow pulsed from them in waves, so
thick it was almost suffocating.

Facing them were three men. They were large, slovenly, and carried
themselves with the swagger of dogs who know they are the only wolves in
the yard.

Jin-woo's Insight laid them bare, and what he saw was not true power,
but a crude mockery of it. Their Ki Cores were not the clear, stable
spheres of a trained practitioner. They were like lumps of dirty,
unrefined iron, glowing with a forced, unstable energy. They knew the
basics of Ki---enough to channel it into their fists to make them
stronger, enough to project an aura of intimidation---but there was no
art to it, no understanding. They used their Ki like a club, not a
rapier. Their Hues were a nauseating swirl of arrogant red and a deep,
cruel black.

The leader, a man with a jagged scar across his lip, kicked a splintered
wooden horse at the woodcarver's feet. "The Toll Takers require their
due, old man. This is our town. Everything that happens in it, happens
because we allow it."

The woodcarver, a man whose Ki was a dim, flickering gold of a gentle
spirit, bowed his head. "We have nothing left to give. You took our
earnings this morning."

The scarred man laughed, a sound that was a jagged tear in the town's
fragile peace. "Then you'll give us your fear instead." He looked at the
crying little girl, the source of the violet grief. "Maybe if you
learned to control your brat, she wouldn't drop things."

That was the key. The trigger. Jin-woo's Insight connected the threads
in an instant: the thug shoving past the child, the precious wooden bird
knocked from her hand, the eruption of a sorrow so profound it had left
a scar on the world.

And then it happened.

Something new and violent flared within Jin-woo. It was a sensation
utterly alien to his twenty years of placid meditation and his newfound
state of universal perception. It was hot, sharp, and utterly consuming.

It was rage.

The perfectly ordered symphony of the Scribe's Insight shattered into
screeching, discordant static. The clear Hues of the world around him
wavered and distorted as if seen through a heat haze. The still,
mountain lake of his own Ki was instantly flash-boiled, a torrent of
scalding energy flooding his meridians. For a horrifying second, he lost
control.

His mind, the perfect, analytical engine, flashed a series of phantom
characters, a concept for which he had no words, only a feeling:

*INTERNAL ERROR. PERCEPTION CORRUPTED BY EMOTIVE LOGIC FAULT.*

The Way of the Brush offered a solution: he could see the deep-seated
insecurity in the scarred man's Ki, the flaw in his meridian born of a
lifetime of being made to feel weak. He could speak a single word,
expose that weakness, and shatter his ego without a single blow.

The Way of the Fist offered a different solution, one that was brutally
simple and screamed for release. He saw a dozen critical weaknesses in
the man's stance. A single, precise strike---a flick of the wrist to the
nerve cluster behind his knee---and he would be crippled. The anger,
this new and terrible god inside him, demanded it.

The scarred man raised a hand, his Ki flaring a brutish red, preparing
to strike the woodcarver. The woman gasped, pulling her child close.

The choice had to be made.

Jin-woo stepped out from the edge of the square. The world was still a
distorted, shimmering mess to his senses, his own anger a roaring fire
he was desperately trying to contain. He could not find the perfect,
placid tone he used on the forest boar. Peace was a language these men
would not understand.

So he would have to speak theirs.

He held up the small wooden bird.

He did not channel peace. He did not channel calm. He took the white-hot
rage that was threatening to consume him, and with the entirety of his
will, he focused it. He let it bleed into his voice, not as a shout, but
as a low, resonant hum that cut through every other sound in the square.
It was the sound of a temple bell struck by a blacksmith's hammer.

"You," Jin-woo said, his voice quiet yet carrying an impossible weight,
his eyes locked on the scarred man. "You dropped this."
