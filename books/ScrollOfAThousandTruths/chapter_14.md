# Chapter 14: The First Choice

The raiders stood stripped bare by Jin-woo's words, their internal flaws
laid out for all to see. They braced themselves for the inevitable,
grueling ordeal that must follow such a deconstruction. A day of
breaking rocks, a night of standing in a cold stream---some physical
punishment to match their internal humiliation.

Jin-woo's serene expression shifted, and a faint, disarming smile
appeared.

"But first, let us eat," he announced, his tone light and
conversational. "Learning is hard on an empty stomach, is it not?"

The men blinked. The sudden shift was a splash of cold water, completely
disrupting their expectations. Before they could process it, villagers
emerged from the surrounding buildings, carrying not weapons or tools of
labor, but simple, rustic tables and steaming bowls of savory stew and
fresh bread. The aroma filled the square, a scent of hearth and
community that was utterly alien to the raiders.

The villagers set down six small tables, four stools at each, in front
of the line of men.

"You will discuss amongst yourselves," Jin-woo instructed, his voice
calm but the command absolute. "And you will form groups of four. Sit at
a table."

He paused, letting them absorb the first part of the task before
delivering the critical clause.

"Amongst the four of you, one will be the leader. He will be responsible
for the successes and the failures of his entire group, from this moment
forward. Choose wisely."

He gestured to the food. "Once a group has chosen its leader, and that
leader has informed Kael of his responsibility, you may eat."

And then, he simply stepped back, hands clasped behind his back,
becoming an observer.

The effect was instantaneous and chaotic. These were men who lived by a
simple, brutal code: take what you want, follow the strongest, betray
before you are betrayed. Cooperation was a foreign language.

Immediately, arguments broke out. The giant Jin-woo had diagnosed as a
"swamp" slammed his fist on a table. "I lead this table! Anyone who says
otherwise deals with me!"

A wiry man shot back, his voice laced with newfound spite. "And lead us
where? Into the mud? The monk said you're wasted strength!"

Suspicion was rampant. Was this a trick? A way to identify the ambitious
ones so they could be punished? Or the weak ones so they could be
culled? They sized each other up, their gazes filled with calculation.
It was no longer a simple measure of who was strongest in a brawl.
Jin-woo's diagnoses had armed them with new, more intimate weapons
against each other. They saw the flaws he had pointed out, and they
hesitated to trust anyone.

Meanwhile, the smell of the food was a potent motivator. Hunger gnawed
at them.

Jin-woo watched, his Scribe's Insight taking in the chaotic symphony. He
saw the red flashes of anger, the murky yellow of scheming, the grey fog
of indecision. He was not looking for the strongest leader to emerge. He
was looking for the wisest group to form. He was watching to see which
of them could look past the flaws he had exposed and see the potential
that lay beside it.

At one table, a quiet, scarred man who Jin-woo had diagnosed as an
"echo" did something remarkable. He didn't try to lead. He listened. As
three others argued, he quietly pointed out that the angriest of them
was also the quickest to see danger, that the greediest was also the
best at finding resources, and that the third, while quiet, was steady.

"Maybe," the echo said, his voice hesitant but clear, "a leader isn't
the one who shouts the loudest. Maybe he's the one who knows what the
rest of us are for."

The other three stopped arguing and stared at him. After a long silence,
one of them nodded. "You. You lead. You're the only one who's actually
thinking."

The scarred man looked surprised, but he stood. He walked over to Kael,
who stood watching with a grim, knowing expression. "I am responsible
for them," he said.

Kael gave a single, sharp nod.

The man returned to his table, and his group, the first to solve the
puzzle, began to eat. The sight of them---the simple act of eating their
meal in peace---sent a shockwave of urgency through the remaining men.
The test was real. The reward was real. And they were failing.

The arguments at the other tables shifted. The shouting began to die
down, replaced by a tense, desperate negotiation. The lesson had begun.
They were not learning a form, or a stance, or a strike. They were
taking the first, agonizing step in learning how to build something
instead of just breaking it.
