# Chapter 15: The River of Choice

The stew was gone, the bread a memory. A fragile sense of accomplishment
settled over the six tables as the last bowls were scraped clean. They
had passed the first test. They had formed teams. They had eaten. It
felt simple, but for men who had only ever known a chaotic free-for-all,
it was a minor miracle.

Their respite was brief. Jin-woo, who had been observing from a
distance, stepped forward once more.

"About ten minutes from here is a river," he said, his voice cutting
through the post-meal quiet. "Take your dishes to the river and wash
them. Then bring them back."

A collective groan went through the men. Washing dishes? It was grunt
work. Demeaning. This was not the warrior's training they had
anticipated.

Jin-woo seemed to read their thoughts and let out a soft, weary sigh. "I
do not have the time to figure out each and every one of you
individually. You will work as a team, or not work at all."

He let his gaze sweep over them, and the steel returned to his voice.

"If you decide to return, I will be in this square. Only groups of four
will return. No smaller group may do so."

The implication was as clear and as cold as the river water itself. The
task was not washing dishes. The task was returning, intact. This
ten-minute walk was the first real taste of freedom, and the first real
test of the bonds they had just been forced to make. Any man could run.
But if he did, he would be damning the three others he left behind,
exiling them from this strange new sanctuary.

The newly appointed leaders looked at their men, a new weight of
responsibility settling on them. It was one thing to be chosen at a
table; it was another to march three other volatile, independent men
into the woods and be expected to bring them all back.

The journey began. The groups gathered their wooden bowls and spoons and
set off down the path, a procession of sullen, grumbling warriors forced
into a domestic chore. The ten-minute walk was a crucible of whispered
temptations.

In one group, led by the giant, a wiry man kept glancing into the deep
woods. "We could be ten miles away by nightfall," he hissed. "Free."

The giant, who a day ago would have simply broken the man's leg for his
insolence, found himself in an impossible position. He growled, "And go
where? Back to scrabbling in the dirt? Here, there's food. A roof.
And... something else." He couldn't put a name to it, but the thought of
facing the monk's quiet disappointment was, for some reason, more
terrifying than the thought of a life on the run.

In another group, two men who shared an old, bloody rivalry began to
shove each other. Their leader, the scarred "echo," stepped between
them. "Do you want to settle this?" he asked calmly. "Fine. But we all
pay the price. If you fight, we can't go back. Is your hatred for him
worth more than your own future?" The two men glared at each other, the
fire in their eyes slowly banking. For the first time, a consequence was
more immediate and more personal than the satisfaction of violence.

They reached the river, a clear, fast-flowing stream. The act of washing
the dishes was clumsy, irritating. Men who had wielded axes and swords
now fumbled with bowls in the cold water. But as they worked, a strange
thing happened. They began to cooperate. One man washed, another rinsed.
They formed small, efficient chains, their movements finding a rhythm.

The walk back was different. It was quieter. The men were lost in their
own thoughts. They had been given a clear opportunity to abandon this
new life, to shatter their fragile teams, and none had taken it. The
choice to return, a choice they all made in silent unison, was a
heavier, more binding contract than any the monk could have placed on
their souls.

They emerged from the trees and saw the town square. All six groups. All
twenty-four men. Jin-woo sat in the center, exactly as they had left
him, a statue of waiting patience. He didn't seem surprised. He didn't
seem pleased. He simply was.

As the last group entered the square, he opened his eyes. He didn't need
to count them. He could feel the twenty-four Ki signatures, frayed and
tested, but complete. They had walked through the shadow of their old
selves and had chosen to walk back into the light.

"Good," Jin-woo said, his voice resonating with a deep finality. "The
page is clean. Tomorrow, we learn the first character."
