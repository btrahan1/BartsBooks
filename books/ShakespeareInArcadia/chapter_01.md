# Chapter 1: The Stagehand’s Soliloquy

The wooden O of the Globe was more than a theater; to Dalton, it was a cathedral of the spoken word, a holy sanctuary where the grit and grime of 1600s London were washed away by the tide of iambic pentameter. The morning air within the galleries was cool and smelled of things that promised immortality: fresh sawdust, sun-warmed beeswax, and the faint, lingering scent of the lavender used to keep the tiring-house from smelling too much of the sweat-soaked costumes within.

Outside the timber walls, the Thames roiled with the filth of a hundred thousand souls, a soup of river mud and sewage that the city called its heart. But here, on the oak boards of the stage, Dalton was the master of a different domain.

He dragged a heavy broom across the stage, the rough bristles catching on the splinters of the oak. To any observer—the watermen passing by the river, the merchants setting up their stalls—he was merely Dalton. A man of thirty years with a perpetual squint and a back that carried the weight of a thousand moved stools. He was the stagehand, the man who ensured the trapdoor didn't groan like a ghost before the ghost was supposed to appear.

But in the silence before the groundlings arrived, Dalton wasn't sweeping. He was charging.

“A horse!” he whispered, his voice catching the natural acoustics of the empty theater and vibrating against the thatch-roofed galleries. “A horse! My kingdom for a horse!”

He lunged forward, the broom handle serving as a spectral lance. He wasn't clearing away the remnants of yesterday's hazelnuts and orange peels; he was on the windswept fields of Bosworth, a monarch in his final, desperate hour, surrounded by enemies and betrayed by fate. He felt the cold weight of a crown upon his brow—a crown forged of imagination and hardened by a thousand hours of eavesdropping on the tiring-house rehearsals.

“Dalton! You addlepated maggot! Move that stool and get to the cellar before I skin you!”

The shout shattered the illusion like a glass dropped on stone. It came from the wings, voiced by Dickon, the stage manager. Dickon was a man whose soul was as soot-stained as his apron, a man who saw the Globe not as a temple, but as a factory that turned timber and noise into coin.

Dalton didn't break character immediately. He withdrew the broom-lance with a slow, dignified grace, his movements deliberate and filled with a phantom nobility. He turned to face the "commoner" who had dared interrupt his soliloquy.

“My good man,” Dalton said, his voice level and resonant, dripping with a misplaced, almost tragic dignity. “One does not simply ‘move a stool’ when the fate of England hangs in the balance. The House of York is at its nadir, and you speak to me of furniture?”

Dickon stepped onto the stage, his boots thumping dully on the wood. He stared at Dalton, his mouth hanging open just wide enough to reveal a missing tooth and the yellowed remains of a breakfast he had clearly not enjoyed. “What in the devil's name are you prattling about? Move the stool, or I’ll move my boot into your backside so hard you’ll be coughing leather for a week.”

Dalton sighed, a long, weary exhalation that spoke of the heavy burden of leadership and the eternal frustration of dealing with the unrefined masses. “Your manners, kind sir, leave much to be desired. It is a sad age indeed when a man cannot find a moment of quiet reflection before the duties of the realm call him away. However, as the crown is heavy and my patience is great, I shall attend to your request with all due haste.”

He didn't just pick up the stool. He approached it as if it were the Stone of Scone. He lifted it with a flourish that made the simple three-legged piece of oak look like a sacred relic being transported to a cathedral.

“Go on then!” Dickon barked, waving a grimy hand. “And don't you be touching the costumes again! Master Burbage says his doublet smelled of stable-straw after you were ‘inspecting’ it yesterday.”

Dalton ignored the jibe. He knew that Burbage—the great Richard Burbage—was merely a vessel for the words. Dalton was the one who *felt* them. 

As he descended the stairs toward the cellar, he nearly collided with Master Alleyn, a minor actor with major ambitions and a voice that could crack a window at fifty paces. Alleyn was dressed in a flamboyant yellow doublet, his feathered cap perched at an angle that defied both gravity and good taste.

“Watch your step, you lout!” Alleyn snapped, sidestepping Dalton as if he were a pile of horse manure. “You nearly brushed against my lace! Do you have any idea what this costs? More than your family earns in a generation, I’ll wager.”

Dalton stopped and offered a bow that was, perhaps, a fraction too deep—a bow that carried a subtle, sharp edge of irony. 

“A thousand apologies, kind sir,” Dalton said, his voice smooth and courtly. “It was not my intention to offend such a… vibrant vision. Truly, your lace is a marvel of the modern age. It speaks of a soul that values the finer things, even if the heart beneath it is perhaps a bit… preoccupied with cost.”

Alleyn narrowed his eyes, his hand going to his hip. “Are you mocking me, stagehand? I’ve seen your sort before. Thinking you’re better than the boards you sweep because you’ve memorized a few lines of verse. You’re a servant, Dalton. Nothing more. Don't forget it.”

“A servant of the Muse, perhaps,” Dalton replied, his eyes shining with a quiet, stubborn light. “We all serve something, Master Alleyn. Some serve their art, and some serve their vanity. I know which master I prefer.”

Alleyn scoffed, his feathers trembling. “Waster! Get to your cellar. And make sure the candles are trimmed for the afternoon performance. If I have to perform in a dim light again, I’ll have your hide.”

Dalton watched him go, a small, sad smile playing on his lips. Alleyn was exactly what was wrong with the world. He had the words, but he didn't have the soul. He had the costume, but he didn't have the character.

He carried the stool to the cellar entrance, his pace measured. For Dalton, every moment was a performance, a rehearsal for a life he was never meant to lead. He had spent every spare copper on cheap, pirated quartos, the ink smearing on his calloused thumbs as he devoured the lines by candlelight in his cramped garret. He had spent every spare hour hiding in the shadows of the tiring-house, watching the actors' gestures, learning how a knight should stand with his weight on his back heel, how a prince should mourn with his head bowed just so, and how a villain should sneer with a curl of the upper lip.

He was a commoner by birth, a stagehand by trade, but a knight by conviction. He had forged a code of honor out of snippets of *Henry V* and *The Winter's Tale*. He believed in the sanctity of the "fair maiden," the necessity of the "just duel," and the absolute requirement of "noble speech."

As the morning sun climbed higher, casting long, slanting shadows across the stage, Dalton found himself alone in the tiring-house. It was a chaotic space, filled with the props of a hundred different worlds. There were wooden swords, papier-mâché crowns, and robes of velvet that were more patches than fabric.

He walked to a heavy wooden chest and lifted the lid. Inside lay a rapier. It wasn't a prop—it was a real blade, though a rusted and neglected one, bought from a down-on-his-luck mercenary for the price of a week's ale. Dalton didn't use it for the plays; he kept it hidden, wrapped in an old oily rag. 

He drew the blade. It didn't ring with the clear, singing tone of a master-crafted weapon. Instead, it made a dry, rasping sound, the rust flaking off like scabs. But to Dalton, it was Excalibur. It was the steel of a champion.

“Tonight,” he whispered to the blade, “we shall defend the honor of the fair Anne. We shall show that ruffian that the spirit of chivalry is not yet dead in this wretched city.”

Anne was a weaver’s daughter who lived three streets over. She was, in Dalton’s mind, the very image of Ophelia before the madness. In reality, she was a pragmatic woman who found Dalton’s constant bowing and flowery language "odd, but harmless." He had convinced himself that she was being "dishonored" by a young apprentice named Tom, simply because Tom had the audacity to speak to her without a formal introduction.

Dalton polished the rusted hilt with the hem of his tunic. He knew he wasn't a swordsman. He knew his "training" consisted of watching actors play-fight with wooden sticks. But he believed, with a ferocity that defied logic, that the "rightness" of his cause would lend strength to his arm.

“The world is a stage,” he murmured, quoting the master himself. “And I shall play the hero.”

He didn't see the irony. He didn't see that he was a man living in a fiction of his own making. He only saw the light of the moon reflected in his rusted sword, and the promise of a glory that no stagehand had ever known.

He stood there for a long time, the sword heavy in his hand, the shadows of the Globe stretching out around him like the ghosts of the characters he so dearly loved. He was ready. He was Sir Dalton. And the curtain was about to rise on his final, most tragic performance.
