# Chapter 2: The Final Performance

The London night was a shroud of ink and coal smoke, illuminated only by the flickering, sickly yellow light of the occasional lantern and the pale, distant glow of a moon that seemed too high and too clean for the streets below. Dalton walked with his shoulders back and his hand resting on the hilt of the rusted rapier concealed beneath his cloak. In his mind, he wasn't walking through a maze of filth-choked alleyways; he was pacing the battlements of Elsinore, a man of destiny approaching his reckoning.

The air smelled of stagnant water and the sharp, metallic tang of the city’s many small cruelties. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, but Dalton felt no fear. A knight, he told himself, does not fear the dark; he merely brings the light of honor into it.

He reached the alleyway behind the Boar's Head. It was a narrow, claustrophobic space, hemmed in by the soot-blackened walls of the tavern and a row of sagging tenements. At the far end, near a pile of discarded barrels and rotting refuse, stood Mistress Anne.

She was backed against the brickwork, her face a pale oval in the gloom. In front of her stood the youth, Tom. He was a barrel-chested apprentice with a face like a slapped ham and eyes that were glazed with cheap ale. He held a dirk—a jagged, ugly thing that looked like it had been hammered out of a horseshoe.

“Come now, Annie,” Tom was saying, his voice a thick, wet rasp. “Don’t be difficult. A bit of sport, that’s all. You’ve been looking at me all week, I know you have.”

“I’ve been looking at you because you’re always in the way, Tom!” Anne snapped, her voice sharp with a mix of fear and irritation. “Go home and sleep off your ale. You’re making a fool of yourself.”

Dalton stepped from the shadows. He didn't run; he walked with the measured, deliberate tread of a protagonist entering a climactic scene. He stopped ten paces from the pair and threw back his cloak, revealing the rusted hilt of his blade.

“Halt!” Dalton’s voice boomed, echoing off the narrow walls with a theatrical resonance that seemed entirely out of place in the muddy alley. 

Tom spun around, blinking in the dim light. “What the... Dalton? What are you doing here, you mad stagehand?”

Dalton drew the rusted rapier. It didn't ring; it made a dry, rasping groan as it left the scabbard, a sound like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in a century. But to Dalton, it was the sound of justice being unsheathed.

“I am Sir Dalton,” he announced, his voice steady and filled with a misplaced, almost holy conviction. “And you, sir, are an uncouth ruffian who has dared to stain the air with your vile intentions towards this fair lady. Stand aside, or prepare to meet the steel of a man who knows the true meaning of honor.”

Mistress Anne stared at him, her eyes wide. She didn't look swooning. She didn't look relieved. She looked like she had just witnessed a man try to put out a fire with a bucket of sawdust. “Dalton? From the theater? What in the name of the Saints are you doing? Put that piece of iron down before you hurt yourself!”

“Fear not, my fair lady!” Dalton cried, his gaze fixed on Tom. “I have seen this scene played a hundred times. The villain always falls, and the righteous always prevail. Your honor shall be defended as if it were the crown itself!”

Tom started to laugh—a short, sharp bark that turned into a coughing fit. “Sir Dalton? You’re the lad who sweeps the pits and moves the chairs! You’re no knight. You’re a fool in a threadbare doublet.”

“Defend yourself!” Dalton boomed, ignoring the mockery. 

He lunged. In his mind, it was a masterstroke—a swift, decisive thrust that would catch Tom off guard and send the dirk clattering into the muck. He had seen Burbage do it a dozen times in *The Merchant of Venice*. He knew the footwork, the extension, the follow-through.

In reality, the London mud was far less cooperative than the stage of the Globe. As Dalton extended his arm, his front boot hit a patch of wet cabbage and slick, rotting fish-scales. His leg went out from under him, and his heavy, rusted blade, unbalanced and poorly maintained, went wide by a foot.

He didn't recover with a graceful roll. He stumbled forward, his momentum carrying him directly into Tom’s reach. 

Tom didn't use a master's technique. He didn't need to. He simply stepped inside Dalton’s clumsy guard and drove the dirk into his side with the brute force of a man accustomed to hauling grain sacks.

The pain was not what Dalton expected. On the stage, a stabbing was followed by a dramatic gasp, a hand clutched to the chest, and perhaps a poetic final speech delivered in a dying whisper. 

This was different. It was cold. A sharp, icy intrusion that seemed to steal the very air from his lungs. It felt like a needle of frost being pushed through his ribs. Dalton’s knees hit the cobblestones with a sickening thud. The rusted rapier clattered away, disappearing into the darkness of a nearby puddle.

He looked up, his breath coming in shallow, ragged hitches. Tom was staring at him, his face pale and sober now. The ale had clearly worn off, replaced by the sudden, terrifying realization of what he had done.

“He’s... he’s bleeding, Tom!” Anne’s voice was high and panicked. She didn't stay to hold his hand or weep over his body. She looked at Dalton, and for a fleeting second, their eyes met. There was no love there. Only a deep, stinging disappointment.

“Fool,” she whispered.

She grabbed Tom’s arm, and together they bolted into the night, their footsteps echoing off the brickwork until they were swallowed by the city’s roar.

Dalton slumped against the wall, his hand pressed to the wound. He felt the warm, sticky flow of blood through his fingers, soaking into the fabric of his tunic. He looked up at the moon, which was still high and clean and utterly indifferent to the man dying in the mud below.

*“The rest,”* he wheezed, the words feeling like shards of glass in his throat, *“is silence.”*

He closed his eyes, expecting the dark. He expected the judgment of the Almighty, or perhaps a final, grand applause from the galleries of heaven. 

But the silence didn't last. 

Instead of the dark, he felt a strange, tingling sensation, as if his very soul were being pulled through a needle's eye. The smell of the alleyway—the mud, the smoke, the rotting fish—was suddenly replaced by the scent of fresh clover and morning dew. The cold pain in his side vanished, replaced by a bizarre numbness.

He opened his eyes. He wasn't in London. He wasn't in the mud. He was floating in a void of soft, white mist. 

And then, the light appeared.

A blue pane of glass materialized in the void, floating three inches from his nose. It glowed with a luminescence that defied all reason, and upon it were runes—no, letters. Letters in a script so precise they must have been carved by the hand of a master engraver.
