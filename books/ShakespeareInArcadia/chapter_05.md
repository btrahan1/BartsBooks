# Chapter 5: Slime Crusade

The road to Oakhaven, which Sam had promised was a mere hour’s stroll through the sun-dappled meadows, was currently entering its sixth hour. The sun, once a cheering herald of a new act, was now sinking toward the horizon, casting long, mocking shadows across the tall grass.

Dalton was not strolling. He was crusading.

“Death! Death, you translucent fiend!” Dalton roared, his voice cracking slightly from a half-dozen hours of theatrical shouting. 

He lunged with the 'Branch of Chivalry,' the grey oak stick whistling through the air. The target was a small, particularly lethargic Green Slime that was minding its own business beside a cluster of dandelions. With a wet *thwack*, the branch struck true, popping the red core and sending a spray of greyish sludge across Dalton’s boots.

**[ SLIME SLAIN! ]**
**[ +2 EXP ]**

Dalton leaned on his stick, wiping a bead of sweat from his brow with the back of a slime-stained hand. He looked like a man who had been through a war, though his only casualties were a few dozen blobs of jelly and his own physical stamina.

Sam was not leaning. He was sprawled on a low-hanging branch of a nearby birch tree, his tiny head buried in his hands. “That was the thirty-seventh one, Dalton. Thirty. Seven. We could have been at the tavern four hours ago. I could have been halfway through a bottle of spectral wine by now, and you could have been sleeping on something that wasn't covered in goo.”

“Master Sam,” Dalton wheezed, straightening his back with a groan that sounded like a dry gate-hinge. “A True Knight does not leave a task unfinished. To leave even a single one of these… these gelatinous usurpers to haunt the path of the innocent would be a dereliction of my duty. I am the shield of the meadow! The scourge of the squish!”

“The innocent don't come this way, Dalton! Nobody comes this way because it’s full of level-one slimes that are about as dangerous as a wet sandwich!” Sam hopped off the branch, his wings buzzing with irritable energy. “You’ve spent all day killing things that don't even have teeth. Look at your screen. Tell me it was worth it.”

Dalton summoned the blue pane of light. He did it with a flick of his wrist, a gesture he had perfected over the last five miles, though the 'System' remained entirely unimpressed by his flair.

**[ USER: DALTON ]**
**[ LEVEL: 2 ]**
**[ EXPERIENCE: 84 / 200 ]**

Dalton stared at the numbers, his expression one of polite, albeit profound, disappointment. “Eighty-four? After a day of such tireless slaughter? I have cleared a kingdom's worth of fiends, and the phantom only grants me a mere eighty-four fragments of growth?”

“They are still Level 1, Dalton, and are pathetically weak,” Sam said, landing on Dalton’s shoulder and tapping the screen with a claw. “The more you kill of the same thing, the less you get. It’s called diminishing returns. Or, in your case, ‘wasting everyone’s time.’ To progress, you must fight stronger creatures! You need wolves, or goblins, or maybe a particularly aggressive badger. Not… whatever this was.”

Dalton looked down at the pool of grey sludge at his feet. He was tired. His legs felt like lead, and his 'Branch of Chivalry' was now perpetually sticky. But he didn't look disheartened. He looked… resolute.

“Understood, my tiny, and somewhat comely friend,” Dalton said, offering a weary but genuine smile. “I see that the path to greatness is not paved with easy victories alone. But no True Knight will leave a task unfinished. This meadow is now safer for my passing, and though the gain be small, the honor is great.”

He looked toward the horizon, where the thin plume of smoke he had seen earlier was now a visible cluster of rooftops huddled against the edge of a dark, imposing forest.

“The town awaits,” Dalton declared, shouldering his stick once more. “And though the journey has just begun, and the day has been long, I find my spirit is unbowed. We shall reach Oakhaven, and we shall find the sustenance and the steel I require to face these ‘stronger creatures’ of which you speak.”

“I’m not comely,” Sam muttered, though he didn't sound entirely offended. “And I’m definitely not your friend. I’m your parole officer. Now move. The sun’s going down, and the slimes around here get… well, they don't get tougher, but they get harder to see in the dark, and I am not spending the night in a field of goo.”

The screen flickered and vanished. Dalton felt a tiny jolt of energy—the reward for his persistence—and he found the strength to keep his pace steady as they approached the outskirts of the village.

The village of Oakhaven was not the sprawling metropolis Dalton had envisioned. It was a collection of rough-hewn timber buildings and thatch roofs, surrounded by a low, jagged palisade of sharpened logs. It smelled of woodsmoke, wet earth, and the pervasive, heavy scent of livestock. It was gritty, it was functional, and to Dalton’s Elizabethan eyes, it was remarkably uncouth.

A single guard stood at the open gate. He was a man with a face like a weathered boot and a spear that looked like it had seen more use as a pig-poker than a weapon of war. He wore a padded gambeson that was stained with grease and what Dalton hoped was mud.

Dalton stopped twenty paces from the gate. He took a moment to straighten his tunic, wipe the worst of the slime from his branch, and assume a posture of dignified nobility. 

“My good man!” Dalton announced, his voice regaining its full theatrical resonance. “You see before you a traveler who has braved the perils of the meadow to seek your hospitality! I am Sir Dalton, and I have arrived!”

The guard didn't move. He didn't even look up at first. He just continued picking his teeth with a splinter of wood, his eyes focused on something far more interesting—a small beetle crawling across the toe of his boot. Finally, he shifted his gaze to the man standing in the mud with a sticky oak branch and a look of intense, misplaced pride.

“Sir who?” the guard asked, his voice a low, gravelly rumble that sounded like two millstones grinding together.

Sam, hovering invisible beside Dalton’s head, let out a long, pained groan. “Here we go. Just don't mention the slimes, Dalton. Please. Just don't mention the slimes. Tell him you’re a traveler. Tell him you’re a day-laborer. Tell him you’re a lost shepherd. Anything but ‘Sir Dalton, Scourge of the Squish.’”

Dalton, however, was already in mid-bow—a sweep of such elegance and depth that his forehead nearly touched the mud. He ignored Sam’s pleading, his focus entirely on the man he had decided was the ‘Gatekeeper of the Citadel.’

“Sir Dalton!” he repeated, rising with a flourish that sent a small droplet of slime flying from his sleeve. “Lately of the Great City of London, and now, by the grace of fate, a champion of these very meadows! I have spent the better part of the day clearing the path to your fair settlement of a gelatinous plague. I seek an audience with your lord, or perhaps a master of the local guild, that I might offer my services to your cause.”

The guard, whose name tag (if he had one) would have simply read ‘Bram,’ stopped picking his teeth. He looked at the slime on Dalton’s sleeve. He looked at the grey-stained oak branch. Then he looked at Dalton’s face—a face that radiated a level of sincere, high-minded conviction that Bram hadn't seen since the last time a tax collector tried to explain why ‘royal tithes’ were a blessing.

“Slimes?” Bram asked, a slow, bewildered smile spreading across his face. “You spent all day… killing slimes?”

“Indeed!” Dalton said, his eyes shining with the fire of a man who had done his duty. “A crusade of no small measure, I assure you. They were everywhere, kind sir! Lunging from the shadows of the daisies, wobbling with a most unholy intent. But fear not, the road is clear.”

Bram let out a bark of laughter that sounded like a dry cough. “Clear? Lad, the slimes grow back every time it rains. You didn't ‘clear’ nothing. You just got yourself sticky.” He leaned on his spear, looking Dalton up and down. “And we don't have a ‘Lord’ here. We got a Reeve, and he don't want to see no one who talks like he’s swallowed a courtier. If you want a job, go to the Blacksmith or the Tannery. They always need someone to haul the heavy stuff.”

Dalton’s smile faltered, but only for a second. The word ‘Reeve’ lacked the poetic weight of ‘Baron’ or ‘Duke,’ but a noble was a noble, regardless of the nomenclature.

“The Reeve!” Dalton echoed, nodding as if he had been expecting the word. “A position of great responsibility, no doubt. I shall seek him out. But first, pray tell, where might a weary knight find a draught of ale and a place to rest his head? My ‘Branch of Chivalry’ has seen much use today, and I find my own vitality is at its ebb.”

Bram gestured with a thumb toward a sagging, two-story building near the center of the village. A faded sign hung over the door, depicting what might have once been a golden lion but now looked more like a jaundiced cat. “The Golden Pottle. Ask for Mott. He’ll give you a bed for three copper, assuming you don't bring that stick inside. And wash the slime off your face before you walk in, or he’ll charge you extra for the cleaning.”

“Three copper?” Dalton reached into the small, rough-spun pouch that had appeared on his belt along with his level-up. He felt the weight of his five copper coins—the reward for his ‘crusade.’ “A princely sum, but I am a man of means!”

“You’re a man of five coppers,” Sam hissed, his wings buzzing with frustration. “That’s two coppers left for food, Dalton. One meal. If you don't find a job by tomorrow morning, you’re going to be eating your ‘Branch of Chivalry.’”

“Master Sam,” Dalton whispered out of the corner of his mouth as he began to walk into the village, “the Lord provides for those who act with honor. We shall find our way.”

As he stepped through the gate, the true reality of Oakhaven settled over him. It wasn't the bustling marketplace of a Shakespearian comedy. There were no brightly colored tents, no jesters, no maidens throwing roses from balconies. Instead, there were pigs rootling in the mud of the main thoroughfare, the sound of a distant hammer striking iron, and the pervasive, sour smell of a community that hadn't seen a sewer system in its entire history.

The villagers he passed were men and women with hard, calloused hands and eyes that held the weary suspicion of people who knew that anything new was usually something expensive. They watched Dalton with a mixture of confusion and mild amusement. He was ‘that odd fellow’ already—the man in the stagehand’s rags who was walking through the muck as if he were leading a triumph through Rome.

Dalton didn't see the pigs. He didn't see the filth. He saw the potential for a new act. He saw a village in need of a hero, even if they didn't know it yet.

He reached the door of the Golden Pottle and stopped. He took a deep breath, adjusted his posture, and prepared to enter.

“Now, remember,” Sam warned, hovering just above his head. “Don't call the innkeeper ‘My Lord.’ Don't call the serving girl ‘Fair Lady.’ And for the love of the Lesser Hells, do NOT mention the slimes.”

Dalton offered a sharp, confident nod. He pushed open the heavy oak door, the hinges groaning in a way that he found delightfully dramatic. 

“My good man!” Dalton’s voice boomed over the low hum of the tavern. “I seek the master of this house! I am Sir Dalton, and I have arrived to grace your establishment with my presence!”

Behind the bar, a man with shoulders the size of hams and a face that looked like it had been carved out of a turnip looked up. This, Dalton assumed, was Mott.

Mott stared at Dalton. Then he looked at the slime-stained branch. Then he looked at Bram, the guard, who was now standing in the doorway, suppressed laughter shaking his shoulders.

“Welcome to the Pottle,” Mott said, his voice a dry rasp. “Wash your hands. Ale’s a copper. Bed’s three. And leave the stick by the door.”

Dalton didn't flinch. He walked to the bar with the grace of a prince, even as his boots squelched in the sawdust. The journey had been long, the crusade had been exhausting, and his gains were meager. But as he looked around the room, he knew one thing for certain.

The play was just beginning.
