# Chapter 6: A Good Night's Rest

The bed at the Golden Pottle was a masterpiece of architectural failure. It consisted of a sagging wooden frame filled with a mattress of straw that felt as if it had been gathered from the floor of a stable during a particularly muddy winter. Every time Dalton shifted, the straw crunched with the sound of a thousand tiny bones snapping, and the pervasive scent of damp wool and old ale rose from the moth-eaten blankets like a vengeful spirit.

But to Dalton, who had spent the better part of his life sleeping on a thin pallet in a drafty London garret, it was a couch of kings. He had fallen into a deep, dreamless slumber the moment his head hit the lumpy pillow, his mind mercifully silent for the first time since his death.

He woke to the sound of a rooster crowing somewhere in the village—a harsh, discordant noise that Dalton found significantly less musical than the trumpets of a royal fanfare. He blinked, his vision clearing as he sat up, only to be immediately confronted by the inevitable blue glow.

Dalton stared at the screen, his eyes widening with a slow, creeping realization of his own magnificence. He didn't see a minor damage bonus; he saw a mark of divine favor.

“Slime Slayer!” Dalton boomed, his voice echoing off the timber walls of the small, cramped room. He stood, ignoring the groan of the bedframe, and struck a pose of triumphant glory, his arms outstretched as if to embrace the invisible galleries of the world. 

“Behold, Master Sam!” Dalton cried, addressing the empty air. “The phantom recognizes the weight of my deeds! I am no longer merely a traveler; I am a Slayer! A title of power, bestowed by the very architect of this realm! St. George had his dragon, and Sir Dalton has his slimes! It is a beginning that shall be sung of in centuries to come!”

Sam, who had been dozing on the footboard of the bed, let out a long, weary moan and rubbed his eyes. “It’s a pity-title, Dalton. It’s what the System gives people who spend six hours doing something that should have taken ten minutes. It’s basically a ‘Participation Trophy’ with a +1 modifier. Don't go getting a larger head than you already have, or you won't be able to fit through the door.”

“You speak with the tongue of a cynic, my tiny friend!” Dalton laughed, his eyes fixed on the screen. “But look! ‘Blunt Weapons’! The phantom acknowledges my mastery of the oak! My ‘Branch of Chivalry’ is now a recognized instrument of my will! And my Constitution is strengthened! I feel… I feel as if I could withstand the very winds of winter without a shiver!”

Sam looked at the numbers. He would never admit it to Dalton—not in a thousand eternities—but he was secretly a bit impressed. Most Level 1 reincarnations spent their first night crying into their pillows. This idiot had gone out, grinded slimes until he was practically made of goo, and actually managed to trigger a Title and a Skill in his first twenty-four hours. It was a pathetic title, sure, but it showed a level of stubborn persistence that Sam hadn't expected from a guy who quoted poetry at trees.

“Yeah, yeah, you’re a regular legend,” Sam muttered, hovering into the air and stretching his bat-like wings. “But even legends have to eat. And unless your ‘Slime Slayer’ title comes with a side of bacon, you’re in trouble. Your stomach’s been growling for the last ten minutes, and it’s louder than your monologue.”

As if on cue, Dalton’s stomach let out a low, predatory rumble—a sound that reminded him that while his spirit was noble, his flesh was remarkably demanding.

“Sustenance!” Dalton declared, patting his midsection. “A knight must maintain his vitality if he is to face the trials of the day. Let us descend to the hall and partake of the morning’s bounty!”

“The bounty is porridge,” Sam said, drifting toward the door. “And maybe a piece of bread that’s only fifty percent sawdust. And remember, you’ve only got two copper left. That’s enough for a very small ale and maybe a look at a sausage. We need a job, Dalton. A real one.”

Dalton gathered his 'Branch of Chivalry' from the corner of the room, smoothing his tunic and attempting to tame his sleep-mussed hair with his fingers. He descended the narrow, creaking stairs to the main taproom, which was currently filled with the grey light of dawn and the smell of roasting grain and woodsmoke.

Mott, the innkeeper, was behind the bar, wiping down a wooden counter with a rag that looked like it had seen better decades. He looked up as Dalton approached, his turnip-like face showing no sign of recognition or welcome.

“Breakfast is on the table,” Mott grunted, gesturing toward a long, common board where a few other villagers were hunched over wooden bowls. “Eat quick. I need the space for the mid-day crowd.”

Dalton sat at the end of the table with the grace of a visiting dignitary. The ‘bounty’ was indeed a bowl of greyish, lumpy porridge and a thick slice of rye bread that required a significant amount of ‘Blunt Weapon’ skill to chew. But to Dalton, it was a feast. He ate with a dignified deliberation, nodding politely to the silent villagers who watched him with a mixture of suspicion and curiosity.

“My good Master Mott!” Dalton called out as he finished his last crust of bread. “The meal was… substantial! It has provided the fuel my spirit required. But tell me, for a man of my standing, what labors might this village require? I find myself in a state of some… fiscal transition, and I seek to earn the coin necessary to continue my journey.”

Mott stopped wiping the counter. He looked at Dalton, then at the grey oak stick leaning against the table. He leaned forward, his massive shoulders hunching.

“You want work?” Mott asked, his voice a low rasp. “I got work. But it ain't ‘noble.’ It’s dirty, it’s dark, and it’s under your feet.”

He pointed a thick finger toward a heavy wooden hatch in the corner of the room—the entrance to the cellar.

“My cellar’s overrun,” Mott said. “Rats. Big ones. They’re eating through the grain-sacks and nesting in the ale-casks. I tried traps, but they’re too smart. I need someone to go down there and clear ‘em out. I’ll give you five copper and a lunch of salt-pork if you kill the lot of ‘em. But if you break any of my barrels, I’ll take it out of your hide.”

Sam, hovering invisible beside Dalton’s ear, let out a sharp whistle. “Five copper? That’s a fortune for a guy with a stick. Take it, Dalton! It’s better than slime-hunting.”

Dalton’s eyes narrowed, but not with distaste. He looked at the cellar hatch and saw not a pit of rodents, but a subterranean dungeon—a dark, labyrinthine maze filled with fierce beasts that threatened the very stability of the village’s food supply.

“Cellar Rats?” Dalton mused, his voice dropping into a register of grim determination. “A plague of vermin in the dark recesses of the earth. A classic trial! Very well, Master Mott. I accept your challenge. I shall descend into the depths, and I shall not return until your grain is safe and your ale-casks are unburdened by these four-legged usurpers!”

**[ NEW QUEST: THE DEPTHS OF THE POTTLE ]**
**[ OBJECTIVE: KILL 10 CELLAR RATS ]**
**[ REWARD: 5 COPPER, 1X SALT PORK LUNCH ]**

Dalton stood, grasping his 'Branch of Chivalry' with a firm, confident grip. He didn't look like a man about to fight rats for a piece of pork. He looked like a hero about to enter the underworld.

“Master Sam!” Dalton whispered as he approached the cellar hatch. “Prepare yourself. We go where the light does not reach. We go to face the shadows!”

“It’s a basement, Dalton,” Sam sighed, hovering down toward the latch. “Just watch your shins. And try not to scream too loud when one of them bites you. It would be… uncouth.”

Dalton pulled back the heavy wooden bolt and lifted the hatch. A blast of cold, musty air hit him, smelling of damp earth, sour hops, and the sharp, musk-like scent of living things in the dark. 

Sir Dalton smiled. The second act was truly under way.
