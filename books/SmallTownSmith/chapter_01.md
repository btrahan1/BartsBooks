# Chapter 1: Escape

The mud of Kragh did not care for noble titles. It sucked at the polished greaves of Sir Gregory’s armor with the same indifferent hunger it showed for the ragged boots of the conscripts dying in the trenches. Overhead, the sky was a bruised purple, choked with the soot of burning thatch and the acrid tang of Algorian fire-oil.

"Hold the line!" Gregory bellowed, though his voice felt like it was being scraped through gravel. "For the Emperor! For the security of our borders!"

The lie tasted like bile. 

He swung his longsword, a masterwork of Algorian steel, parrying the heavy, desperate blow of a Kragh tribal warrior. The man was half-starved, his "armor" nothing more than cured hides and bone charms. He wasn't a soldier; he was a farmer defending a patch of dirt that Gregory’s superiors wanted because it sat atop a vein of tin. 

*Security,* Gregory thought, as he stepped inside the man's reach and delivered a mercifully quick thrust to the throat. *We are securing their graves for our coin.*

The battle was a cacophony of screams and the rhythmic thrum of Algorian crossbows. The "barbarians" were being systematically erased. Gregory looked back toward the command ridge, where General Valerius sat atop a white charger, draped in silk and gold-trimmed plate, watching the slaughter through a silver-rimmed glass. Valerius hadn't drawn his sword once in three campaigns. He didn't need to. He had Gregory—The Shield of Algoria—to do the wet work.

A sudden roar shook the earth. The Kragh had saved a final trick: a cache of primitive black powder buried beneath the very ridge the Algorians were pushing toward. The ground buckled. A geyser of dirt, fire, and shattered stone erupted, throwing horses and men into the air like Autumn leaves.

Gregory was slammed backward. His vision swam as his visor struck a jagged rock. For a moment, there was only a ringing silence, a white void where the world used to be. 

When he came to, he was pinned under his horse, a magnificent stallion named Omen that was now nothing more than a cooling weight of muscle and bone. Gregory’s left side was numb, his armor dented and caked in a slurry of blood and ash. 

The smoke was thick here—a roiling gray curtain that separated him from the main force. He could hear the Kragh survivors howling as they fell upon the stunned Algorian flank. 

This was it. 

The thought didn't come with fear. It came with a profound, terrifying clarity. 

Gregory reached for the clasp of his cloak—the crimson silk of the Imperial Guard. He tore it away, letting the mud claim the symbol of his shame. He worked at the buckles of his breastplate, his fingers trembling not from the cold, but from the sudden rush of adrenaline. 

He found a fallen Algorian officer nearby, his face unrecognizable from the blast, his build similar enough in the chaos. Gregory slid his signet ring—the lion and the sun—onto the dead man's finger. He placed his own shattered sword, the one the Emperor himself had gifted him, beside the body. 

He didn't look back. 

He stripped the rest of his plate, leaving it in a heap like the skin of a shed snake. Beneath, he wore a simple linen tunic and leather breeches—the undergarments of a knight, but to the world, the clothes of a commoner. He grabbed a discarded Kragh cloak, a rough thing of grey wool, and wrapped it around himself to hide the fine quality of his remaining clothes.

He crawled through the smoke, away from the screaming and the steel, heading south. Toward the mountains. Toward anywhere that wasn't Algoria.

***

The journey to Twin Rivers was not a walk; it was a slow, agonizing exorcism. 

For the first three days, Greg—he had decided the name on the second night, huddling beneath a rocky overhang while a mountain storm lashed the peaks—lived on nothing but adrenaline and the few strips of salted beef he’d scrounged from a dead scout's pack. His body, conditioned for the weight of plate armor and the explosive bursts of cavalry charges, rebelled against the steady, grinding pace of a man afoot. 

His feet, accustomed to fine leather riding boots, blistered within hours. By the end of the first week, the blisters had popped, bled, and begun to callous over a raw, angry red. Every step was a reminder of what he had left behind—and what was chasing him. 

He avoided the main roads, sticking to the goat paths and the dense, pine-choked ridges of the Kragh-Algorian border. He saw the patrols in the distance, plumes of dust rising from the hoofbeats of the very men he had once commanded. 1st Imperial Lancers. *His* boys. He watched them from the brush, his heart hammering against his ribs, not with the thrill of command, but with the cold dread of a deserter. He saw Captain Aris, a young man Greg had mentored, leading a search party near the Black Stream. Aris looked haggard, his eyes scanning the treeline with a desperate intensity. 

*They aren’t looking for a traitor,* Greg realized, watching them pass. *They’re looking for a hero. They’re looking for my bones.*

That realization hurt more than the hunger. He was letting them mourn a lie. But the truth—that their commander had abandoned them because he couldn't stomach the "security" they were dying for—would have been a different kind of death.

By the tenth day, the hunger became a physical entity, a wolf snarling in his gut. He reached a small hamlet, a collection of three huts and a goat pen. He stood at the edge of the woods for an hour, watching a woman hang laundry. He had gold in his hidden belt—enough to buy the whole hamlet—but to show a gold crown would be to sign his death warrant. A commoner didn't carry imperial gold. 

Instead, he approached her with his hands raised, looking like the wretch he had become. 

"Please," he said, his voice cracking. "Work for bread?"

The woman had looked at him with suspicion, her hand hovering near a wood-axe. But she saw the desperation in his eyes—and perhaps a shadow of the man he used to be. She pointed to a pile of un-split logs. 

He spent four hours swinging that heavy axe. It was different from a sword. The balance was forward, the impact jarring through his shoulders. But as the wood hissed and split, he felt a strange satisfaction. For every log he stacked, the world felt a little more solid. She gave him a loaf of rye and a bowl of watery cabbage soup. It was the first meal he hadn't stolen or scavenged. It tasted of sweat and honest effort.

***

Twin Rivers finally appeared on the fourteenth day, cradled in the junction of the Oakhaven and the Silverun. 

Greg paused at the valley’s edge, leaning on a sturdy branch he’d fashioned into a walking stick. The air here was different—softer, smelling of damp earth and blooming clover rather than the metallic tang of the Kragh frontier. The town itself was a messy, beautiful sprawl. Buildings leaned against one another like drunken friends, their roofs a patchwork of slate and thatch.

He walked through the gates—nothing more than two stone pillars with a lazy guard nodding in the afternoon sun—and felt the weight of a thousand eyes. In the Capital, men bowed. Here, they just looked. He was just another traveler, another dusty soul looking for a rest or a way out.

He passed a tavern, the *Drunken Trout*, where the sound of a lute drifted through the open windows. For a moment, his feet faltered. A drink. A bed. The gold in his belt burned. But no. A commoner wouldn't spend a gold crown on a tavern bed. He needed a purpose. He needed roots that didn't involve blood.

And then, he heard it.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

It was a heartbeat. The rhythmic, metallic pulse of the town. He followed it, his pace quickening despite the ache in his legs. 

The smithy was a wide, blackened structure at the edge of the river, its back cooled by a waterwheel that groaned with ancient effort. The heat hit him before he even reached the threshold—a wall of dry, blistering air that felt like a cauterization of his old life.

Inside stood Silas. 

The man was a titan, his skin the color of well-oiled leather, mapped with the white lines of old burns and the soot of a thousand fires. He was working a piece of iron that glowed like a captured star. Every strike of his hammer was deliberate, a conversation between man and metal.

Greg stood in the doorway, his silhouette framed by the dying light of the valley. He watched the way the iron yielded, the way Silas moved with a surprising grace for a man of his bulk. There was no anger in the smith’s work. No "securing the border." Just a thing being made right.

"Something you need, traveler?" Silas asked, his voice a low rumble that competed with the roar of the forge. He didn't look up. He didn't need to. He knew the weight of a man’s step.

"I'm looking for work," Greg said. He felt the word *Sir* try to form in his throat, a ghostly reflex of a decade in command. He swallowed it. "I don't have much. But I have strong arms and I don't mind the dark."

Silas paused then, his hammer resting on the anvil. He wiped a river of sweat from his forehead and looked Greg over. His eyes were sharp, the eyes of a man who spent his life looking for flaws in steel. He saw the ragged cloak, the calloused hands, and the way Greg stood—shoulders back, feet planted, despite the exhaustion.

"You speak like a man who's lived in a city, but you look like a man who's lived in the dirt," Silas grunted. "You know anything about the forge, Greg?"

Greg blinked. He hadn't given his name yet. Silas just nodded toward the river. "The guard at the gate. He’s my cousin. Mentioned a 'big, dusty fellow' asking for the smithy."

"I know nothing of the forge," Greg admitted. "Except that it makes things. And I’m tired of breaking them."

Silas let out a short, dry laugh. "Honesty. That’s a start. Most apprentices come in here thinking they’ll be forging master-swords in a week. Usually, they last about three hours before the heat drives them to the river."

He stepped aside, gesturing to the heavy leather bellows. "I need someone to keep the heat steady. If you can do that without passing out or burning my shop down, you can sleep in the shed. There’s a pot of stew on the hearth in the back. Eat your fill tonight. Tomorrow, the sun and the hammer start at the same time."

Greg walked toward the bellows. The leather was worn, smelling of salt and old oil. As he gripped the wooden handles and began the rhythmic pump, watching the coals glow from a dull ember to a brilliant, blinding white, he felt a strange, quiet peace.

The fires of Kragh were behind him. The politics of Algoria were a ghost story. 

Here, in the heat and the soot, Greg began to disappear. And as the bellows wheezed and the iron began to glow once more, the apprentice blacksmith set to work.
