# Chapter 2: The Sound of Steel

The first thing Greg learned about the forge was that it never truly slept. 

Even in the predawn gray, when the mist from the Silverun River still clung to the eaves of the shop like a damp shroud, the hearth hummed. It was a low, vibrating presence, a slumbering beast that required constant attention to remain docile. 

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

Silas was already at it. The master smith didn't seem to sleep at all. He was a creature of rhythmic violence, his hammer falling with a precision that Greg found both admirable and deeply unsettling. 

The sound brought back memories. Most of them were bad. 

The percussion of the hammer against the anvil echoed the sound of iron-rimmed shields clashing in the shield wall. The hiss of hot steel being plunged into the tempering barrel was the twin of the sound a cauterizing iron made on a soldier’s stump. And the orange-red glow of the coals—that was the color of the burning villages in Kragh, seen through the haze of a dying afternoon.

Greg stood in the shadows of the shed, his muscles screaming in protest. Every fiber of his being was stiff. His back felt as though it had been fused into a single, unyielding block of stone, and his hands were so swollen he could barely curl his fingers. In his former life, he would have spent an hour in meditative circulation, moving his Ki through his meridians to flush out the lactic acid and knit the micro-tears in his muscles. He could have been fresh and agile in minutes.

He reached for that internal well out of habit. He felt the familiar warmth of his core—a golden, swirling pool of energy that had once been the envy of the Imperial Guard. It pulsed against his will, eager to flow, to ease the pain, to make the heavy task ahead as light as air.

*No,* he told himself, his jaw tightening. *Not now. Not ever again.*

If he used his Ki, he was still Sir Gregory. He was still the man who could kill a dozen soldiers without breaking a sweat. If he wanted to be Greg the Smith, he had to earn it the way Silas did: with sweat, blood, and the slow, agonizing endurance of a common man. This was a different kind of tempering. Not one made of battle against men, but a battle against fire and steel. And more importantly, a battle against himself.

He stepped into the forge.

"Late," Silas grunted. He didn't look up from the plowshare he was shaping. 

"The sun isn't even up," Greg replied, his voice raspy. 

"The metal doesn't care about the sun. It cares about the heat. Get to the bellows."

Greg took his place. The handles of the bellows felt like lead. He gripped them, and for the next three hours, his world narrowed to the space between his hands and the hearth. 

*Hiss. Roar. Hiss. Roar.*

The rhythm was hypnotic. At first, it was torture. His lungs burned from the soot-heavy air, and the heat from the forge seemed to peel the layers of his resolve away. He found himself counting the strokes, his military discipline taking over. 100 strokes. 500. 1000. 

He began to see the "battle" in it. The fire was an opponent—fickle, hungry, and dangerous. If he pumped too hard, the fire grew white-hot and threatened to burn the metal away. Too slow, and it turned a sullen, useless red. He had to balance it. He had to read the fire the way he used to read a battlefield. 

He found himself smiling. It was a grim, soot-stained expression, but it was real. 

Silas observed him in silence. The smith was no fool. He had seen "broken" men before, men running from debts or wives or the law. But Greg was different. Greg moved with a suppressed economy of motion that spoke of years of high-level training. He stood at the bellows not like a weary laborer, but like a sentry on the walls of a besieged city. He watched the way Greg suppressed his winces, the way he adjusted his stance to distribute his weight—techniques that weren't taught in any village or hamlet.

Silas also noticed the lack of "flow." He knew about Ki, of course; everyone did. The nobles flaunted it like silk. But this man—this "Greg"—was actively fighting his own nature. He was working three times as hard as he needed to, purely out of some internal stubbornness. 

*A man with a secret,* Silas thought, wiping a smudge of grease from his forearm. *And a man with a heavy burden on his soul. But he works. By the gods, he works.*

Silas wouldn't turn away a good worker. No how, no way. Good help was harder to find in Twin Rivers than a straight branch in a cedar forest. He’d give Greg his space, and keep him as long as he kept up.

"Hold!" Silas barked, signaling for Greg to stop. 

The silence that followed was deafening. Greg’s arms shook as he let go of the bellows. His tunic was soaked through with sweat, clinging to his chest like a second skin. 

"Go to the river," Silas ordered. "Wash the soot off before you scare the customers. Old Man Miller is coming by with his mare. If she catches a whiff of you, she'll kick the shop down."

Greg nodded, his head swimming. He walked out of the shop and down the narrow path to the Silverun. The water was icy, fed by the mountain snows he had traversed a lifetime ago. He stripped to his breeches and waded in, the cold shock pulling a GASP from his lungs. 

He looked at his reflection in the moving water. The beard was thicker, the eyes more sunken. But underneath the grime, he saw a glimmer of something he thought he’d lost. 

Pride. 

He hadn't used his Ki. He had survived the first three hours of the second day on sheer, human grit. 

As he scrubbed his skin, he thought about the "tempering" he was undergoing. A piece of iron had to be heated, hammered, and cooled repeatedly to become strong. It had to be purged of its impurities. 

*Maybe,* Greg thought, watching the black soot swirl away in the current, *I’m just a piece of bad iron. And Twin Rivers is the anvil.*

He returned to the shop just as Old Man Miller arrived. Miller was a wiry man with a face like a dried apple and a temper to match. He was leading a chestnut mare that looked as though she’d seen as many winters as her master.

"Silas! This girl’s been favoring her left fore since the spring thaw," Miller complained, gesturing vaguely at the horse. "If you can't fix her, I'm going to have to trade her for a mule, and I hate mules. Stubborn bastards."

Silas walked around the horse, his large hands gentle as he felt the mare’s leg. Greg stood back, observing. He knew horses—he’d owned some of the finest stallions in the Algorian cavalry—but he had always left the shoeing to the farriers. 

"She’s got a stone bruise, Miller. And her shoes are worn to paper," Silas said. "Greg, get the paring knife and the rasp. It’s time you learned that a smith isn't just about fire. Sometimes, we’re doctors for the dirt."

For the next hour, Greg knelt in the dust, holding the heavy weight of the mare’s hoof between his knees as Silas taught him how to clear the debris and shape the horn. It was back-breaking work. The mare was restless, shifting her weight and occasionally nipping at Greg’s shoulder. 

Again, the temptation to use his Ki flared. A simple pulse of energy through his legs would have made him as immovable as a mountain. He could have held the horse’s weight with a single finger. 

Instead, he leaned into the mare, talking to her in the low, steady tone he’d used with Omen. He used his physical strength, his real strength, to steady her. 

"Easy now, girl," he whispered. "We’re both just trying to find a better way to walk."

By midday, the mare was shod and Miller was gone, grumbling a few coins into Silas’s palm. 

"Not bad," Silas said, looking at the shoes Greg had helped fit. "Your hands are steady. Too steady. You ever serve in the army, Greg?"

The question was casual, but the air in the shop suddenly felt as hot as the hearth. Greg didn't look up from the rasp he was cleaning.

"I’ve seen my share of marches," Greg said carefully. 

"Figured as much. You have the look of a man who’s waited for a signal his whole life." Silas picked up a heavy tongs. "Well, there are no signals here. Just the dinner bell. Get inside. My sister, Clara, is bringing over some stew."

Greg followed Silas into the small living quarters attached to the shop. Clara was a sharp-eyed woman who looked like a smaller, more refined version of her brother. She placed a large pot on the wooden table and looked at Greg with frank curiosity.

"So this is the one Silas is trying to kill with the bellows?" she asked, a smirk playing on her lips. 

"He’s still breathing," Silas said, sitting down with a grunt. "That’s more than I can say for the last three."

The stew was thick with potatoes, carrots, and bits of fatty mutton. Greg ate with a ferocity that surprised him. In the Palace, he had dined on glazed pheasant and spiced wine, served on silver platters. That food had always tasted of politics—of poison at worst and flattery at best. This stew tasted of earth and salt. 

It was the best thing he had ever eaten.

As the afternoon sun slanted through the windows, casting long shadows across the wooden floor, Greg felt a deep, soul-shaking exhaustion. But as he looked at Silas and Clara, heard their easy banter about the town gossip and the rising price of coal, he felt something else.

Safety. 

The Sound of Steel was still ringing in his ears, a constant reminder of the "battle" he had chosen. But for the first time, Greg wasn't fighting for an Empire. He wasn't fighting for a border.

He was fighting for himself. 

"Tomorrow," Silas said, leaning back and picking his teeth with a splinter of wood. "We start on the horseshoes for the trade caravan. It’s a big order, Greg. Hope you’re ready for the real heat."

Greg looked at his blistered, soot-stained hands and smiled. 

"I'm ready."
