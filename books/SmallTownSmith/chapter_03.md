# Chapter 3: Humble Beginnings

The order was for two hundred sets of shoes. 

To a layman, it was a mountain of iron. To Silas, it was a mortgage payment. To Greg, it was salvation. 

For three straight days, the rhythm of the forge didn't vary by a single second. The sun rose, the fire was stoked, and the bars of raw iron were pulled from the shadows of the shop to be transformed. Greg worked the bellows until his arms felt like they were made of cooling slag, then he moved to the second anvil, the "training block," as Silas called it. 

Silas had given him a simple task: take a pre-measured bar, heat it to a cherry red, bend it over the horn, and flatten the ends. If it was even a hair's breadth off, Silas would toss it back into the scrap pile without a word. 

Greg wasn't just working; he was thriving. He found himself smiling at the anvil, a small, private upturn of the lips that felt foreign and wonderful. 

In the Capital, every moment had been a chess match. A simple "Good morning" could be a feint. An order to move the 4th Infantry was a political statement designed to undermine a rival Duke. Here, the only decision he had to make was whether the iron was hot enough. 

*More heat. Less heat. Bend. Strike. Flatten.*

It was mindless. It was repetitive. And it was the most peaceful he had been in twenty years. No questionable orders. No lives hanging on his tactical genius. Just the hammer and the shoe. 

"He’s not normal," Clara whispered.

Greg didn't flinch. He didn't even skip a beat with his hammer. He was twenty feet away, the roar of the forge was constant, and Clara was behind the heavy timber wall of the living quarters, her voice barely a breath. To anyone else, she would have been silent. 

But even without his Ki, Greg’s body was not "normal." Years of internal cultivation had left his senses razor-sharp. His hearing was a predatory gift, a remnant of a life where a snapped twig meant the difference between an ambush and a safe night’s sleep. He heard the rustle of her skirts, the clink of the wooden bowl she was cleaning, and the concerned hitch in her breath.

"He does the work of three men and he smiles the whole time," Clara continued, her voice dropping even lower. "Most men would be cursing your name by now, Silas. But him? He looks like he’s at a festival."

"Let him be, Clara," Silas grunted. His voice was a low rumble, easier to hear through the shop. "I’ve seen his kind. He’s running from something big. A man doesn't hide in a forge unless he’s trying to burn away his past."

"But he hears things, Silas. I whispered for a rag earlier and he handed it to me before I even finished the sentence. He was across the room."

"Then we’ll call it a blessing," Silas said, the sound of his own hammer drowning out the rest. "Good ears make for a good smith. Now go fetch more coal before the fire dies."

Greg kept his face a mask of neutral effort. He didn't want them to know how much he heard. He didn’t want them to know he was terrified of how much he *enjoyed* the silence of his own mind. 

As the hours bled into days, he noticed something else. Much to his own surprise, he was gaining strength. 

Not the explosive, artificial strength of Ki—the kind that allowed him to shatter stone or lift a carriage—but a deep, dense, "honest" strength. His forearms had thickened, the muscles corded like the very hemp ropes the river-men used. His dexterity was improving, too. At first, his hammer strikes had been clumsy, relying on raw power to shape the iron. Now, he was learning the *finesse* of the strike. He was learning how to let the weight of the hammer do the work, how to flick his wrist to create a perfect curve.

His stamina was becoming a thing of town legend. The other apprentices in town—mostly boys in their teens looking for a trade—usually spent their afternoons lounging by the river once their masters looked away. Greg never stopped. He was the first at the forge and the last to leave. 

*Honest work... huh... who would have thought?* he mused, watching a shower of sparks dance across his leather apron.

In the military, his strength had always been a tool of destruction. He had trained to be the sharpest blade in the Emperor’s sheath. But here, the strength was creative. For every horseshoe he finished, a horse would walk easier. For every nail he forged, a fence would hold. It was a quantifiable, positive output. There was no blood on these shoes. No "security" that required a widow's tears.

By the fifth day, the trade caravan arrived. 

It was a chaotic affair—twenty wagons, fifty horses, and a swarm of merchants, guards, and teamsters. They brought with them the smell of spices, the braying of mules, and the first "real" news from the outside world.

Twin Rivers was a quiet pool, but the caravan was the stone thrown into it.

Greg stood at the back of the shop, stacking the crates of shoes he’d helped forge, when a group of caravan guards lounged near the entrance. They were wearing the livery of the Southern Trade Guild—hard-faced men with scarred knuckles and eyes that never stayed in one place.

"You hear about Kragh?" one of the guards asked, spitting a stream of tobacco juice onto the dirt. 

Greg’s hands paused. Just for a second.

"What about it?" Silas asked, wiping his brow. 

"The Algorians finally broke the main line. Burnt the capital to the ground. They say the Emperor’s calling for a triumph. But there’s a shadow on it."

"A shadow?" 

"Sir Gregory. The Shield. He’s gone. Felled by a Kragh blast, they say. The army’s in a right state. Half the men think he’s a god and the other half think the Emperor’s commanders let him die on purpose because his popularity was getting too high."

The guard chuckled, a dry, cynical sound. "Political mess, it is. Now they’re looking for a replacement. They’re conscripting every boy from here to the coast to 'stabilize' the new territory. Price of peace, I suppose."

"Price of greed," Silas muttered under his breath, then raised his voice. "Well, the war’s far away. Here, we just need shoes. You want these on the wagons or are you going to stand there gossiping like old hens?"

Greg stayed in the shadows. He felt a sudden, cold surge in his chest—a ghost of the man he’d left in the mud. For a moment, he wanted to step out. He wanted to tell them that the "stabilization" would be a slaughter. He wanted to tell them that Sir Gregory wasn't a god; he was a fool who had taken too long to see the truth.

Instead, he picked up the next crate. It was heavy, laden with fifty iron shoes. He didn't use his Ki. He used the muscles he’d earned in the last week. 

"Greg!" Silas called out. "Stop daydreaming and help the teamsters load up. The faster they leave, the faster we get paid."

Greg stepped into the light. The caravan guards looked him over—a big, soot-stained man with a quiet face and hands that looked like they could crush a skull. They didn't see a knight. They saw a laborer. 

"Heavy load there, big man," one of the guards said, impressed. "You ever think about guard work? Pay’s better than hitting iron, and you get to see more of the world than just this valley."

Greg looked at the man. He saw the cheap sword at his hip, the lack of discipline in his stance, and the shallow arrogance of someone who thought "seeing the world" meant looking down on it from a wagon.

"I like the world just fine from right here," Greg said calmly. 

He didn't smile this time. But as he hoisted the crate and walked it toward the lead wagon, he felt the rhythm of the bellows returning to him. 

*More heat. Less heat. Bend. Strike. Flatten.*

The world might be burning, but here, the iron was responding. And for now, that was enough.

That evening, after the caravan had trundled out of town and the shop was finally quiet, Silas found Greg by the river, scrubbing the day's grime from his arms.

"They're taking the boys, Greg," Silas said, his voice unusually soft. 

"I heard," Greg replied. 

"Twin Rivers has been lucky so far. But with the war 'won,' they'll be looking for taxes and blood to keep the new borders. My nephew... he’s sixteen next month. He’s not a soldier. He’s a wood-carver."

Silas looked at Greg, his sharp eyes searching for something. "You have a look about you. A look that says you've seen what happens to boys like that."

"I have," Greg said. He didn't Elaborate. He couldn't.

"If it comes to it," Silas said, "if the recruiters show up... what will you do?"

Greg looked at the water. He could still hear the "Sound of Steel"—the rhythmic, honest heartbeat of the forge. But he also heard the echoes of the "Barbaric" Kragh being put to the sword.

"I'll do what I have to do," Greg said. 

It was an honest answer. But as he stood up and walked back toward the forge, Greg realized that his "Humble Beginnings" might be shorter than he’d hoped. The world had a way of finding things it had lost. 

And Sir Gregory was a hard man to keep buried for long.
