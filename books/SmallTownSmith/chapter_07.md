# Chapter 7: The REAL Training

Greg stood by the window of the forge, watching the first leaves of Autumn turn a stubborn, burnt orange. 

In his mind, he was mapping out a campaign. Not one of conquest, but of survival. He estimated he had a month, maybe two, before the first snows choked the mountain passes. Once winter arrived, the Empire’s bureaucracy would freeze alongside the rivers. Recruiting parties wouldn't risk their horses or their pride on the icy roads of the frontier. They would wait for the spring thaw.

That gave him six months. Six months to take twenty-two ragtag, soft-handed boys and turn them into something that wouldn't just look "pathetic" to a recruiter, but would be capable of defending themselves if the lies failed. 

"They’re outside," Clara said, stepping into the shop. Her voice was cautious. She still hadn't quite forgotten the sight of the boys limping into town covered in nettle-rash and mud. "They aren't exactly... eager, Greg."

Greg didn't look back. "Fear is a good foundation. It means they know what’s at stake."

He stepped outside. The twenty-two teens were gathered in the dirt square. They looked like a flock of sheep that had just survived a wolf-attack and were now being told the wolf was their new teacher. They balked the moment Greg appeared. 

Tyler, usually the first to speak, stayed in the back, his eyes fixed on his boots. The Miller twins were huddled together, whispering about "the woods."

"I thought we were done with the Blackthorn," one of them muttered, his voice trembling. 

Greg didn't answer. He just looked at Silas. 

Silas stepped forward, his massive arms crossed over his chest. He didn't need to shout. The sheer weight of his presence was enough. He looked at each boy, many of whom were sons of men he’d known for thirty years. 

"You have two choices," Silas said, his voice a low, heavy bell. "You can go back to your chores, wait for spring, and let the recruiters take you. Or you can follow Greg. He’s the only man in this valley who knows what you’re up against. If you don't like his methods, you can leave. But don't come crying to me when the blue surcoats return and you don't have a bandage to hide behind."

The silence was absolute. Slowly, painfully, they straightened their shoulders. They didn't want to go. But they feared the recruiters more than they feared Greg. 

"Follow me," Greg commanded. 

He didn't lead them toward the woods this time. He led them toward the Silverun Lake. 

The morning air was crisp, but by the time they reached the old dock, the sun was beginning to burn off the mist. Greg didn't stop at the dock. He led them to a rocky, secluded cove further up the shore—a place where the water was deep and the bottom was strewn with large, smooth river stones.

"Strip," Greg ordered. "To your drawers."

"Is this more swimming?" Tyler asked, his bravado finally making a weak reappearance. 

"No," Greg said. "This is life."

The training was unlike anything they expected. There were no wooden swords. No shields. Instead, Greg pointed to a pile of stones he’d gathered on the shore. 

"Each of you, pick up a stone. One that weighs at least a third of your own body."

Once they had their burdens, Greg pointed to the water. "You are going to walk across the bottom of this cove. From here to that buoy I placed fifty yards out. You will walk. You will not swim. When you run out of air, you come up, take one breath, and go back down. If you drop the stone, you start over."

"That’s impossible!" one of the boys shouted. "We’ll drown!"

"The water doesn't want you to drown," Greg said, his voice echoing the philosophy he’d shared with Clara. "It wants to support you. But you have to control your panic. You have to learn how to keep your heart rate low when your body is screaming for air. You have to learn how to move with a weight that isn't your own."

He waded in, holding a stone the size of a small boulder. He didn't splash. He simply sank. 

They watched him. Through the clear water, they saw him walking—slow, steady steps. He didn't struggle. He didn't thrash. He reached the buoy, surfaced once for a silent breath, and walked back. He emerged from the water like a statue being unearthed.

"The real training is in the mind," Greg said, water dripping from his chin. "In a battle, the first man to panic is the first man to die. Now. Get in."

For hours, the cove was a scene of silent, underwater struggle. 

It was grueling. It was frustrating. The boys would sink, take three steps, and then rocket to the surface, gasping and splashing, their stones forgotten at the bottom. 

Greg was relentless. He stood in the chest-deep water, his eyes scanning every one of them. He wasn't the "big guy" who worked at the forge anymore. He was a Drill Master. He called out corrections, his voice cutting through the sounds of the lake.

"Don't fight the weight, Miller! Use it! It keeps you grounded!"

"Tyler, slow your breath! You're breathing like a rabbit! Deep from the belly!"

Clara watched from the shade of the willows. She was supposed to be there to assist with any actual injuries, but she found herself captivated by the transformation. Greg was different here. The "Noble Captain" she had glimpsed through her lens was now fully present. He had a way of looking at a boy that made the youth feel both terrified and seen. He didn't just want them to be strong; he wanted them to be *aware*.

"It’s not just exercise, is it?" Clara asked during a brief break.

Greg shook his head. "Lung capacity. Core stability. Panic suppression. This is the foundation of the Dragon Guard’s training. It turns a boy who 'reacts' into a man who 'responds.' If they can walk fifty yards underwater with a hundred pounds of stone, a ten-mile march with a pack will be a vacation."

As the weeks passed, the "Lake Training" became a part of the town’s secret heartbeat. 

The boys stopped complained. They started showing up early. They began to compete—not to see who was the strongest, but who could stay under the longest. They developed a silent language of nods and gestures. The "groups" and "cliques" of the village faded, replaced by a cohesive unit. They were no longer the Miller twins or Tyler the weaver’s son. They were "The Boys of the Lake."

Greg began to introduce other elements. Sprints up the sandy dunes with their stones. Teamwork exercises where they had to form a human chain to lift a "sunken" log. 

Much to his own surprise, Greg found his own strength evolving again. Without his Ki, he was forced to be more mindful of his own form. He was teaching the boys how to survive, but in doing so, he was refining his own mastery of the "natural" body. He found that his movements were becoming even more efficient, his strikes (which he practiced alone at night) more lethal because they were backed by "honest" muscle rather than internal pressure.

By the end of the first month, the results were undeniable. 

Tyler was no longer gangly. His shoulders had broadened, his posture was upright, and his eyes had lost their skittishness. Even his voice had dropped an octave. The Miller twins were no longer huddled together; they stood separate, confident, their hands steady.

One evening, after the training was done and the boys were heading back to the village, Tyler stopped by Greg. 

"Mr. Greg?"

"Yes, Tyler?"

"I... I had to help my dad pull the wagon out of the mud today. He was going to go get Silas and the mules. I just... I just grabbed the axle and gave it a shove. It came right out."

Tyler looked at his hands, his expression one of pure amazement. "I didn't even feel like I was trying. It was like... like I was just walking with a stone."

Greg smiled. It was the smile of a man who had seen a seed take root. "That’s strength, Tyler. The kind that doesn't go away half-way through a fight."

"Are we going to learn how to fight next?" Tyler asked, a spark of the old excitement returning. "With swords?"

Greg looked at the darkening mountains. The first frost would be here within the week. 

"No swords, Tyler," Greg said. "Not yet. First, you learn how to use what you have. A sword is just a piece of iron. You... you are the weapon."

As Tyler ran off to join the others, Greg felt a cold wind whip across the lake. Winter was coming. He’d achieved the first step—he’d built the engine. Now, during the long, dark nights of the frost, he would have to teach them how to drive it. 

He had a plan for the winter. It involved the forge, some "hidden" orders for scrap iron, and a very specific type of tool that looked like a plow, but worked like a pike. 

Silas met him at the edge of the water. "They're different, Greg. Even their parents see it. They move differently. They even *look* at the road differently."

"They're starting to see the world for what it is, Silas," Greg said.

"And what is it?"

Greg looked at the horizon, where the faint, flickering lights of a distant Algorian signal-pyre could be seen on the high peaks. 

"A place that won't give you anything unless you’re strong enough to keep it."

Greg turned back toward the forge. The first month was over. The foundation was laid. Now, it was time to turn blacksmiths into guardians. 

And as the first snowflake fell into the dark waters of the Silverun Lake, the "Real" training was only just beginning.
