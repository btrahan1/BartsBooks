# Chapter 8: The History of The Spear

The winter had settled into the valley of Twin Rivers with a heavy, muffling weight. The Silverun Lake was a sheet of gray iron, and the forest was a skeletal maze of white and shadow.

While the town huddled around their hearths, the twenty-two boys were gathered in the old communal barn, their breath hitching in the frigid air as they sat on hay bales. In the center, Greg stood by a small, portable brazier he had brought from the forge. The orange light flickered across his face, making him look less like a smith and more like a scholar of a forgotten, bloody age.

"Man’s first weapons were his hands," Greg began. His voice was low, but it carried effortlessly to the back of the barn. "He learned to make a fist and throw it at people’s faces. It was brutal, but effective. It was the birth of ego—the idea that you could impose your will on another through raw, physical contact."

He held up a clenched fist, the firelight catching the thick callouses and the steady, unmoving strength of his forearm. 

"Then," Greg continued, his hand relaxing, "a man picked up a rock. And he killed his brother with it, or so the story goes. The rock didn't change the intent, but it changed the outcome. It made the damage deeper. It made the killer feel a little less of the blow on his own skin."

The Miller twins looked at each other, their eyes wide. They had never heard anyone talk about violence this way. In the village, a fight was a scuffle behind the tavern. In the capital, it was a drama. In Greg’s mouth, it was a science.

"Then, a man learned to throw that rock," Greg said, miming the motion. "The first distance weapon. Again, crude but effective. However, it left you without a weapon. No one said that early man was all that smart. You throw your rock, you miss, and now you’re just a man with two empty hands standing in front of a hungry wolf."

Tyler let out a small, nervous chuckle, but it died quickly under Greg’s steady gaze.

"Then, one of the smarter ones picked up a stick. He used it to keep the wolf at bay. He realized that distance was life. The further away the teeth are, the safer you are. Then that man sharpened the stick to a point. Others soon followed, and this began the dominance of man over animals. And eventually," Greg’s voice dropped an octave, "man over man."

He reached behind him and pulled out a long, straight branch of ash wood—unworked, but sturdy. 

"The spear is the most honest weapon in the world," Greg said, running a thumb along the wood. "It doesn't require the years of training a sword demands. It doesn't require the noble lineage of a knight’s lance. It is the weapon of the commoner. The weapon of the defender. It is a tool that says: 'I am here, and you are there, and I intend to keep it that way.'"

He looked at the group of boys. They were no longer the "scrawny" things they had been in the summer. Their chests were broader, their eyes clearer. They were ready for the next layer of the tempering.

"Today, we are going to go out and make spears. Then, I will teach you how to use them."

***

The forest in winter was a different place. The Blackthorn briars were brittle and dormant, and the snow provided a canvas that showed every track.

Greg led them to a grove of ash trees on the northern slope—trees that had grown slow and dense in the cold shadows. He taught them how to select a shaft. He didn't look for the biggest or the prettiest; he looked for the straightest grain and the tightest rings. 

"A spear is an extension of your arm," Greg explained as he showed Tyler how to fell a slender ash sapling with a single, clean stroke of a hand-axe. "If the wood is weak, the arm is weak. If the wood is crooked, the strike will be crooked."

For several hours, the forest was filled with the rhythmic *thwack* of axes and the sound of peeling bark. Greg moved among them, his hands sure and steady as he corrected their choices. He showed them how to strip the bark without nicking the "heartwood," how to check for balance by resting the center of the shaft on a single finger. 

Clara followed behind them, wrapped in a heavy wool cloak. She carried a bundle of resin-shavings and leather thongs. She had been the one to suggest the ash grove, knowing the quality of the wood there. She watched Greg as he worked with the boys. He wasn't the "Dragon" today; he was something closer to a father, though a stern one. He praised a good cut and silently corrected a bad one. 

"Why spears, Greg?" she asked, stepping alongside him as they headed back to the barn with their harvest of wood. "In the stories, the heroes always have swords."

"Swords are for individuals," Greg replied, his voice a low rumble against the winter wind. "A swordsman is a soloist. He dreams of glory. He dreams of his name being sung. A spear... a spear is for a line. It is for a community. You can’t hold a village with twenty swordsmen; they’ll all be too busy trying to be the hero. But twenty spearmen, standing shoulder to shoulder? That’s a wall. That’s a home."

Clara looked at the twenty-two boys carrying their ash shafts. They looked like a strange, primitive forest on the move. She realized then that Greg wasn't just teaching them to fight. He was teaching them how to be a single, unbreakable entity.

***

Back in the barn, the "REAL" work began. 

Greg set up a small station with a pot of boiling linseed oil and a set of draw-knives from the forge. Under his direction, the boys began to shape the heads of their spears. They weren't using iron—not yet. The Empire’s tax-men would notice a shipment of iron spearheads. Instead, they were using fire-hardened wood.

"The point must be sharp, but the base must be thick," Greg instructed, showing the Miller twins how to char the tips of their ash shafts over the brazier until the wood turned a deep, obsidian black, then scraping away the charcoal to reveal the incredibly hard fire-tempered point. 

"This wood is harder than some cheap bronze," Greg said, tapping the point against a shield of oak. It didn't splinter; it bit.

Once the spears were shaped and oiled, Greg cleared the center of the barn. 

"Now," Greg said. "Position One."

He took his own spear—a simple, unadorned shaft of seasoned hickory. He stood with his feet shoulder-width apart, the spear held at his hip, pointing straight toward the heart of an imaginary foe.

"A spear is not a toy," Greg said. "It is not for waving. It is for the thrust. One motion. Direct. Efficient."

He demonstrated. A single, lightning-fast extension of his arms. The spear tip hissed through the air. There was no wasted movement. No flourish. Just the sudden, terrifying arrival of the point.

"Again," Greg commanded.

The twenty-two boys followed suit. Twenty-two ash shafts moved in unison. It was clumsy at first—the sounds of wood clacking together, the boys stumbling over their own feet. But Greg was unrelenting. 

"Again! From the hip! Use your legs, not just your shoulders!"

"Tyler, your point is sagging! Keep it at eye-level! If you can't see the enemy’s eyes over your tip, you're dead!"

For the rest of the day, the barn echoed with the sound of "The Thrust." 

Greg didn't let them stop until their arms were shaking. He didn't let them stop until they could move as one. He began to teach them the "March and Set"—the way a line moves forward while keeping the points forward, and the way they drop to one knee and set the butt of the spear into the earth to receive a charge. 

"If a knight on a horse comes at you," Greg said, his eyes reflecting the dying embers of the brazier, "your instinct will be to run. If you run, you die. But if you set this spear... if you hold your ground... the horse’s own weight will do the work for you. You don't have to be stronger than the horse. You just have to be more stubborn than the rider."

The boys looked at their fire-hardened spears. They weren't "pathetic" anymore. They were the beginnings of a phalanx. 

As the evening deepened and the boys headed home, their new spears hidden beneath their cloaks or stashed in the rafters of the barn, Greg stayed behind with Silas.

"They're coming along," Silas said, leaning against the doorframe. "Better than I hoped."

"They're learning the mechanics," Greg replied, his voice weary. "But winter is long. And mechanics aren't the same as the 'will.' They haven't seen blood yet, Silas. They haven't seen the blue surcoats with the intent to kill."

"They’ll have you," Silas said simply.

Greg looked at his own spear. He thought about the Dragon in his core. He thought about the rock, the stick, and the fist. 

"They’ll have a smith," Greg said. "And for now, that has to be enough."

He looked at the sky. The snow was falling harder now, blanketed the world in a deceptive, silent peace. But inside the barn, the scent of charcoal and linseed oil lingered—the scent of a village that was no longer waiting to be taken. 

The dominance of man over animals had begun with a stick. Greg hoped that the dominance of Twin Rivers over the Empire would begin the same way.
