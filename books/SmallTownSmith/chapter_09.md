# Chapter 9: The Iron Mine

Confidence, Greg had learned, was like the heat in a forge. Used correctly, it tempered the soul. Used poorly, it brittle the spirit or burned the hands of anyone nearby. 

The boys—if they could be called that any longer—were coming along nicely. Their shoulders were square, their eyes no longer darted away from a challenge, and they walked with a rhythmic, heavy tread that echoed the coordination they had learned in the lake. 

But with that strength came the inevitable swagger of youth. It happened on a cold Tuesday evening at the Drunken Trout. Two of the boys—the Miller twins—had been a bit too forward with a serving woman named Elara, using their new-found physical presence to hem her in near the pantry. They hadn't touched her, but they had loomed, their voices loud with the arrogance of those who finally felt like the biggest fish in a small pond.

That incident happened exactly once.

Greg had been sitting in the corner, nursing a small mug of cider. He hadn't shouted. He hadn't used his Ki. He had simply stood up and walked over. The air around him hadn't just cooled; it had curdled. He had looked at the twins with a gaze that reminded them of the night in the Blackthorn Woods—the night he had shown them the "price of peace." 

He hadn't said a word to them in the tavern. He had simply gestured to the door. 

The next morning, the twins had been "trained" differently. They didn't go to the lake. Greg had them hold a single spear at arm’s length for four hours in the biting frost, explaining that a man who couldn't control his own impulses was a man who would betray his line in the first ten seconds of an engagement. 

"Strength is a burden," Greg had told them, his voice as sharp as a winter wind. "It exists to protect those who don't have it. If you use it to intimidate the very people you are training to save, you are no better than the Kragh raiders or the Imperial taxes. You are just a different kind of parasite."

The point was made. The twins had apologized to Elara by noon, and for the rest of the week, the boys were the most polite, helpful citizens Twin Rivers had ever seen. 

***

"I heard there was an iron mine near here, Silas," Greg said one evening. 

They were in the shop, the fire of the forge a low, comforting hum against the howling wind outside. Greg was sharpening a set of wood-chisels, his movements hypnotic and precise. 

Silas paused in his work, wiping a smudge of grease from his forearm. "The Old Crow Mine? It’s five miles up the northern ridge. My grandfather used to take ore from it, but it haven't been worked in forty years. The vein went deep, and the Empire moved their operations to the coast where the transports are easier. Why?"

Greg looked at the glowing coals. "I have a plan for spring. The recruiters won't be expecting a 'pathetic' village to have any teeth. But if they see us building defenses, they’ll call it rebellion. However, if they see us reopening a mine... if they see a group of strong boys 'working the earth' to pay their taxes... they’ll see industrious subjects."

Silas chuckled, a deep, appreciative sound. "And a mining pick looks a lot like a war-pick, doesn't it?"

"It has a similar weight," Greg replied, a small smile playing on his lips. "I need twenty-three mining picks. I’ll have the boys carve the handles—ash and hickory, like the spears. But you and I need to make the picks themselves."

"You want real steel for those," Silas said, his expression turning serious. "The fire-hardened wood was fine for practice, but if we’re going into the Old Crow, we need something that can bite through granite. I’ve got a crate of old leaf-springs and iron bars in the shed... but we’ll need to refine them."

"Then let’s start tonight," Greg said. 

For the next week, the forge was a hive of clandestine activity. Each night, after the town had gone to bed, Greg and Silas worked together. It was a dance of two masters. Silas provided the raw, massive power—the heavy hammer and the years of knowing the local iron. Greg provided the precision. He showed Silas how to laminate the steel, folding it to create layers that would absorb shock without shattering. 

They weren't just making picks. They were making tools that were balanced like weapons. The heads were elongated, one side a heavy hammer for crushing rock, the other a sharp, curved beak designed for penetration. 

While Greg and Silas forged the steel, the boys were given a new assignment. In the communal barn, under the watchful eye of Clara, they began to carve the handles. 

"Not too smooth," Greg told them during an inspection. "A tool needs a grip. If your hands are sweaty or bloody, you don't want the wood sliding."

Tyler was working on his handle with a fervor that bordered on obsession. He had seen the first pick-head Greg had produced—a gleaming, dangerous-looking piece of craftsmanship that looked like it belonged in the hands of an Imperial sapper. 

"Are we really going to mine iron, Mr. Greg?" Tyler asked. 

"We are," Greg said. "And the iron we find... we’re going to use it. Every scrap. We’ll make nails, we’ll make hinges, and maybe... we’ll make a new set of tools for the whole village."

He didn't mention the spear-heads. He didn't have to. The boys saw the way Greg looked at the forest, the way he measured the distances between the trees. They knew that the "Iron Mine" was more than just a job. It was an armory.

By the end of the second week, twenty-three picks sat in the corner of the forge. They were beautiful, in a brutal, industrial way. Each pick was marked with a small, discreet stamp—a simple "T.R." for Twin Rivers. 

"They're ready," Silas said, looking at the row of steel. "But the Old Crow is a dangerous place, Greg. There are things in those deep shafts that aren't human. Hibernating bears, at the least. And some say the spirits of the old miners don't like visitors."

Greg picked up the lead pick. It felt perfect in his hand—balanced, heavy, and lethal. He felt the golden core of his Ki hum in response, a silent acknowledgement of the "work" ahead. 

"Bears and spirits we can handle," Greg said. "It’s the living that worry me."

He looked at the map Silas had sketched on the dusty table. "Tomorrow, we take the boys up. We don't just mine. we learn how to hold a tunnel. We learn how to fight in the dark. Because when the spring comes, Silas, the Empire won't just want our grain. They’ll want the iron we’ve 'found.' And they’ll be very surprised to see who’s guarding it."

As Greg prepared his gear for the hike up the ridge, Clara stepped into the room. She was holding a bundle of thick, greased leather. 

"For your hands," she said, handing him the gloves she’d been sewing for days. "The rock is cold. And I don't want you getting a blister when you’re busy being a 'worker.'"

Greg took the gloves. They were perfect—lined with wool, reinforced at the palms. "Thank you, Clara."

"Just... come back, Greg," she whispered. "The boys look at you like a god. But Silas and I... we just want our smith back."

Greg looked at her, and for a moment, the "Commander" faltered. He saw the firelight in her eyes, the genuine concern for the man beneath the soot. 

"I'm not a god, Clara," he said softly. "I'm just a man who's tired of seeing people taken."

"Then be a man, Greg. Not a hero."

He nodded, the weight of the picks and the winter and the coming spring all settling back onto his shoulders. 

The Old Crow Mine was waiting. And inside its dark heart, the boys of Twin Rivers would find more than just ore. They would find their own metal.
