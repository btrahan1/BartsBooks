# Chapter 10: The Ghost Mine

The hike to the northern ridge was a lesson in silent endurance. 

While the valley of Twin Rivers was blanketed in a soft, white peace, the ascent toward the "Old Crow" was a jagged climb through frozen scree and ancient, skeletal pines. The twenty-two boys moved in a single, disciplined line, their fire-hardened spears lashed to their packs and their new mining picks held in their steady hands. They were no longer the gangly, unsure things of the summer. They were lean, efficient, and beginning to understand that the cold was merely an opponent to be out-gritted.

But as the mouth of the mine appeared—a dark, yawning wound in the side of the granite peak—the confidence began to waver.

"We're going into the ghost mine?" Tyler asked, his voice cracking as he looked at the rotted timbers of the entrance. 

"There’s no such thing as ghosts," Greg said, his voice echoing off the frozen rock. He paused, adjusting the heavy pack of lanterns and pit-spikes he was carrying. "Well, there are, but I doubt any will be in this mine. They usually stay near graveyards and battlefields—places where the soul feels it has unfinished business."

"Usually?" Tyler croaked, his grip tightening on his pick-handle. 

"Usually," Greg repeated, a small, dark amusement in his eyes. 

The Miller twins shared a look. One of them, the taller one named Marcus, tried to regain his bravado. "Can't be much harder than spear work, or rock-laden water walking, right? At least here the air is... well, it’s there."

Greg looked at Marcus, and for a moment, he smiled. It was a slow, unsettling expression—the "evil" smile that the boys had learned to fear. It wasn't the smile of a friend; it was the smile of a man who was about to introduce them to a very specific kind of hell. 

"Hmm. We’ll see," was all Greg said.

He struck a flint, lighting the first of the oil lanterns. The yellow light flickered, pushing back the shadows just enough to reveal a tunnel that smelled of damp decay, sulfur, and the cold, metallic tang of old earth. 

"Spears out," Greg commanded. "Picks in the belt. We don't know what’s been using this place as a den since your grandfathers left it."

They entered. 

The silence of the mine was different from the silence of the woods. It was absolute. There was no wind, no distant river—just the sound of twenty-three pairs of boots and the rhythmic drip of water from the stalactites. The air grew heavy and stale, pressing against their lungs like a physical weight.

Greg led them deep into the mountain, his navigation aided by the faint, rusted tracks of the old ore-carts. He wasn't just looking for iron; he was looking for a defensible position. He found it three hundred yards in—a cavernous junction where the ceiling was high but the tunnels leading out were narrow. 

"This is our base," Greg announced. He pointed to a dark, jagged vein in the wall—a streak of reddish-black that shimmered weakly in the lantern light. "That’s the blood of the mountain. Iron. Marcus, Tyler—start with the chisseling. The rest of you, form a perimeter. We rotate every hour. Three mining, twenty on the watch."

"The watch?" one of the boys whispered. "Against what?"

"The dark," Greg said. "And whatever lives in it."

For the next four hours, the Old Crow echoed with a new kind of music. 

*Cling. Cling. Thud.*

The mining was a different beast than the forge. It required a shorter, more explosive stroke. The boys quickly discovered that swinging a pick in a confined space forced them to use their hips and their legs—the same muscles Greg had been building in the lake. But here, the resistance was absolute. The granite didn't yield like the water did; it fought back with jarring vibrations that threatened to shake the teeth from their heads.

Greg watched them. He didn't pick up a hammer yet. He stood in the center of the junction, his eyes scanning the three dark tunnels. 

In his mind’s eye, he didn't see miners. He saw a fortress. 

The spears were a start. Fire-hardened wood was a devastating surprise for anyone expecting an unarmed village. But Greg had a greater hope. If they found enough of this raw iron... if Silas could refine it into workable sheet-metal... 

A group of men with spears was a force—a formidable obstacle that could hold a bridge or a gate. But a group of men with spears and shields... that was a fortress. 

He pictured them: Tyler and the Miller twins, standing shoulder-to-shoulder, their left arms braced behind heavy, iron-rimmed shields, their spears reaching out through the gaps. A phalanx. The "Shield of Twin Rivers." With a shield-wall, twenty men could hold off a hundred. They could survive the arrows of a recruiter’s escort. They could weather the charge of the Imperial cavalry.

*First the point, then the wall,* Greg thought. 

Suddenly, a sound echoed from the deepest of the three tunnels—the "North Drift." It wasn't a drip of water. It was a heavy, sliding sound. Like a large body dragging itself over stone. 

The "clinging" of the picks stopped instantly. Every boy froze, their breath held. Tyler’s lantern flickered, the oil low. 

"Greg..." Tyler whispered, his eyes fixed on the darkness of the North Drift. 

"Stay in the line," Greg commanded, his voice a low, steady anchor. "Spears forward. Low stance."

The sliding sound came again. Closer this time. And then, a low, guttural growl that vibrated through the very floor of the cavern. 

Out of the darkness, two amber eyes reflected the lantern light. They were wide, predatory, and set in a massive, scarred head. A cave bear—a creature of pure muscle and bad temperament—had been hibernating in the North Drift, and the sound of the picks had woken it. 

It was a mountain of fur and claws, easily weighing a thousand pounds. It stepped into the light, its breath a foul cloud of carrion and old death. 

The boys began to scramble backward, their "ghost" fears forgotten in the face of a very real, very hungry predator. 

"Hold the line!" Greg roared. 

The command wasn't a suggestion. It was a physical wall. The boys hesitated, their instincts screaming for flight, but Greg was standing right behind them. He didn't have his spear out. He just stood there, his hands open, his presence immovable. 

"Tyler! Marcus! Set your spears!" Greg barked. "Butts in the dirt! Aim for the chest!"

Tyler’s hands were shaking so hard the ash-wood rattled against his armor-less chest. But he looked at Greg, and he saw the man who had walked across the bottom of the lake. He saw the "Dragon" who wasn't afraid of the dark. 

Tyler dropped to one knee, wedging the butt of his spear into a crack in the rock. Marcus followed a split-second later. The other nineteen boys, seeing their leaders stand, formed a semi-circle of fire-hardened points.

The bear roared, a sound that threatened to extinguish the lanterns. It charged—a terrifying, lumbering mass of dark fury. 

"Hold!" Greg commanded. "Hold..."

The bear hit the first line of spears. 

The sound was sickening—the crunch of fur and muscle meeting fire-hardened ash. The bear shrieked, its weight driving it onto the points. Two of the spears snapped under the pressure, but Tyler and Marcus held their ground, their feet braced against the stones. The bear swiped, its claws missing Tyler’s head by an inch, but it was anchored. It was trapped by its own momentum.

Greg moved then. 

He didn't use a spear. He didn't even use a pick. He stepped forward in the chaos, his hand reaching for the bear’s massive throat. To the boys, it looked like he was just reaching into the whirlwind. 

But Greg was circulating his Ki. 

Just a pulse. A white-hot burst of energy that focused all his gathered "tempering" into a single, devastating palm-strike to the bear’s sternum. 

There was a muffled *thump*—the sound of an iron hammer hitting a side of beef. The internal shockwave shattered the bear’s heart and collapsed its lungs instantly. 

The bear convulsed once and then slumped, its massive weight pulling the remaining spears to the ground. 

Silence returned to the Old Crow. 

The boys stood trembling, their breath coming in ragged gasps. They looked at the dead bear, then at their broken spears, and finally at Greg, who was standing over the carcass, his breathing perfectly steady.

"Ghost mine, huh?" Greg said, wiping a smudge of bear-fur from his tunic. 

Tyler looked up, his face pale but his eyes burning with a new, terrifying realization. "We... we killed it. We actually killed it."

"You held the line," Greg corrected him. "The spears did the work. If you had run, it would have caught you in the tunnels and killed you one by one. You stood together. That’s why you’re alive."

He looked at the broken ash-wood shafts. "Take the bear back to the village. The meat will feed the town for a month, and the hide will make twenty good sets of winter boots. And as for the iron..."

He pointed to the red-black vein in the wall. 

"We start mining at dawn. We’re going to need a lot of iron for the shields. Because the next thing that charges us... won't have fur."

As the boys began to butcher the bear under Greg’s direction, the "Ghost Mine" didn't seem so scary anymore. It was just a place of work. 

And for the first time, Greg saw it in their eyes. They didn't just feel "confidence." They felt powerful. 

The Shield of Twin Rivers was no longer a vision. It had been forged in the dark.
