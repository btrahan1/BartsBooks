# Chapter 15: Running and More Running

The air in Twin Rivers had changed. 

It wasn't just the spring thaw or the scent of blooming gorse. There was a vibrancy to the atmosphere, a subtle hum of energy that seemed to emanate from the cluster of buildings surrounding the forge. A week of "Learning to Breathe" had done more than just calm the boys; it had ignited them. 

Every member of the Twin Rivers Guard was now technically a "cultivator." Even Petey, who had struggled the most with the silent meditation, had finally found his spark—a warm, golden ember that sat in the center of his chest like a small, private sun. They were all cultivating now, the simple rhythmic breathing Greg had taught them becoming an unconscious habit as they worked the fields or hammered the iron.

Tyler, however, was in a league of his own. 

He had an inborn talent for the circulation of Ki that Greg hadn't seen since his days recruiting for the Imperial Dragon Guard. Tyler didn't just feel the ember; he felt the flow. He was already beginning to move the heat through his limbs, his movements becoming smoother, his strength more focused. Increasingly, the others were turning to him. When Marcus had a question about the 'four-four-eight' count, he didn't wait for Greg—he asked Tyler. When a shield strap needed adjusting for a better center-of-gravity, it was Tyler who provided the solution.

Greg watched it all from the shadows of the forge, a quiet, profound satisfaction settling over him. He was no longer the sole anchor of their strength. He was becoming the foundation atop which they were building their own.

"It doesn't bother you how they now defer to him?" 

Greg turned. Clara was standing in the doorway, a basket of fresh eggs on her hip and a curious look in her eyes. She had been watching the Guard’s progress with a mixture of awe and protective suspicion.

Greg laughed. 

It was a deep, resonant sound—a rich baritone that seemed to vibrate the very tools on the wall. It was a surprising, visceral laughter that cut through the seriousness of the morning. 

Clara stared at him, her mouth slightly agape. She couldn't recall ever hearing him laugh before. In the forge, he was silent. On the dock, he was stern. In the tavern, he was watchful. But this... this was genuine mirth. She liked it. She liked it a lot. It made the air feel warmer, and her heart did that strange, fluttery skip that she was quickly losing the battle to ignore.

"Heavens no!" Greg managed to get out, wiping a stray tear of amusement from the corner of his eye. "I'm just a simple Apprentice Smith! Why would I want twenty-two young men looking to me for all the answers? A leader who can’t be replaced isn’t a leader—he’s a lighthouse, and eventually, the storm always takes the light."

"Yeah, right," Clara said, her eyes twinkling as she stepped further into the shop. "A 'simple apprentice' with the physique of a marble statue and the tactical mind of a Grand Marshall. You’re a terrible liar, Greg. So, what’s next for your ‘not-disciples,’ Mr. Smith?"

Greg’s laughter subsided, replaced by a glint in his eyes that Clara recognized all too well. It was the look he got right before he was about to make the boys regret ever leaving their beds.

"Running," Greg said. "Lots and lots of running. They have the gear, they have the courage, and now they have the spark. But they lack stamina. In a real fight, courage lasts ten minutes. Muscles last twenty. But victory... victory goes to the one who can breathe after an hour of pushing a shield against another man’s chest. I intend to fix their lack of air."

***

The morning "run" began at dawn. 

Greg didn't let them leave their gear behind. He had the twenty-two men of the Guard don their gambesons—which were now fully riveted with bull-hide—and strap their iron-ringed shields to their backs. They carried their mining picks in their hands, the weight of the steel-heads a constant, jarring presence.

"We run to the Old Crow Mine and back," Greg announced. "Twice."

A groan went up, collective and miserable. The Old Crow was five miles of vertical climbing. Twice meant twenty miles of mountain terrain in full gear. 

"You have Ki now," Greg said, his voice a lash that cut through their complaints. "Use it. Don't just let the ember sit in your gut. Move it to your lungs. Move it to your legs. When you feel the 'burn,' feed the fire. The more you use, the more you make. Now, move!"

They moved. 

It wasn't a sprint. Greg wouldn't let them burn out in the first mile. He set a steady, rhythmic pace—the "Infantry Trot" that the Algorian Legions used to cross continents. *Stomp-hiss. Stomp-hiss.* 

Greg ran with them. He didn't look like he was trying. While the boys were gasping within the first two miles, their faces turning a bright, sweating red, Greg’s breathing remained slow and controlled. He didn't even seem to break a sweat. He moved with an effortless grace that was maddening to watch. 

He ran alongside Tyler, who was pushing himself to the absolute limit. Tyler’s face was pale, his eyes fixed on the mud of the road, his shoulders heaving with every step.

"Feed the legs, Tyler," Greg said, his voice as calm as if they were sitting at dinner. "Don't fight the pain. Invite it in. The pain is just a signal that you're growing. If you stop, the pain wins. If you run, the pain becomes part of the engine."

"I... can't..." Tyler wheezed.

"You can. Because if you don't, the man behind you has to carry your shield. And if he carries your shield, he can't use his spear. Do you want Marcus to die because you were tired?"

Tyler’s jaw tightened. He didn't answer, but his pace didn't slacken. He closed his eyes for a second, focusing on the golden spark in his gut. He pushed the heat down, felt it flood his thighs and calves. The ache didn't vanish, but it became... secondary. A dull background noise to the rhythmic fire of his circulation.

By the second ascent to the mine, the "Guard" was a group of walking ghosts. 

They were covered in mud, their gambesons soaked with sweat, their breaths sounding like a choir of broken bellows. But they didn't stop. They couldn't. Every time a boy stumbled, Greg was there, his hand a solid weight on a shoulder, his voice a quiet reminder of what they were guarding.

"Stamina is the price of life," Greg told them as they crested the ridge for the final time. "The Empire will be faster. They will have horses. They will have better steel. But they will be arrogant. They will expect you to tire. When they realize you can fight all day and still have breath for the evening... that is when their courage will fail. You are becoming a mountain, boys. And mountains don't get tired."

When they finally staggered back into the square of Twin Rivers, the sun was at its zenith. 

They collapsed. Twenty-two men dropped where they stood, shields clattering against the cobblestones, picks falling from numb fingers. They lay in the mud, gasping, their chests heaving in a chaotic, beautiful symphony of exhausted lungs.

But they were smiling. 

Petey was laughing, a hysterical, gasping sound. "I... I did it. Twenty miles. I’m... I’m a mountain."

Marcus and his brother were leaning against each other, their faces caked in grime, sharing a look of profound, shared accomplishment. They had been tested by something they couldn't cheat with gear or guts. They had been tested by time. 

Greg stood in the center of the square, his arms crossed. He looked at them with a pride that was almost painful to behold. 

"Wash up," Greg ordered, though his tone was unusually gentle. "Get some food. Tomorrow... we run it three times."

The groan this time was drowned out by a chorus of defiant, exhausted laughter. 

Silas walked out of the forge, wiping his hands on a rag. He looked at the "mountain" of men lying in the square, then at Greg. 

"They're ready for the weight, Greg. The shields, the armor... it’s not too heavy for them anymore."

"It was never too heavy, Silas," Greg said. "They just didn't know how to carry it. Now they do."

Clara watched from the window of the living quarters. She saw Greg standing in the sunlight, the "Simple Apprentice" who had just turned twenty-two village boys into an elite force of endurance. She saw the way they looked at him—not as a god, but as a map. A way to be something better. 

And as Greg turned to head back to the anvil, his own step light and sure, Clara felt a sudden, fierce hope. 

The spring was here, and the Empire was coming. But Twin Rivers was no longer a target. It was an entity that could breathe, and run, and endure. 

And for the first time, Clara felt that the "Price of Peace" might actually be affordable.
