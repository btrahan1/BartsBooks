# Chapter 16: Boar Hunting

The evening meal was usually a quiet affair in Twin Rivers, but lately, the communal barn had begun to sound like a mess-hall on the eve of a triumph. 

Great pots of mutton stew, loaves of heavy rye bread, and wheels of sharp cheese vanished with a speed that defied the laws of physics. The twenty-two men of the Guard sat on the long wooden benches, their faces glowing with a health and vitality that made the older townspeople marvel. But that vitality came with a price.

"You boys eat too much!" Greg shouted, his voice booming over the clatter of wooden spoons and the scraping of bowls. He was leaning against a pillar, a half-eaten crust of bread in his hand. "You are bankrupting the town! I’ve seen locusts with more restraint than this lot!"

He was smiling as he spoke, his expression lacking the razor-sharp intensity of the morning drills. He looked relaxed—almost content. 

Petey, his mouth currently half-full of stew, spoke up. "But, we're hungry, Mr. Greg! Really, really hungry! I feel like if I don't eat every two hours, my stomach is going to start eating my ribs!"

There was a chorus of agreement from the other benches. Marcus Miller nodded vigorously, holding up an empty bowl as if it were a battle-trophy. "He’s right. It’s like there’s a fire in my gut that never goes out. Silas says it’s the training, but my mother says I’m growing into a giant."

Greg laughed again—the deep, resonant laughter that had started to become more common in the last few weeks. "Of course you are, you little scavengers! You are doing the work of three men, but you are eating for five! Between the mining, the running, and the 'breathing,' you’re burning through the town’s winter stores faster than we can count them."

He pushed himself off the pillar and walked to the center of the barn, his shadow stretching across the straw-covered floor. 

"Silas and the Elder have been looking at the ledgers," Greg said, his tone shifting to one of playful challenge. "They say if you keep this up, we’ll be eating grass by mid-summer. So, I’ve decided it’s time for you boys to earn your keep. Tomorrow, we go boar hunting!"

The barn erupted in a mix of cheers and nervous murmurs. Boar hunting was a staple of the frontier, but it was rarely a "group activity" for twenty-two men. Typically, it was the work of experienced hunters with hounds and heavy crossbows. A wild boar was five hundred pounds of muscle, tusks, and pure, concentrated rage. 

"Boars?" Tyler asked, his eyes gleaming. "Like... the big tuskers from the Blackthorn?"

"Exactly like those," Greg said. "We’ve had reports of a sounder moving through the lower oak-groves. They’ve been rooting up the root-vegetable fields and terrorizing the milk-cows. We’re going to solve two problems at once: we’ll secure the fields, and we’ll fill the larder with enough salted pork to keep you boys from bankrupting the village."

***

The morning was crisp, the dew still frozen on the ferns as the Guard marched into the forest. 

They didn't carry their shields this time—shields were too cumbersome in the thick undergrowth—but they all carried their steel-tipped spears. Greg led the way, his movements silent and fluid. He had them moving in a "loose V" formation, designed to sweep a wide area while maintaining line-of-sight. 

"Boar hunting with a spear is about one thing," Greg told them as they paused to track a set of cloven-hoof prints in the damp earth. "Stability. A boar doesn't have a neck you can easily reach. It’s a charging wall of bone and gristle. It will hit your point at thirty miles an hour. If you aren't set, it will drive the spear-butt through your chest before the iron even bites."

He looked at Tyler. "Remember the lake, Tyler. The 'Receive Charge' stance. Use your Ki to anchor your feet. You don't fight the boar’s weight—you let the earth take it."

The hunt lasted for three hours. They tracked the sounder through the dense thickets, the scent of pine and wet fur growing stronger as they moved closer to the oak-groves. 

Suddenly, a series of high-pitched squeals and the sound of breaking branches echoed from a ravine ahead. 

"Positions!" Greg commanded in a low, sharp whisper. 

The Guard fanned out. They didn't run; they moved with the "Stamina" they had earned on the mountain roads, their breathing synchronized. They reached the edge of a small clearing and saw them—a dozen boars, led by a massive patriarch with tusks the size of Greg’s forearms. 

The patriarch saw them. It didn't flee. It pawed the ground, its small, red eyes burning with a primal fury. It let out a roar that sounded like a saw hitting a knot in a log. 

"Tyler, take the center! Marcus, Petey—flanks!" Greg directed, stepping back to let them take the lead. 

The boar charged. 

It was a terrifying sight—a grey-black shadow that accelerated instantly, its weight tearing through the underbrush like a landslide. It headed straight for Tyler. 

"SET!" Tyler bellowed. 

He dropped to one knee, the butt of his ash spear wedged firmly into the root of an oak tree. He leaned his shoulder into the shaft, his hands gripping the wood with white-knuckled intensity. He closed his eyes for a fraction of a second, flooding his legs with the golden heat of his spark. 

*Clack-Crunch.*

The impact was enough to lift Tyler’s front knee off the ground. The spear-shaft groaned, the ash wood flexing under the incredible pressure. The boat shrieked as the steel point buried itself deep into its chest, the weight of the animal driving it further onto the blade. 

For a moment, it was a test of pure, unadulterated will. The boar was still alive, its legs churning the earth into a bloody slurry, its tusks snapping at the air inches from Tyler’s face. 

"SUPPORT!" Greg shouted. 

Marcus and one of the other boys stepped in from the sides, their spears flashing. They delivered two clean strikes to the boar’s flanks, finding the vitals Greg had taught them about during the bear-butchering. 

The patriarch gave one final, shuddering groan and collapsed. 

In the clearing, the rest of the sounder was quickly surrounded. The boys didn't panic. They used their spears to keep the animals at bay, working in small groups of three to isolate and finish the hunt. There was no chaos. No "scares" like the goblin camp. They were a unit now. 

When the last of the boars were down, the clearing was quiet, save for the heavy breathing of the twenty-two men. 

"Not a bad morning’s work," Greg said, walking into the center of the clearing. He checked the patriarch—it was a monster, easily the largest boar anyone in Twin Rivers had seen in a generation. 

He looked at the boys. They were covered in mud and blood, their tunics torn by brambles, but they were grinning. They looked at the pile of meat and they didn't see a "chore"—they saw a feast. 

"Six hundred pounds of meat, at least," Marcus estimated, wiping his spear-tip on a patch of moss. "My mother is going to be curing ham for a month."

"And your stomach will be full for a week," Greg teased, clapping Tyler on the back. "Well done, Lead. You didn't blink."

"The wood held," Tyler said, his voice still shaky but his eyes bright with pride. "I felt the earth take the weight. Just like you said."

They spent the rest of the afternoon hauling the kills back to the village. Greg had them fashion litters out of branches and shields, a final "stamina" exercise as they carried the heavy carcasses over the ridge. 

As they marched into Twin Rivers, the news preceded them. The townspeople came out of their houses, cheering as the "Town Guard" returned with the spoils of the hunt. There were no "scars" this time, only victory. 

That evening, the communal barn was the site of a true celebration. The air was thick with the scent of roasting pork and woodsmoke. Greg sat with Silas and Clara, watching the boys eat. 

They were still eating for five men, but this time, no one was complaining about the cost. 

"They're finding their place, Greg," Silas said, Raising a mug of cider. "They aren't just boys playing soldier anymore. They’re providers. That’s a powerful thing for a young man to feel."

"It’s the final layer of the tempering," Greg agreed. "They’ve learned to fight, they’ve learned to endure, and now they’ve learned to contribute. They aren't just protecting the town—they *are* the town."

Clara looked at Greg, her eyes soft in the firelight. "You really do care about them, don't you? It’s not just a 'plan' for you anymore."

Greg looked at Tyler, who was currently telling a highly exaggerated version of the hunt to a group of wide-eyed children. 

"Every life in this valley is worth a hundred imperial borders, Clara," Greg said softly. "I’m just making sure the world remembers that."

As the feast continued late into the night, the "Price of Peace" felt very far away. But as Greg looked at the fire-hardened spears leaning against the barn wall, he knew the test was coming. 

But for tonight, there was enough pork. There was enough laughter. And there was a village that knew it was no longer alone in the dark.
