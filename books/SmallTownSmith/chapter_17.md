# Chapter 17: Courting

A month had passed since the boar hunt, and in that time, the Twin Rivers Guard had achieved something that many imperial legions took years to master: a rhythm that didn't require a commander’s voice. 

Their schedule was now as predictable as the tides. At dawn, they gathered at the lake to "breathe," their sparks now burning with a steady, reliable heat. Then they ran until noon—miles of vertical trekking that had turned their legs into pillars of corded iron. The afternoons were spent in the "Old Crow," bringing out the iron that was the lifeblood of their transformation. By evening, they were back in the square, the *clack-clink* of their shields echoing off the village walls as they ran through the drills Tyler now led with a natural, authoritative ease.

Greg found himself more and more on the sidelines, a ghost in the smoke of the forge. He was still the architect, but he was no longer the Drill Master. He watched as the men took up swimming in the evenings without being told, heading for the very spot near the island where he had first sought his own peace. They were grasping the rudiments of internal balance, following Tyler’s lead as the boy-turned-leader translated Greg’s complex philosophies into the blunt, honest language of the valley.

But Greg’s hands were not idle. 

For the last month, the anvil had rung with a specific, haunting note. Every night, while the town slept, Greg had been working on twenty-two shafts. They weren't wood. They were steel—refined from the purest veins of the Old Crow and folded a thousand times. But more than that, they were infused. With every strike of his hammer, Greg had allowed his own Ki to flow into the metal, weaving his internal energy into the crystalline structure of the steel. 

They were masterpiece creations—nigh unbreakable, balanced for the hands of the twenty-two men he had come to see as his own. If the Empire brought fire, these shafts would not melt. If they brought giants, they would not snap. They were his final, silent vow to their survival.

He was finishing the twenty-second shaft, the iron glowing a dull, ominous crimson, when a shadow fell across the threshold of the forge. 

Greg finished the quenching, the hiss of the cooling trough sounding like a sigh of accomplishment. He added the shaft to the rack with the others, their surfaces shimmering with a faint, iridescent oil that spoke of the power trapped within. 

He wiped his hands on a greasy rag and turned. 

Clara was standing there. She held her hands behind her back, her posture uncharacteristically stiff. She was wearing her best Sunday tunic, a soft blue that matched the spring sky, and her hair was braided with a precision that Greg hadn't seen since the Capital’s balls.

Greg smiled. It was a gentle, genuine expression. "Hello, Clara. What brings you here? The dinner bell hasn't rung yet."

Clara didn't answer immediately. She looked at the rack of steel shafts, then at Greg’s soot-stained face and the broad, powerful shoulders that were now a permanent fixture in her heart. She took a deep breath—the 'core-breath' Greg had taught the men—and brought her hands from behind her back. 

She was holding a bouquet of wildflowers—primrose, clover, and a few sprigs of wild lavender that grew on the ridge. 

Earlier that morning, she had gone to Silas and asked for his formal permission. Her brother had stared at her as if she had suddenly grown a pair of antlers. 

"Permission for what?" Silas had asked, his mouth full of bread. 

"To court Greg," Clara had stated, her chin held high.

Silas had nearly choked. "But... but he’s the man! He’s supposed to... to do the flowers and the talk and the... whatever it is men do!"

"If I wait for him to make a move, Silas, I'll be an old maid with twenty-two 'nephews' and a smith who thinks a romantic evening is sharpening a plowshare," Clara had snapped. "He’s a good man, but he’s as dense as the iron he works. I’m doing this myself."

Now, standing in the heat of the forge, she held the flowers out toward him. 

"I am here to court you, Greg," Clara said, her voice clear and unwavering, though her cheeks were burning a vivid pink. 

Silas, who had been coming in from the back shed with a crate of coal, stopped dead. His jaw dropped so far it was a wonder it didn't hit the stone floor. He stood there, mouth agape, the crate slipping inches in his grip. 

Greg didn't move. He looked at the flowers, then at Clara’s determined face, and then at the soot on his own hands. In the world of Sir Gregory, "courtship" was a sequence of formal letters, strategic alliances, and distant nods across a ballroom. It was a transaction. 

But this? This was the honest, blunt vulnerability of Twin Rivers. 

"I'm sorry," Greg managed to get out, his own mouth slightly open in shock. "Could you... could you please repeat that?"

"I said, I’m here to court you," Clara repeated, stepping closer, ignoring the heat of the hearth. "You’ve spent all your time teaching the men how to breathe and how to fight and how to be a 'Guard.' But you’ve forgotten to live for yourself, Greg. I’ve been watching you from my ridge for months. I know you're not just an apprentice. I know you're carrying a world of trouble on your back. And I’ve decided that I’d like to help you carry it."

She thrust the flowers toward his chest. 

"So. Do you accept the flowers, or am I going to have to go get Silas’s sledgehammer to get your attention?"

Greg looked at her. He saw the fire in her eyes, the same fire he had worked so hard to build in the boys. He saw the strength that didn't come from Ki or steel, but from a heart that knew what it wanted and wasn't afraid to ask for it.

He reached out, his soot-stained fingers brushing against her soft skin as he took the bouquet. The scent of the lavender and primrose cut through the smell of coal-smoke and hot iron like a promise.

Silas finally found his voice, though it was a strangled, high-pitched thing. "Clara! You... you're supposed to... he’s the... what about the rules?"

"The rules are for people with more time than sense, Silas," Clara said, not taking her eyes off Greg. 

Greg looked at the flowers, then at the twenty-two steel shafts behind him. He had been preparing for a war. He had been preparing to disappear again once the Empire was defeated. He had been building a fortress, but he had forgotten to build a home.

A slow, warm smile spread across his face—not the "Dragon" smile, and not the mentor’s smile. It was the smile of a simple man who had just been given a gift he didn't think he deserved.

"I accept the flowers, Clara," Greg said softly. 

He didn't have a bouquet to give her in return. He didn't have a poem or a fancy title. But he reached behind him and picked up the twenty-second steel shaft, the metal now cool and shimmering with its internal power. 

"I don't have flowers," Greg said, handing her the cold, beautiful steel. "But I have this. It’s the last of the Guard’s spears. I was going to keep it for myself... but I’d rather you have it. It’s as unbreakable as my word. If you have this... you'll never be alone in the dark."

Clara took the steel shaft. It was heavy, and it felt alive in her hands, a low hum of energy vibrating through the metal. She looked at Greg, her blush deepening, but her eyes were full of a fierce, sudden joy.

Silas stood in the background, still holding his crate of coal, watching the most unconventional courtship in the history of the frontier. He shook his head, a small, begrudging smile finally touching his lips.

"Well," Silas muttered to the empty forge. "I guess I better go tell the boys. Greg’s been 'conscripted' by something more dangerous than the Emperor."

Outside, the *clack-clink* of the training shields continued, the men of the Guard oblivious to the fact that their Commander had just been surrendered to the most powerful force in Twin Rivers. 

Greg looked at Clara, and for the first time since he had faked his death in the mud of Kragh, he wasn't looking at the horizon for enemies. 

He was looking at the woman in the blue tunic, and he was home.
