# Chapter 1: The Spark

The first thing Tanner knew was not a word, nor a face, but a rhythm.

*Clang. Hiss. Clang.*

It was a heartbeat that did not belong to him, yet it was the cadence to which his world danced. He sat in a wooden pen, the slats smooth and smelling of linseed oil, positioned exactly five paces from the Great Anvil. To a creature less than three years old, the distance was a vast canyon of heat and shimmering air. 

At the center of that heat stood the Mountain.

His father, Valerius, did not look like a man when he was at the forge. He looked like an avatar of the earth itself. His skin was deep bronze, slicked with a sheen of sweat that caught the orange glow of the embers and turned him into a statue of living copper. Every time his hammer fell, a spray of sparks—tiny, dying stars—danced into the air. 

Tanner watched them. Not with the idle curiosity of a child, but with a gaze that felt heavy, even to himself. He saw things the sparks left behind. To most, they were just light and heat. To Tanner, they were trails of *Might*. He could see the way the impact of the hammer forced the ambient energy of his father’s body into the stubborn iron. It wasn’t just a strike; it was an argument. The hammer insisted, and the iron, eventually, agreed.

*Clang.*

The impact vibrated through the floor, through the wooden pen, and into Tanner’s small, bare feet. He liked the feeling. It was solid. It was real. 

"Watch the grain, Valerius," a voice drifted from the shadow of the back room. 

It was a voice like silk pulled over stone—resonant, ageless, and carrying the faint scent of crushed pine needles. Tanner’s mother, Elara, stepped into the light. She was as lithe as Valerius was stout. Her ears were long and swept back, tapering to points that twitched whenever a particularly loud strike rang out. 

She walked to the workbench where a hide of drake-leather lay pinned. In her hand was a needle of bone, threaded with silver-infused silk. She didn't use a hammer. She used her fingers, which moved with a speed that made them look like a blur of pale moonlight.

Tanner turned his head, his neck straining against the collar of his simple linen tunic. If his father was the Mountain, his mother was the River. Where Valerius forced the world to change, Elara persuaded it. 

He could see it there, too. As she pushed the needle through the tough, pebbled hide, a faint blue luminescence followed the thread. It was *Magic*. It wasn't the explosive power of a sorcerer’s fireball; it was the quiet, persistent hum of the world’s weave being pulled into a new shape. 

Tanner reached out a small hand, his fingers grasping at the air between the forge and the workbench. He could see the two energies—the orange thrum of Might and the blue ripple of Magic—meeting in the center of the room. They didn't mix. They spiraled around each other like ghosts, never touching, yet always aware of the other’s presence.

In that moment, a third presence entered the shop.

The bell above the door didn't ring—it was silenced by a gloved hand before it could utter a sound. A tall figure stood in the doorway, framed by the bright Elveron sun. He wore a cloak of mottled green and grey, colors that seemed to shift and change even as Tanner looked at them.

"Kaelen," Valerius grunted, not looking up from the glowing orange bar of steel. "You're late for the solstice."

"The woods don't keep the same calendar as your clocks, Valerius," the newcomer replied. He stepped forward, pulling back his hood to reveal features that were a sharper, more weathered version of Elara’s. This was Grandfather Kaelen.

He didn't look at the adults first. He looked at the child in the pen.

Tanner stared back. He saw something different in Kaelen. Around the old ranger, the energies of the world didn't spiral or argue. They sat still. Kaelen carried a bow on his back, carved from the heartwood of a lightning-struck oak. To Tanner’s eyes, the bow wasn't an object carried by the man; it was an extension of his very soul. The two were bound by thousands of identical threads of resonance.

Kaelen walked over to the pen and knelt. His eyes were the color of a forest after rain—deep green and full of secrets.

"Still watching, little sprout?" Kaelen asked softly.

Tanner reached out and touched the cuff of Kaelen’s cloak. Immediately, he pulled his hand back. 

It felt… wrong. 

The cloak was masterwork, woven by the finest elven looms in the High District. It was infused with shifting-charms and durability-hexes. But to Tanner’s touch, it felt like a cold stone. There was no connection between the cloth and the man wearing it. The energy of the cloak was a foreign thing, a parasite clinging to Kaelen’s frame.

Kaelen saw the flinch. A small, knowing smile touched his lips.

"He feels it, Elara," Kaelen said, his voice rising just enough to be heard over the hiss of the quenching bucket.

Elara paused, her needle hovering over the leather. "He is sensitive, Father. We know this."

"It’s more than sensitivity," Kaelen said. He reached into his belt and pulled out a small, ornate dagger. The hilt was ivory, the blade a shimmering alloy of mithril and steel. It was a gift fit for a prince. He held it out toward Tanner.

The child looked at the beautiful thing. He saw the power humming within the blade—a sharp, aggressive magic that promised to cut through anything. It was a weapon of incredible strength.

But Tanner didn't reach for it. Instead, he looked down at the floor of his pen. There lay a small, discarded scrap of iron from his father’s forge and a short length of twine from his mother’s bench. 

Tanner picked up the scrap of iron. It was ugly, pitted, and cold. He picked up the twine. It was frayed and dull. 

Slowly, with the clumsy determination of a child, he began to wrap the twine around the middle of the iron scrap. He fumbled, his small fingers lacking the dexterity of his parents, but he didn't stop. He pushed the twine, tucked it under itself, and pulled.

As he did, the air around his hands began to shimmer. 

It wasn't orange. It wasn't blue. It was a pale, clear gold.

Kaelen’s breath hitched. Valerius stopped his hammer mid-swing. Elara stood up, her chair scraping harshly against the stone floor.

Tanner wasn't using Might to force the materials. He wasn't using Magic to persuade them. He was simply… recognizing them. Because he was the one doing the work, the iron and the twine recognized *him* back. For a brief, flickering second, the ugly scrap and the frayed string became more than the sum of their parts. They became a Tool.

Tanner held up his creation. It was a lopsided, poorly-wrapped piece of junk. But he looked at it with a pride that made his small chest swell.

Kaelen took back the princely dagger, tucking it away as if it were nothing more than a kitchen utensil. He looked at his grandson with a fierce, terrifying intensity.

"Listen to me, Tanner Smith," the old ranger whispered. "The world will try to give you gifts. It will offer you swords that can split mountains and armor that can turn dragon fire. It will tell you that you are weak, and that these things will make you strong."

Kaelen leaned closer, his forehead almost touching the slats of the pen.

"But remember this: Borrowed strength is false strength. A sword you did not forge does not know your hand. A cloak you did not stitch does not know your stride. If you do not make it, you do not own it. And if you do not own it, it can always be taken away."

Tanner didn't understand the words. Not yet. But he understood the *intent*. He looked at his lopsided iron scrap, then at his father’s mighty hammer, then at his mother’s delicate needle.

He looked at the world, and for the first time, he didn't just see the light and the heat.

He saw the work.

And in his small, stubborn heart, the spark caught fire.
