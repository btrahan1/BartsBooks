# Chapter 6: Train

The sun had not yet cleared the ridge when Tanner slipped out of the back door.

He hadn't been told to. No one had asked him. He had simply listened to the conversation at the table—to the words *teach* and *begin* and *today*—and drawn his own conclusions about what that meant and where it happened. 

He crossed the cobbled kitchen yard in bare feet, the stone still cold and faintly damp with dew. He ducked under the fence rail and stepped into the practice yard, where the earth was packed flat and bare from years of boots and the occasional thrown body. The silver-leaf oak stood at the western edge, its long shadow still stretching east across the ground like a pointing finger.

Tanner stopped in the center of the yard and looked around.

The yard felt different when it was empty. In the mornings of the spar, it was a place of heat and noise and whirling essences. Now, in the quiet, it was simply a patch of earth between a fence and a tree. It smelled of dry soil and crushed sage and the faint ghost of his father's sweat.

He looked at his staff.

It had been a stick two weeks ago. Since then, it had lived against his leg by day and at his side by night, and it had changed the way any tool changes when it is held consistently and with intent. The rough-snapped end had been worn smooth by hours of grip and transfer. The bark along the upper third had been worried away entirely by his rubbing, polishing, and—on three separate undignified occasions—his teeth, leaving pale, satiny wood beneath. At the halfway point, where his left hand most often rested, there was a faint, permanent warmth even in the morning cold. 

It was two feet of pine. It barely reached his shoulder. But it no longer looked like something that had fallen off a fence. It looked deliberate.

Tanner closed both hands around it—left low, right high—and planted his feet.

He thought about Kaelen. He closed his eyes and walked back through every morning of the spar he had watched from his pen. He saw the old Ranger's feet: never fully planted, never fully lifted, moving in a weight-transfer that looked casual and was anything but. He saw the hands: the top hand steering, the bottom hand being the engine. He saw the way the dark staff moved through its forms—not swinging from the arms, but rotating from the center of the chest, the hips, the core.

He had no formal language for any of this. He did not know the words "form" or "stance" or "rotation." But he had the Sight, and the Sight did not need words. It had showed him the way Kaelen's essence flowed down through his legs and into the earth like roots, and then redirected upward, channeling through the staff in a clean, unbroken current.

He opened his eyes.

He tried.

The first sweep was a disaster. His feet were too close together and he nearly toppled sideways, saving himself by jabbing the staff into the dirt like a punt-pole. He reset. Feet wider. He tried the weight shift—left, right, left—and found the rhythm of it, the slow lean and recover that told the body it was balanced without being braced. That was better.

He brought the staff into the first movement of the form—a low, horizontal sweep that Kaelen used to break the ankle-line, followed by a pivot and an overhead drop. 

The sweep was fine. Slow and wobbly, but the geometry was there. He felt the staff's weight travel through the arc, and felt his own center responding to it, micro-adjusting without being told.

The pivot was where it fell apart. His back foot caught as he turned, and the overhead drop became an overhead stumble. He sat down hard in the dust.

He got up. He did not look around to see if anyone had witnessed this. He did not rub his bruised tailbone for more than a second. He reset his feet, closed his hands around the staff, and started the sweep again.

*Sweep. Pivot. Drop.*

Better. He caught himself this time, though the drop was short—his arms didn't have the length to complete the full arc of Kaelen's version. He compensated instinctively, snapping his wrists to extend the radius, and the staff described a tight, whipping circle that felt—for a single half-second—exactly right.

The golden resonance flickered. Not around his hands this time, but along the full length of the staff, tip to grip, like a fast-traveling light beneath the wood's grain. It was there and gone before he could be sure he'd seen it.

He stood still for a moment, breathing.

Then he did the form again.

And again.

By the time the sun cleared the ridge and the yard filled with hard morning light, he had done the opening sequence of Kaelen's form forty-one times. He knew this not because he had counted, but because his body had begun to know the shape of it the way it knew the shape of a spoon or a step. Imperfectly. Incompletely. But *really*. The knowledge was in his legs and his wrists, not just in his eyes.

He was sweating. His arms ached with a low, satisfied burn. His feet were dirty and slightly numb from the cold earth, and there was a new scrape on his left palm from the third or fourth fall that he hadn't noticed until now.

He planted the staff and stood with it, breathing through his nose, watching the long shadows of the yard shorten.

"Train," he said quietly—not to anyone, not to himself, exactly. Just a declaration to the air. A statement of fact. This was training. He was doing it. It was real.

He heard the back door open.

Three sets of footsteps crossed the kitchen yard: the heavy, deliberate tread of his father, the light and near-silent step of his mother, and the almost undetectable placement of Kaelen's ranger's boots.

They stopped at the fence rail.

For a long moment, none of them spoke.

Kaelen studied the yard. He saw the forty-one sets of foot impressions in the disturbed dust—the resets, the recoveries, the single perfect set in the center where the boy had simply stood and breathed. He saw the scrape on Tanner's palm and the dirt on his knees. He saw the staff, and how it now lived in the boy's grip not as a prop but as a extension.

He saw that the opening sequence of his own form—a form he had spent three years learning as a young ranger—was recognizable in the boy's body. Clumsy, abbreviated by tiny arms and a two-foot staff, but *recognizable*.

Valerius rested his arms on the fence rail. He tilted his head at the impression pattern in the dust. He had done this long enough to know: no one told his son to do something forty times. No one would. He simply found the thing that was wrong and fixed it until it was less wrong. 

That was not a child playing.

That was a craftsman.

Elara said nothing. She was looking at Tanner's face—his expression of complete and quiet absorption, the way he was already studying Kaelen as if recalibrating what he'd just practiced against the original reference.

Tanner looked up at his grandfather. He tilted the staff slightly, a question and an offering at once.

Kaelen unlatched the fence gate and stepped into the yard. He reached back and unslung the dark staff from his own back. He set it on the ground against the fence—not hiding it, just setting it aside.

Then he walked to a spot opposite Tanner and planted his feet.

Bare ground. No staff of his own. Just the old ranger and the packed earth and the morning.

"Again," Kaelen said. "From the first step. I will walk it with you."

Tanner reset his grip. Left low. Right high.

He did not smile. He did not fidget. He simply waited for the count.

"And," said Kaelen.

They moved together—the old ranger's long stride and the boy's short one tracing the same shape into the dust—and the morning light caught the staff as it swept, and for a single breath the golden shimmer was bright enough that even Valerius saw it from the fence.

He didn't say anything. He just pressed his knuckle to his lips and watched his son train.
