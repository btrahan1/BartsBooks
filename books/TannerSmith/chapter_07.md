# Chapter 7: Metal Caps

The week had a shape to it, and Tanner learned the shape the way he learned everything—by feeling it until it made sense.

Dawn belonged to the yard. Every morning, before his parents had finished their first cups of tea, he was already in the practice space with the staff, walking through the forms alone. He had memorized the first sequence so thoroughly now that he could do it in the dark—and twice he had, rising before the Ranger's Moon set, drawn out by some internal alarm that had nothing to do with light. He worked on the pivot, which had been the fracture point, until the foot-catch became a controlled transfer and the transfer became invisible. He worked on the overhead drop until his wrists understood the snap that made the short staff reach beyond its length.

He fell. He got up. He ran the form again.

By the fourth morning, Kaelen had stopped demonstrating and simply watched, arms folded, face unreadable. He would speak once per session—a single correction, nothing more. *Your left heel.* Or: *The staff moves before the body, not after.* Small adjustments dropped quietly into the air and then left to Tanner to figure out. No second explanations. No praise.

Tanner took each correction the same way: he stopped, stood still for a moment as though listening to something no one else could hear, and then ran the form again incorporating the change. He never asked a follow-up question. The question was answered in the attempt.

---

Midmorning belonged to his mother's bench.

This time was different—warmer, slower, more conversational, though the conversations were almost entirely conducted through demonstration and imitation rather than words. Elara would show a stitch, slow and deliberate, narrating what her hands were doing with the same patient voice she might use to explain a color.

*"Cross-stitch is for joining. Running stitch is for guiding mana through a seam. Blanket stitch is for edges that need to breathe."*

Tanner would watch until he felt he had it, then reach for his own practice hide—a rough, tannery-grade piece of cheapest cowhide she had put aside specifically for his destruction—and attempt the same. His copies were cramped and irregular, the spacing uneven, the tension inconsistent. But they were real stitches doing real work, not a child's impression of sewing. By the third day he had stopped poking himself with the bone needle almost entirely. By the fifth, Elara noticed that he was watching the mana-grain of the practice hide the way she did, tilting his head fractionally before each stitch to find the path of least resistance.

She didn't comment on it. She just quietly upgraded his practice material to a piece of medium kidskin. 

He didn't notice the upgrade as a gift. He simply assessed the new texture, found the difference in grain-depth, and adjusted his approach. She watched him do this and thought: *He doesn't see kindness. He sees information.* It wasn't a criticism. It was the truest thing she had ever understood about her son.

---

The afternoon belonged to the forge. 

Or rather—the forge's doorway. 

Tanner had spent the first three afternoons sitting on the threshold, the staff across his knees, watching. Valerius did not alter his work to accommodate the boy. He pulled metal, struck it, shaped it, quenched it. The forge blazed and hissed and filled the air with that particular scorched-iron smell that Tanner had known since before he could walk. 

On the fourth afternoon, Valerius looked up from his work and found his son still in the doorway, watching with the same expression he used on everything—that flat, total absorption, as though every detail were being filed away in some internal ledger for future use. 

"You going to come in or just hold the frame up?" Valerius asked.

Tanner stood and walked in. He stopped three feet from the anvil—close enough to feel the radiant heat pushing against his face—and looked at the half-formed piece of iron cooling on the block. It had been a wheel-spoke. It was becoming a hinge.

He reached out a hand toward it. Not to touch—he understood heat perfectly well—but to feel the Might-flow in the cooling metal. He could see, with the Sight, how the energy of his father's strikes had been absorbed into the grain of the iron, compressing the structure, re-ordering the crystalline geometry. It wasn't chaos. It was *convincing*. The hammer had made an argument over and over, and the iron had finally agreed.

Valerius watched him reach for the heat-shimmer and simply went back to work. He understood the boy was measuring, not playing.

The fifth afternoon, Tanner helped pump the bellows. He was barely strong enough to be useful, but his timing was exact—he had counted the rhythm of his father's breath and matched the bellows pumps to it, keeping the fire steady rather than feeding it in irregular surges.

Valerius said nothing. But he stopped manually adjusting the airflow.

On the sixth afternoon—the afternoon that would matter later, when the story of Tanner Smith was told to people who had not been there to see it—Tanner walked into the forge at the start of the afternoon session, set his staff against the far wall, and walked to stand in front of his father.

Valerius was preparing stock iron for the next day. He looked down at the boy waiting patiently at his knee.

"Forge," said Tanner. He pointed to the anvil—the fire—the hammer on the rack. Then he pointed at Valerius. "Teach."

Valerius straightened slowly. He looked at the three-year-old boy who had spent a week watching him work with the focused intensity of a journeyman planning his master exam. 

"You want to learn smithing."

Tanner nodded once. Then he turned and walked to where his staff leaned against the wall. He picked it up, holding it horizontally, presenting both ends for examination. Valerius crossed to him and crouched to look.

The ends of the pine shaft were beginning to show wear. Not structural failure—the pine was still sound—but the wood at the striking tip had become fibrous and splayed from repeated impact with the rails and the packed earth of the yard. The base had a similar fraying where it met the ground during stance work. Both ends were losing material.

Tanner touched the tip with one finger. He looked at his father.

"Metal caps," he said.

Two words. A complete design brief.

Not *give me metal caps.* Not *can you make metal caps.* He had pointed at Valerius and said *teach.* He was asking to be shown how to make them himself.

Valerius sat back on his heels. He looked at the frayed ends of the pine staff—at the practical problem requiring a practical solution—and then he looked at the face of his son, who was watching him with the patient certainty of someone who has already decided what the answer is going to be and is simply waiting for the adult to catch up.

Something moved in Valerius Smith's chest that he did not have a name for. It was not exactly pride, though pride was part of it. It was more like recognition—the feeling a smith gets when he picks up a piece of raw ore and knows, in the deep bone way that cannot be taught, that what he is holding has the potential for something extraordinary if it is worked correctly.

He stood up, plucked his heaviest leather apron from its hook, and held it out.

It was comically large. It would wrap around Tanner twice and still drag the floor. 

"Put this on," said Valerius.

Tanner took the apron. He struggled with the neck strap for a moment—his arms weren't long enough to tie it behind himself—so he simply held the body of it against his front and looked at his father.

Valerius tied the back straps around twice, cinching it so the leather bunched up around Tanner's middle like a strange skirt. He took a spare kerchief from the bench and folded it into a band, tying it across Tanner's eyes and forehead to keep flying scale out. He found old leather gloves meant for his own hands and could only make the right one stay on by folding the fingers down, leaving Tanner looking like he'd suffered some kind of catastrophic laundry accident.

He stood back. Tanner stood in the mismatched outfit, staff in his gloved right hand, left hand bare and raised slightly, ready.

Valerius looked at him for a long moment. 

Then he picked up his own hammer and went to the fire.

"Watch first," said Valerius. "Then you will try. Metal caps start with understanding why iron bends and why it doesn't. You'll learn that today if it takes all evening."

He pushed a short length of thin iron rod into the coals. The forge breathed orange.

Tanner set the staff down carefully against the anvil base and stepped up to watch. He was barely tall enough to see the work surface, standing on his toes, his wrapped gloves gripping the anvil edge for balance.

He watched. He filed. He remembered.

Outside, the Ranger's Moon was already rising, and the stars of Elveron were coming out one by one, and in the workshop, the lesson that would never fully end had just quietly, without ceremony, begun.
