# Chapter 9: Happy Birthday

He heard them before he reached the bottom step.

It wasn't the sound of the forge or the particular silence that preceded his mother's most focused stitching. It was a different kind of quiet—the held-breath quiet of people waiting to surprise someone—and it had the faint, unmistakable warmth of something planned. Tanner paused on the second-to-last stair, his staff balanced upright in his left hand, and listened.

Three heartbeats. He counted by the scrape of boot on stone.

He came down the last step and into the kitchen.

"**Happy Birthday, Tanner!**"

The trio erupted—his mother's voice bright and clear, his grandfather's a low rumble that shook the cups, and his father's a bellowing enthusiasm that was slightly undercut by the yawn he was visibly fighting. Elara had strung dried autumn berries and silver-fern along the mantelpiece. There was a small cake on the table, honey-glazed and dusted with crushed rosehip—Tanner's one and only demanded indulgence—and three faces that had clearly been awake in the dark, waiting.

Tanner looked at all of it. His eyes were wide with something they rarely admitted: pure, unguarded delight.

"Wow," he said. Then—with the gravity of a very important announcement—"I'm *four* now."

Valerius pressed the back of his hand to his mouth and barely contained a laugh. "You are indeed four now. A venerable age."

"We had to get up way earlier than usual to catch you before you started training," his father grumbled, the words belied entirely by the warmth in his face. He was already fully dressed and his hair was still damp from a rushed wash at the basin. He was not, by nature, a pre-dawn person. This had cost him.

Tanner understood the weight of that sacrifice in the way he understood everything—through its evidence. He looked at his father's damp hair, his mother's slightly over-bright eyes that had not fully landed on any object in the room, and his grandfather, who appeared as rested as ever because Kaelen treated sleep as an optional exercise. The picture was clear enough.

He walked to the table, set his staff carefully against his chair, climbed up onto the seat, and looked at the cake.

"Thank you," he said.

A small word from a boy who had spent his third year learning, mostly, to mean things precisely. He said it without the theatrical warmth some children use, just directly, which made it land like something solid rather than decorative.

Elara pressed her knuckles briefly to the corner of her eye. Valerius busied himself cutting the cake.

---

They ate. Kaelen drank his reheated tea and watched Tanner consume an amount of honey cake that suggested he had not, in fact, eaten anything in the previous three days. Tanner did not usually care particularly about food. Today was a noted exception.

It was when the plates were cleared and the morning light had come fully through the window that Elara folded her hands on the table and looked at her son with the particular expression that meant a prepared thing was about to be said.

"How would you like to go on an adventure around town today, Tanner?"

The effect was immediate. The mild, sociable four-year-old who had been enjoying his cake became absolutely, vibratingly still for approximately one second.

"Really?" he said.

"Really," said Valerius.

Tanner's birthday present, as his parents had reasoned it over several long evenings, was a problem with a narrow solution. They could not give him materials—he would ask why they hadn't taught him to find and assess them himself. They could not give him tools—he would want to make them. They could not give him books, because he was four and his reading was still three months behind his comprehension. He was well ahead of most children his age and frustratingly impervious to most of what children usually wanted.

What he wanted, at his core—what his parents had understood by watching him every day of his short life—was *to know things*. To see how they worked. To find the pattern underneath the surface and hold it up to the light.

And the town of Elveron was full of patterns he hadn't touched yet.

Tanner sat up straight. Something was happening behind his eyes—a filing, a cataloguing—and when he opened his mouth what came out was not one request but a list delivered at the measured, methodical pace of someone who had been thinking about this for a very long time.

"The wood crafter," he began. "He uses a different grain-split than Father does. I've seen the boards outside. And the enchanter—the one in the Crossing District, not the High District—I can feel different patterns when we walk past and I want to know what they are. The alchemist, because Mother uses herbal preparations in her tanning baths and I don't know where they come from. The rope-maker, because the rope on my pen is a different twist than the cord on the bellows and I think they work differently and I want to know why." He drew breath. "The glassblower. The stone-mason by the east wall—his mortar isn't the same as what Father used on the forge base. The—"

"*Woah there, son.*"

Valerius held up one enormous hand, palm out. It was the hand that could stop a cart. It stopped Tanner mid-sentence with the same efficiency.

"We don't have to visit *everywhere* today."

Tanner closed his mouth. Then, with visible effort, he nodded. He knew—analytically—that a single day was finite. He was not always happy about this.

"Pick three," Elara said gently. "For today. The others will keep."

Tanner thought. He looked at his staff, as he often did when considering something carefully, as though the wood and iron had opinions worth consulting.

"The wood crafter," he said. "The enchanter. And..."

A pause. A longer one than usual.

"The rope-maker," he decided. "Because rope is practical and I use a lot of it."

Kaelen made a sound into his cup that might have been a laugh and succeeded in being neither confirmed nor denied.

"Then it's settled," said Valerius, pushing back his chair. He pointed at the ceiling—at the room above where Tanner slept. "Go change into your things. Tunic and trousers. The leather shoes you made that you like."

Tanner was already on his feet.

He took the stairs in a pace that was not running—he was careful about the staff on the stairs—but could not have been described as walking by anyone with honest intentions. He was back on the landing in four minutes.

The difference between the child who had come down at dawn and the child who reappeared was the clothes.

The tunic fit him now in the way a thing fits when it was made specifically for the body wearing it. It had started life as a painful, cramped exercise in cross-stitch and had been taken apart and re-done three times before Tanner was satisfied with the seam tension. It was a plain undyed linen—he had specifically refused the prettier blue cloth his mother offered because *he did not make the dye and he was not ready to make the dye yet*—but the collar lay flat and the sleeves were precisely the right length, and along the inner seam of each cuff there was a row of running-stitches done in his mother's silver thread that he had used to practice mana-guiding, which had the side effect of making the cuffs shimmer faintly in strong light.

The trousers were heavier—a tough canvas-weave—and showed evidence of three separate let-out alterations as he'd grown through his third year. They were slightly more advanced than the tunic in their construction, which was backwards from how most people learned. He had made the trousers second and had simply proceeded as if the difficulty the tunic had presented was no longer relevant.

The soft leather shoes were his smallest proud thing. They were elk-hide, smoke-cured to a warm, dark brown, with a sole stiffened using small iron-tack inlays that he had hammered in himself and a seam across the toe that his mother had declared, when she first saw it, *technically correct and borderline inexplicable.* They fit his feet as though they had been grown there. When he moved, they made no sound.

He came down the stairs dressed entirely in his own work, staff in hand, iron caps gleaming at both ends.

The three adults looked at him. There was a small silence.

"Right," said Valerius, who was a man who knew when a moment had occurred and when the thing to do was simply walk forward into it. He picked up his coat. "Let's go see Elveron."

Tanner stepped to the door. He reached up and put his hand on the latch—a gesture he had been doing since he was barely tall enough to reach it, always with the same quality of attention, as though the latch were a puzzle he was still considering.

He opened it.

Beyond the workshop's threshold, Elveron unfolded in the full light of morning—the calls of the market two streets over, the deep toll of the Temple bell marking the hour, the distant musical clatter of the High District's silver-thread chimes, and under it all the steady hammer-rhythm of the Low District waking up.

Tanner stood in the doorway for one breath—two—taking the whole of it in through the Sight, seeing the threads of Might and Magic woven through the town's air like rivers seen from a great height.

There was so much to know.

"Adventure," he said. And stepped outside.
