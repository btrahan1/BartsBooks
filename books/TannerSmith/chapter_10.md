# Chapter 10: The Wood Crafter

The shop sat on Bevel Street, wedged between a cooperage and a candle-maker, and announced itself before you reached the door by the smell: sawdust and resin and the particular dry sweetness of worked heartwood, a smell that had soaked so deeply into the building's stone that it probably predated the crafter himself.

A sign above the door read **ALDRIC BURNE — WOODWRIGHT & JOINER** in letters that had been carved, not painted—a small advertisement of craft in themselves, though the serifs were a little heavy on the left side where the chisel had caught a knot.

Tanner noticed the serifs. He filed the information and pushed open the door.

The shop was a world of ordered density. Finished pieces populated the floor the way a forest populates a hillside—slightly too close together, each one occupying exactly the space it needed and no more. Trestle tables ran down the center. Chests were stacked three-deep along the left wall in graduating sizes, iron hinges dark against pale oak. Bedframes leaned in the far corner like sleeping giants. A row of stools ran below the window like a congregation listening to a sermon. 

And along a rack beside the door, arranged by length and girth: the staves.

Tanner went to the rack first. It was the natural order of things—he came in with a staff, he looked at the other staffs. He set his own upright against his hip and considered the selection with the same expression he used at the forge when he was evaluating a seam.

They were smooth. Uniformly smooth, and that was actually the first note he filed against them—a craftsman's smoothness, consistent and repeatable, that came from a draw-knife and a steady hand and experience accumulated over many years. Each stave was a single piece of good straight-grained hardwood: ash, mostly, with two in what looked like hornbeam.

He picked up the ash stave nearest him—about his grandfather's height and diameter—and held it horizontally, sighting down its length the way Kaelen had shown him to check for warp. Straight. He gripped it in both hands and gave it a short, sharp flex test. The wood resisted with the confident stiffness of correctly seasoned stock. He set it back.

"Adequate," he said, under his breath. 

He had learned the word from his mother two weeks ago in a context that had involved his second attempt at a canvas pocket—the one that lay flat but whose drawstring tension wasn't quite right. *Adequate* meant: it will do the work it is asked to do, and no more. It was not a compliment. It was not exactly a criticism either. It occupied the cold middle ground of truth.

Behind Tanner, in the doorway, Valerius turned his face away and appeared to study the candles in the shop next door with great interest. Elara's mouth pressed flat in the way it did when she was suppressing something. Kaelen simply stood with his hands folded, watching the woodwright's face.

Aldric Burne had come out from his back workshop when the bell above the door rang, wiping his hands on a leather apron older than most of the furniture in the room. He was a compact man in his middle years, forearms ropy with the particular muscle that comes from a lifetime of draw-knife and mallet work, with a grey beard trimmed so precisely that it looked like it might have been jointed rather than grown. He had been expecting Valerius Smith's custom—the smith occasionally needed good hardwood for handle-blanks—and had not been expecting to be evaluated by a four-year-old with iron-capped pine and quiet, flat eyes.

He watched the boy set the ash stave back on the rack and then turn, without pause, to the chests.

And there, something shifted.

Tanner approached the nearest chest the way he had approached nothing else in the shop. He crouched down to put himself at its level—eye-to-eye with the joinery—and was still for a long moment.

It was a blanket-chest, good solid oak, sizable enough that Tanner could have climbed inside with room to spare. The lid was a single wide board, bookmatched from a split trunk so that the grain mirrored itself perfectly across the center line. The corners were dovetailed—tight, clean, the pins and tails interlocking with the kind of precision that meant Aldric Burne had been cutting dovetails for thirty years and had stopped thinking about it approximately twenty-five years ago.

Through the Sight, it was extraordinary.

The wood's Might—latent, organic, slower and older than iron's—was distributed through the oak in long, horizontal rivers that followed the grain exactly. At the corners, where the dovetails locked, those rivers crossed and intertwined and the result was a knot of compressed, still energy that held the joint not just mechanically but in a deeper way that Tanner didn't have a name for yet. The corner was *mutual*. Each piece was holding the other. Neither was dominant.

He had never seen this before. In the forge, iron was shaped by force into a new form. In the leatherwork, hides were persuaded by thread and tension. But here, the wood was doing something different: it was cooperating. Two pieces of the same tree, put back together in a new configuration, and their energies recognized each other and *held*.

He ran his fingers along the dovetail joint without touching the wood—hovering, feeling the heat-shimmer of the Might coming off it.

"How?" he asked, looking up at Aldric.

Aldric blinked. He had been woodworking for thirty-one years and could not recall a child crouching next to a blanket-chest and asking *How?* in the tone usually reserved for philosophers. He glanced at the boy's parents. Valerius gave him a look that said: *Go ahead. He means it.*

"Dovetail joint," Aldric said, carefully. "You lay out the tails on one piece, cut them, use them to mark the pins on the other, cut the pins, and drive them together."

Tanner looked back at the joint. He touched it—actually touched the corner now, fingertips on both faces of the interlocking wood. The warmth of the cooperating Might flowed into his hand.

"They hold each other," he said. Not a question.

"That's the idea, yes. A good dovetail needs no glue. Tension and friction."

"And the grain," said Tanner.

Aldric paused. He looked at the boy more carefully. "...Yes. And the grain. You want the grain running so the pieces resist in opposite directions. That's what locks it properly."

Tanner nodded, once, and moved to the next chest. And the next. He went through seven of them systematically, crouching at each one, pressing his fingers into the corner joinery. The shop was silent except for the sound of his soft leather shoes on the floor.

When he reached the last chest—a small one, cedar, with a fitted tray inside—he looked at the tools mounted on the wall beside it. The shop had a full rack visible from the floor: draw-knives, gouges, a full set of chisels graded by width, mallets, marking gauges, card scrapers, a brace-and-bit. Every tool had its place; every place had a tool. The rack itself was Aldric's design, planed flat and fitted with turned pegs.

Tanner looked at the tools for a long time.

He was building things in his mind. Tanner could feel it the same way you feel a room getting warmer—from the outside, watching his face go somewhere while his eyes stayed still. He was constructing invisible objects out of techniques he'd just seen, fitting dovetails into designs he hadn't yet touched, wondering what cedar smelled like when it was worked versus when it was finished.

He wanted to come back.

He looked at the floor.

Aldric kept a good shop, but wood shavings accumulate faster than any broom can keep pace with, and the back corner near the chest rack had a significant drift of curled oak shavings, sawdust, and what appeared to be a handful of offcut wedges that had fallen and been ignored. The broom leaned against the wall nearby with the resigned posture of a tool that knew exactly what it was not being used for.

Tanner looked at the broom. He looked at the floor. He looked at the rack of chisels.

He looked at his parents, hovering near the door with their hands clasped in the manner of people trying very hard not to interfere with anything. He had not asked them for coin before they left—had not, in fact, mentioned coin at all—and they had not offered any because they knew better.

He was not going to ask them for it now, either.

He turned back to Aldric Burne, who had been watching all of this with the expression of a man who believed he understood most things and was no longer certain.

"I would like to come back," Tanner said. His voice was polite with the particular firmness of someone who has already made a decision and is simply informing you of it. "And watch."

"Watch," Aldric repeated.

"Your work. The joinery, particularly. And the tools." He paused. "I don't have coin."

Aldric said nothing, waiting to see where this went.

"But your floor is dirty," Tanner continued, with no cruelty and no softness, only the flat accuracy of observation. "And your broom is in the corner. I can sweep." He held the old woodwright's gaze. "Every time I come. Before I watch. That would be fair."

Aldric Burne stared at a four-year-old who had just proposed a legitimate labor exchange with the seriousness of a guild apprenticeship negotiation.

He looked at Valerius, who had his knuckle pressed to his mouth again in that particular spot where he kept his feelings when they threatened to show at inappropriate times.

He looked back at the boy with the pine staff and the self-made shoes and the absolute, untroubled conviction that fair was the right way to do things.

"Come by Wednesday mornings," Aldric said finally. "Bring something you can sweep corners with. My broom's too big for the tight spots."

Tanner nodded. "I'll make something," he said, as though any other answer were inconceivable.

He picked up his staff, thanked the woodwright with the same directness he'd thanked his family for the cake, and walked back to the door.

From the doorway, he paused and looked at the chest rack one more time. The dovetails. The cooperating grain. The mutual hold.

He was going to learn all of it.

He stepped back out into Bevel Street and looked up at his father. "The enchanter next," he said. "Which way?"
