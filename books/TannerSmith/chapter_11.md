# Chapter 11: The Enchanter

He had thought about enchanting in the way you think about a word you've heard in another room—you catch the shape of it but not the meaning, and it stays with you precisely because the gap between the two nags at you.

Runes, he knew. He had seen them his whole life. The dark staff carried them like old scars, their green-lit edges nagging at the Sight every time Kaelen set it against the fence. The grandfather's bow—a long, recurved piece of yew that lived on the wall above the kitchen hearth—had three runes inlaid along the grip in pale silver, so worn with use that they were nearly flush with the wood. He could feel the old magic in them the way you feel sun-warmed stone under your palm: radiating outward, slow and steady, from somewhere deep inside.

But he didn't know what they said. He didn't know the language.

He knew what they *did*, approximately. He had heard Valerius and Kaelen discussing the bow once, late, when they thought he was asleep. His father had been asking about the upkeep costs of re-enchanting a working tool, and Kaelen had listed the properties of the bow in the tone of a man reciting a grocery list: draw-smoothing, weather-seal, and something his father had called *dubility*—

Actually, Kaelen had said *durability*. Tanner had puzzled over this later with his mother's help and decided it meant the thing didn't break easily. *Dubility* was his current working pronunciation and he had not yet been corrected because no one had heard him use it aloud.

Durability he understood. Durability was sensible. You made a good thing and then you spent more on it to make it last. That was craft logic.

But then his father had said something else. He'd said: *I heard the Crossing District enchanter put a self-repair rune on Barten's saddle. Is that even real?*

And Kaelen had gone quiet for a moment—the specific quiet of someone choosing whether to share a piece of knowledge—and then said: *The principle exists. The application is extremely difficult. It requires the rune to understand the original intent of the object. Most inscribers don't have that depth of theory.*

Self-repair.

Tanner had lain in his sleeping mat for a long time after that, staring at the ceiling, thinking about what it would mean for a piece of wood to know it was supposed to be a staff. To know its own shape so well that when it was damaged, it could find its way back to what it was meant to be.

That was not just useful. That was *alive*.

---

The bakery announced itself by smell before the shop announced itself by sign, which was a common property of bakeries and one Tanner had been mentally cataloguing as a design principle—that some things speak before they are seen, and there was information in the order of perception.

He stopped at the window.

He looked at the trays: honey-rolls, seed-twists, berry tarts in rows of six, and—on the upper shelf—small flaked pastries dusted with candied citrus rind that caught the morning light like tiny, edible windows.

He looked at these for a moment. Then he looked at his mother.

He'd had the birthday cake this morning. He was not hungry. He was thinking about the enchanter.

He put on his sweetest face.

His mother actually took a small step back. In four years she had seen Tanner's face hold concentration, curiosity, frustration, pride, and the particular blank calm that meant he was processing something very large. She had never previously seen this expression—chin slightly lowered, eyes wide and somehow both innocent and absolutely calculated—and for a single disorienting moment she wasn't sure she recognized her own son.

"Can you buy me a pastry?" he asked. "For my birthday?"

The pause that followed was occupied by Elara recovering her footing, Valerius turning toward the window display with the focused attention of a man who had absolutely no opinion about anything happening behind him, and Kaelen examining the middle distance.

"...Of course," said Elara.

They went inside. Tanner selected one of the flaked citrus pastries with the same deliberate care he used to select iron stock. Everyone had something—Elara a seed-twist, Valerius two honey-rolls consumed one immediately after the other with no apparent guilt, Kaelen a sensible cup of tea. Tanner received his pastry, turned it over once in his fingers to assess its structural integrity for transport, and then asked his mother to wrap it in the square of clean cloth she habitually kept in her work-belt.

"You're not going to eat it?" Elara asked.

"No," he said, tucking it carefully inside the fold of his tunic against his chest.

He led them back out into the street. His mother fell into step beside him. After about thirty paces she looked at him sideways.

"What is it for, Tanner?"

He considered whether to explain. He decided it was probably faster. "Women like pastries," he said simply, and turned left at the corner.

Behind him, Valerius made a sound that could not be identified as any specific emotion but was clearly intense.

---

The enchanter's shop had no fancy sign. It had a symbol burned into the lintel—a runic mark that the Sight rendered as a deeply active thing, crackling with contained energy even in the daylight—and a door painted a very specific shade of dark blue that didn't look painted so much as *decided*. 

The name above the symbol was a small, plain brass plate: *MARY.*

Tanner liked it immediately. No suffix, no title, no guild designation. Just Mary. He knocked.

The woman who opened the door was shorter than Tanner had imagined from the voice he'd heard calling to someone across the Crossing District market once. She was somewhere between his mother's age and his grandfather's, with the kind of face that had clearly once been very pretty and had settled instead into something better: interesting. Her hair was silver-streaked black, pinned up with what appeared to be two inscription styluses, and her fingers were darkly stained with the particular ink used for non-permanent rune drafting. She wore a canvas work-apron over a grey dress and was not, at this particular moment, expecting visitors.

She looked at the adults, then at the child, then at the pine staff with its iron-capped ends.

"Smith family," she said. She had good eyes. The iron caps were unmistakably forge-work, and the staff's grain-warmth was perceptible even from the doorway to anyone with even a moderate Sight. "And the boy is Tanner, I take it."

"Yes," said Tanner. He reached into his tunic and produced the wrapped pastry, which he held out with both hands. "Happy to meet you. This is for you."

Mary looked at the pastry. She looked at him. She looked at the adults—Elara's expression of amused resignation, Valerius's visible internal struggle, Kaelen's complete composure.

"A gift?" she asked.

"A courtesy," Tanner said. "I want to ask you things, and I don't have coin, and I can't sweep your floor today because it's my birthday. So I brought a pastry instead."

The corner of Mary's mouth did something complicated. She took the pastry with both hands, holding it the same careful way he'd handed it over.

"Well," she said. "Come in, then."

---

The interior of the shop was an education in organized obsession. Every surface held something: stacked inscription plates, sheets of vellum covered in draft runes held flat by small river-stones, reference volumes bristling with markers, jars of the various metallic inks arranged by volatility rather than color. Hanging from the ceiling on fine copper wire a set of demonstration pieces turned slowly in the air from the door-draft—a gauntlet, a small shield, a length of braided rope, each one glowing in the Sight with different flavors of active rune-work.

Tanner stopped under the gauntlet and looked up at it. He could see the lines of inscription along the knuckle-plates—the runes were simple here, a teaching example rather than a working piece—and for the first time in his life, he could see the structure of an enchantment up close.

It was like a sentence. Not a word, not a fragment, but a complete thought written in a language made of light and function rather than letters. The runes directed the mana through the metal in a defined path—here compressed, here expanded, here held steady—and where the mana went, the metal changed. It didn't glow more. It *became more itself.* The steel in the inscribed sections was simply more steel: harder, more cohesive, responding to impact with a rigidity that hadn't been in the original forging.

"Durability," Tanner said, pointing up at the gauntlet.

Mary glanced up. "That's right. Hardening inscription. Simple one—three runes, linear flow. What you'd put on mining tools or boots."

"And there—" he pointed to a different rune on the wrist-plate "—that's different."

Mary looked at the wrist-plate properly. "Yes. That's a conditioning rune. Keeps the metal from becoming brittle in cold temperatures. Different problem, different solution." She tilted her head. "You can see that distinction?"

"The flow changes direction at that one," Tanner said. "It goes back toward the source instead of through."

A small silence. Mary looked at him the way Aldric had looked at him, but with slightly different information behind her eyes. Aldric had seen a child who asked good questions. Mary was seeing something she categorized differently.

She pulled a stool to the worktable and set the pastry carefully beside her drafting board.

"Sit," she said. "Not you, parents. Him. This may take a while."

Kaelen sat down on the bench by the wall with the air of someone who had been expecting exactly this. Valerius leaned against the doorframe to the back room and crossed his arms. Elara found a window ledge.

Tanner climbed onto the stool and met Mary at eye level across the drafting board.

"You want to know about enchanting," Mary said. It was not a question.

"I want to know about self-repair," Tanner said. "But I don't know enough about the other things yet to ask about it properly. So I need to know more first."

Mary picked up a drafting stylus. She started, very simply, at the beginning.

An inscription was a command. It told the ambient mana of the world what to do when it flowed through an object. Simple inscriptions gave simple commands—*be harder, be warmer, be lighter.* Complex inscriptions gave complex ones—sequences, conditions, priorities, responses. A self-repair inscription was a specific kind of highly complex command: *if damaged, return to prior state.* 

But to return to a prior state, Mary explained, the inscription had to carry a memory of that state. And a memory required that the inscription had been written at a moment of perfect understanding—when the inscriber knew every thread of the object's being and could record it exactly.

Tanner was very still for the duration of this explanation. He was doing the translating work behind his eyes — taking Mary's words and mapping them against what the Sight showed him of the gauntlet turning overhead. He could see it. The command-structure of the existing runes. The places where more runes could be added. The shape of a more complex sentence.

"So to write the memory," he said slowly, "you have to understand the thing. All of it."

"Every property. Yes."

"And you couldn't write the memory for someone else's object, because you don't understand it well enough."

Mary paused. This was not usually where four-year-olds went with the explanation. "In most cases, no. The inscriber's understanding transfers with the inscription. If the understanding is shallow, the memory is incomplete, and the repair fails."

Tanner looked down at the drafting board. He looked at his staff, leaning against the stool beside him.

He thought about the dovetails cooperating in the wood-crafter's shop. He thought about his own staff—the pine, the iron caps, every failed blank and every blister—and how the Sight had blazed gold when he fitted the caps because the materials *recognized* each other.

If he wrote a memory into an object he had made himself, from beginning to end—an object every atom of which he understood intimately—

He did not say this out loud. He was four years old and he was only beginning to know what he didn't know.

But something clicked into place in his mind with the clean, solid certainty of a good dovetail seating home, and he thought: *someday.*

He looked up at Mary. "Thank you," he said. "Can I come back? I don't have a broom offer. But I can draft copy." He pointed at the stacks of inscription reference sheets. "My letters are mostly good now."

Mary looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up the pastry and took a small, careful bite.

"I'll let you know when I need copy," she said. "Come back in the spring."

Tanner nodded and slid off the stool. He picked up his staff. He looked one more time at the gauntlet turning overhead in its copper-wire spiral—the simple, linear hardening inscription, the conditioning rune at the wrist.

*Adequate,* he thought.

But only for now.
