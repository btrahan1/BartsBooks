# Chapter 13: Glass

He had said he only wanted to look through the window.

He had not specified *for how long.*

The glassblower's shop occupied the corner where the side-street met the back lane of the Crossing District's market row, and it announced itself not by smell—the way the cordage shop and the bakery had—but by *sound.* A low, sustained breath emanated from inside, rhythmic as sleeping, but hot. And then a pause. And then the breath again, longer this time.

Tanner positioned himself at the large front window, set his staff upright, and pressed his face close enough to the glass that his breath fogged it at the bottom.

He had thought he was coming to look. What he found was that looking was going to take a while, because what was happening on the other side of the window was the most impossible thing he had seen in his entire four years.

The glassblower was a slight woman with her hair wrapped in a cloth and her face sheened with heat. She stood before a furnace that was running hotter than his father's forge—visually hotter, the kind of heat that made the air above it ripple in continuous waves. At the end of a long iron pipe she held in both hands was a globe of something that should not have existed: it was orange as molten iron, fluid as honey, and it was *clear.* 

Not clear the way water was clear. Clear the way nothing physical had any right to be. He could see the light passing through it while it moved, while it dripped slightly under its own weight, while she rotated the pipe to keep it centered. It was simultaneously the softest and the hottest thing in the room.

She put the pipe to her lips and blew.

The globe expanded. Slowly, impossibly, the hot liquid stretched outward from its center, responding to her breath the way his mother's silk thread responded to tension—by going where it was guided, by taking the shape of the force applied to it. She stopped. She rotated. She blew again, longer this time, a low and steady exhalation that had clearly been practiced until it was as consistent as a bellows.

The globe became a sphere. Then she was moving it to a different station, tools in hand, and Tanner lost the thread of the process because the next steps were too fast and too unfamiliar for him to follow without context.

But he had seen enough.

*Sand,* he thought. He had heard this somewhere—that glass came from sand. He pulled the information from its filing place and held it against what he was seeing. *Sand. Heated past all reason until it becomes liquid. Then shaped by breath and tools. Then it cools and becomes... this.*

He pressed his palm flat against the shop window.

The glass was cool under his hand. Solid. It did not flex or give. But through the Sight it was the strangest thing he had ever looked at—neither the slow geological Might of wood nor the compressed argument-energy of iron. The glass held its mana perfectly still, like a held breath, suspended between the liquid state in which it had been shaped and the solid state it now wore. It wasn't *settled.* It was arrested. Locked mid-transformation and held there by the cooling.

*It is forever almost something else,* Tanner thought. *It is always just barely not liquid.*

Which meant—

He frowned slightly, working through it.

Which meant that the mana inside it was not peaceful. It was patient. There was a difference. Iron that had been quenched carried the memory of the strike, compressed and resolved. Glass carried the memory of *flow,* suspended and unresolved, which meant that any new energy introduced to it would find that patient potential and—

He didn't know yet what it would do. He didn't have the knowledge to complete the sentence. He filed the beginning of it—*glass holds potential in suspension*—and accepted that the rest would have to wait.

He looked at his mother's decorative pieces in his mind. Two small bottles of pale blue-green, a shallow dish that caught the light and threw it in long bands across the table at midday. Pretty. He had always known they were pretty. But only now, standing at this window, did he see *why* they were pretty: the light didn't just reflect off glass. It went *into* it. The light entered and moved through the arrested potential inside and came out changed, bent, colored by the experience of passing through something that was still almost liquid.

*Women like those things,* he thought, and for the first time understood that it wasn't mere decoration—or not only that. It was that women, on the whole, seemed to appreciate things that were doing more than they appeared to be doing. That looked simple and were, on examination, very complicated. He filed this as a general principle worth remembering.

But his mind was already moving to the other question, the more pressing one, and he had to find the word for it first.

*Prac...* he began, inside his head. *Prac-ti...* He thought of his mother saying it—the way the syllables sat in her mouth, even and deliberate. *Practical.*

*What practical use is it?*

He turned the problem over and considered it the way he'd considered rope on the walk here, giving it his full, systematic attention.

A container. Obviously. Glass made better containers than clay for certain things, because you could see what was inside it without opening it. The alchemist's shop—which they hadn't visited yet—probably used them constantly. He stored this application under *future relevance* and moved on.

Magnification. He had heard this word once from Kaelen—the old ranger describing a lens used in a Far-Distance scout kit, a curved piece of glass that made distant objects appear close. Tanner thought about his Sight, and how it let him see the mana-grain of materials in fine detail. He thought about what it would mean to combine that ability with a piece of curved glass that magnified the physical surface of the material while the Sight showed him the mana beneath it. 

He thought about small work. Inscription work. The fine rune-lines Mary had shown him, the precision required to place them correctly on the knuckle-plate of a gauntlet.

If you could see the material better—

He pressed his forehead to the window glass briefly. It was wonderfully cold against the heat of thinking.

*And heat-focus,* he continued. The lens could be used the other way—not to magnify what you were looking at, but to concentrate what the sun was looking at. He had seen Kaelen start a fire once with nothing but a drop of still water on a piece of bark by holding it at just the right angle. Same principle. Glass could hold that angle better. More precisely. For longer.

*Starting fires without flint. Concentrated heat for detail-work too fine for the forge. Seeing what the eye cannot normally see.* 

He added them to the list and looked up at the shop door.

Valerius's hand landed on his shoulder. A light hold—not pulling him away yet, just present.

"You said window," his father said.

Tanner looked at the door. He looked at the window. He looked at the glassblower inside, who had begun the long slow cool-down of her latest piece, holding it in a controlled environment away from the furnace's direct heat.

"I know," Tanner said.

He did not argue. A deal was a deal; he had said *window* and he had meant it at the time. That he had discovered, in the act of looking, that an hour's proper conversation with the person inside would have been better than a week's looking from outside was not the glassblower's problem or his father's problem. It was merely something that was true, which he could act on at a later date.

He would come back. He didn't have a service to offer yet—he didn't know enough about the craft to know what he could do that would be useful. That was, in fact, a prerequisite: he needed to know the outline of a trade before he could offer anything to it. So he had to do more window-looking first, and the window-looking had to be enough for now.

He stepped back from the glass. There were the clear rectangles of his palms, fogged just at the edges, and the small circle where his forehead had rested.

He looked at his mother. She was watching the glassblower through the window too, and her face had the soft, particular expression she wore around beautiful things—not the sharp attention she gave to her own work, but something quieter, more personal.

"I'm going to make you something," Tanner said, to her.

"You already made me something," Elara said gently.

"Something else," he said. "When I know how." He turned back one more time to look at the globe the woman inside was setting in its cooling cradle—the perfect sphere of arrested light that was forever almost liquid. "Something that catches the sun."

Elara reached down and took his free hand. Her fingers were warm against his forge-cooled palm.

"I'll look forward to it," she said.

The bells of Elveron began again—the seven-o'clock cascade this time, rolling west across the valley—and Tanner let himself be led back up the side-street toward home and dinner, his staff in one hand and his mother's fingers in the other, his mind already stacking the day's acquisitions into their proper shelves for the night's quiet contemplation.

Four shops. Four sets of new edges. And one promise that would take years to keep.

He was already, in his careful and methodical way, beginning to figure out how.
