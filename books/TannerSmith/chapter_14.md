# Chapter 14: The Future

The birthday dinner was warm and unremarkable in all the best ways.

Elara had made a lamb stew that had been sitting since morning, the broth gone deep and amber, and there was a round of bread that Valerius had clearly been waiting to tear into since they got home. The kitchen smelled of rosemary and the faint, clean ghost of sawdust that had somehow followed them back on Tanner's clothes. The lamp at the center of the table was the good one—the tall brass one Valerius had forged as a wedding gift for Elara, which only came out on notable evenings.

Tanner ate. This was itself an event, since he often treated meals as interruptions to things he was in the middle of thinking, but the stew was good and the bread was better and the day had been long enough that his body had decided to take what it needed without consulting his mind first.

They talked about the shops. Valerius did an impression of Aldric Burne's face during the *adequate* assessment that made Elara cover her mouth with her bread. Kaelen described, with precise and accurate cruelty, the apprentice rope-maker's ears going red. Tanner listened to all of this without protest, because it was accurate and he did not see the point of objecting to accuracy.

It was at the end of the meal—plates cleared, the lamp burning lower, the stew-pot moved to the back of the fire to stay warm for morning—that Kaelen set down his cup and looked at Tanner across the table with the particular quality of attention that meant something considered was coming.

Tanner looked back and waited.

"Tanner," Kaelen said. "What do you want to do with your life? What do you want to *be?*"

The question settled onto the table like something with weight. 

Tanner looked at it. He did not answer immediately—he never answered immediately if the question deserved better than that, and this one very clearly did. He set his own cup down, folded his hands in his lap, and thought.

The family waited. This was a thing they had learned over four years: when Tanner went still like this, the correct response was to match it. Valerius had picked up the analogy once, privately, for his own satisfaction—that interrupting Tanner mid-thought was like opening the forge door during a controlled heat. The work wasn't ruined, but you lost something you couldn't fully name, and the metal was never quite as true as it would have been.

So they were still. Elara had her hands wrapped around her cup. Valerius sat with his elbows on the table, looking at his son. Kaelen watched with that patient, deep-forest quality of attention that had no particular expectation in it, only presence.

The lamp burned. Outside, a dog barked twice in the Low District and was answered, then silent. The coals in the forge settled with a faint tick.

---

Inside his mind, Tanner was working.

The question was *what do you want to be,* which was different from *what do you want to learn* and different again from *what do you want to do tomorrow.* It was asking about the shape of an entire life, and he understood—in the abstract, numerical way he sometimes understood things that were emotionally large—that most four-year-olds were not expected to answer this question with genuine analysis.

He had approximately ten years before the world would formally ask him to choose. He knew this because he had watched the older boys in the Low District begin their formal apprenticeships at fourteen, fifteen at the latest, and he had asked his father once when that would happen for him and Valerius had said *when you're ready and the craft is ready for you.* Which was not very specific, but was honest in the way his father was always honest, and he had filed it as *approximately ten years* and moved on.

Ten years was a long time. Long enough to learn a great many things. He had already identified, this very day, four more crafts he needed to understand, and there were others on the list before that. The alchemist. The glassblower properly, not just the window. The stone-mason. The cooper. The people who made the things that the town used to exist.

But ten years was also not forever. He would have to choose something, eventually. Some spine around which everything else would be organized.

He thought about the wood crafter. The satisfaction of dovetails, the grain cooperating with itself. He could be a woodwright. He had felt the pull of it clearly.

He thought about the enchanter. Mary, and the inscription-command language, and the word *someday* that had clicked into place so firmly when she explained self-repair. He could be an inscriber. He had felt that pull too.

He thought about the forge. The hammer that made an argument until the iron agreed. He thought about the forge every morning when he woke up, the way other children thought about favorite food or sunny weather. He could be a smith. He was already, in the smallest and most preliminary sense, a smith.

He thought about the rope-maker—about *controlled conflict creates strength*—and about his grandfather's forms in the practice yard, and the way the staff felt when it moved correctly, extending him outward rather than being swung by him.

He thought about what had brought all of these things together today. Not the crafts themselves, but the reason he cared about them. The reason he had spent his fourth year making things instead of simply having them. The reason he had built his own staff, forged his own caps, stitched his own clothes, swept the woodwright's floor in exchange for the right to watch.

He was not making things to have them. He was making them because a person with their own tools was a person who could not be disarmed. A person who understood their own armor could not be underdressed. A person who made their own weapons understood exactly how far those weapons could reach and what they could and could not do, and that understanding was the difference between someone who picked up a sword and someone who was a sword.

He thought about Elveron. He had walked through it today with different eyes—new eyes, four-years-and-a-day eyes instead of just-four-years eyes—and he had seen something he hadn't had the context to see before. The town was full of people making things, good things, beautiful things. And the town was also full of people who could not protect those things. Who relied on the walls at the valley's edge and the watchmen on the gate and the Guild of Acquisitors' distant, mercenary scrutiny. Who lived at the mercy of forces that didn't especially care about them.

He thought about his mother's glass bottles, catching the afternoon light.

He thought about Kaelen's warning: *there are people who collect such things.*

He thought about who stood between those people and the things worth keeping.

He thought about what *he* wanted to be. Not what he would make. Not what craft he would devote himself to. But what he would *be,* in the world, when all the making was done and the question came down to the simplest version of itself:

*What are you for?*

He did not think about this last part quickly. He turned it very slowly, the way you turn a casting in the last cooling, looking for the flaw that would make everything else academic.

He found it wasn't a flaw. It was an answer.

---

Fifteen minutes had passed.

Kaelen's tea was cold. Valerius had not moved except to breathe. Elara's hands were still on her cup and her eyes were on her son with an expression that was not anxiety—she knew him well enough not to be anxious—but something softer and more uncertain than her usual steadiness, as though she were standing at the edge of something large and could not yet see what was over it.

Tanner looked up.

He met each of them in turn—his mother's eyes, his father's, his grandfather's—with a directness that was at once completely childlike and completely not.

"I want to be a Protector," he said. His voice was quiet and serious and had no tremor in it, the way a well-made joint has no give in it.

The word sat on the table.

Kaelen was the first to move. He uncupped his hands from around his cooling tea, very slowly, and set them flat on the table. His jaw had tightened. His eyes were extraordinarily bright.

Valerius said nothing for a long moment. He looked at his son and then he looked at the ceiling—at some point above them, past the ceiling, as though consulting something further away—and when he brought his eyes back down they were clear and certain in the way iron is clear and certain when it has been correctly worked and cooled.

He reached out and put his hand over Tanner's, which were still folded in his lap. The enormous hand covered both of them entirely.

"That's a big job," Valerius said.

"I know," said Tanner.

"You'll need to know a great many things."

"I know," said Tanner.

"More than we can teach you ourselves."

"I know," said Tanner. "That's why I started today."

Elara made a sound. It was short and involuntary and she pressed her fingertips to her mouth immediately after, but it had already happened—a compressed, bright thing, halfway between a laugh and something that wasn't a laugh at all.

Kaelen was still looking at his grandson. When he finally spoke, his voice was lower than usual, with something in it that Tanner recognized from the kitchen table the night after the doll—that particular weight of a man speaking after long thought.

"A Protector does not only hold a weapon," Kaelen said. "He holds a line. Between what matters and what would end it." He paused. "Do you understand what you are saying?"

Tanner considered the question. He thought, briefly, about the glassblower's arrested moment of liquid light. About the dovetail's mutual hold. About rope's controlled conflict bearing what neither force could hold alone.

"I think a line is the same kind of thing," he said. "It only works if both sides of it matter."

Kaelen was quiet for a very long time.

Then the old ranger reached out and put his hand on top of Valerius's hand, which was on top of Tanner's hands, and the three of them sat like that for a moment in the lamplight—the enormous forge-hand, the ancient ranger's hand, the small and calloused hands of a boy who had decided, at four years old, what he was for.

"Then we have work to do," Kaelen said at last.

Outside, Elveron breathed in the night. The Low District's hammers were silent. The High District's chimes had stilled. And in a kitchen on the middle street, the lamp burned quiet and steady as Tanner Smith closed his eyes, leaned his head briefly against his father's arm, and slept—for the first time in weeks—without dreaming of things he hadn't learned yet.

He had enough to hold for one night.
