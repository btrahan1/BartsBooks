# Chapter 15: The Fifth Year

A year is a long time when you are working.

Tanner Smith did not waste an hour of it.

The structure of his days had crystallized after the birthday tour of Elveron and held that shape through all four seasons with the stubborn integrity of a properly dovetailed joint. Dawn was the yard—always the yard, regardless of weather. He had learned, by the middle of autumn, to run his forms in cold rain without his footing betraying him, and by winter he was doing them in snow-melt mud that would have humbled a cavalryman. Kaelen watched and corrected. The corrections grew further apart. The form grew longer, the second sequence added, then a third—a flowing, deceptive middle movement that was Kaelen's particular innovation, designed so that every strike concealed the next one.

The staff had changed again. Tanner had sealed the grain of the pine with a boiled linseed treatment he had prepared himself from seed-oil and studied heat-times, and the wood had taken on the warm, amber gleam of something that had been properly cared for. He had wound a grip-wrap at the balance point using cord he had twisted himself at Corran's shop—hemp-core braided over a cotton inner, the counter-twist tight enough to give traction without bulk. He had re-seated the iron caps twice as his skills improved sufficiently to be critical of his first work, and the second set were notably better than the original: thinner walls, more precisely fitted, with a subtle shoulder-ridge around the socket to prevent lateral movement.

He had inscribed the word *hold* in small, careful rune-letters on the inside of each cap before seating them, following Mary's instruction on basic inscription theory. He did not know yet whether it had worked—a Sight-trace of the caps suggested it had done *something* to the iron's mana-state, though what specifically would take more study to determine. He had noted this in the small book he had begun keeping, a double-cord-bound thing of cheap paper he had assembled himself, where each page held whatever he had learned that day and what questions it had opened.

The book was already half full.

---

Mornings at the leatherwork bench had moved past the cross-stitch by the end of autumn. His mother had introduced him to the running and blanket stitches, then to gusset construction—the engineering of volume into flat material—then to the particular challenge of fitting: measuring a living, irregular body and translating those measurements into a flat pattern that became, when assembled, a three-dimensional garment. He had spent three weeks on the fitting problem alone, making a succession of practice armatures from cheap canvas, adjusting each one against a carved wooden form Valerius had knocked together for the purpose until the armature sat with no puckers, no gaps, and no binding.

By spring he had made himself a second pair of trousers—significantly more sophisticated than the first, with a gusset-seated knee that allowed full extension without tension—and had begun work on something more ambitious: a long vest of cured elk-hide, built for movement, with a pattern of ventilation slits along the torso that he had calculated would allow airflow during hard exertion without compromising coverage. He had no name for this kind of garment. He had simply worked out, from first principles, what he would need to wear in order to fight effectively once Kaelen's weapon-forms demanded full range of motion, and then made it.

His mother watched him sew the ventilation-slit binding with the good silver thread and said nothing for a long time. Then: "That's armor design, Tanner."

He had looked at it. "Yes," he agreed. "Soft armor. A beginning."

---

The forge afternoons were where he had grown the most, and the most painfully.

Iron is patient with no one. But it had, over the course of the fifth year, developed something that might have been described—by a romantic—as a grudging respect for the small hands that kept returning to it. Tanner had not grown much in height—he was still one of the shorter children his age in the Low District, a position he was indifferent to—but the months of bellows-work and hammer-practice had changed his upper body. There was real muscle in his shoulders now, not a child's rounded softness. His grip had developed the particular density that comes from sustained work, and his hammer-calluses were in the right places, which Valerius had pointed out was the first useful indicator that a student was striking correctly.

He had made the iron caps for the staff, remade them, and then made several other small pieces: hooks for his mother's leather-drying rack, three pairs of pliers of his own design that he used for the leatherwork, a small pair of shears. Each piece was functional, none were elegant, all were his. He had ruined at least four times as many pieces as he had finished and had never, not once, lost his composure over a ruined piece. He simply quenched the failure, assessed what had gone wrong, and began again.

Valerius had told him, at the end of the winter: "You have a journeyman's patience. Most journeymen don't find it until their third year."

"I'm not a journeyman," Tanner had said.

"No," said Valerius. "You're something else."

---

Wednesdays were Aldric Burne's floor, and then Aldric Burne's workbench.

The sweeping arrangement had expanded, as it was always going to, because Aldric was not the kind of man who wasted available resources—and a child who could identify a bad dovetail by touch and was meticulous with a broom was not a resource to be wasted. By winter, Tanner was sanding. By spring, roughing out spoon-blanks that Aldric would finish. By summer, he had been set at a small dedicated station with a selection of chisels sized appropriately for his hands—not children's tools, but adult tools with shortened handles—and had begun doing, under close supervision, the early stages of box-joinery.

He had also, quietly and without making a production of it, begun making his own tools. The first was a small marking gauge—the measuring implement used to scribe lines parallel to an edge—because the standard one was too large for precise work at his scale. He had forged the metal fence himself, carved the wooden stock from a piece of close-grained fruitwood he'd selected from Aldric's offcut bin, and fitted the adjusting wedge with a precision that made Aldric pick it up and test it three times before handing it back without comment. The comment was in the fact that he handed it back.

---

He had made his arrangement with the glassblower—whose name was Senna, which he had finally learned—in early spring, after three additional months of window-watching had given him enough vocabulary to propose something useful. Her furnace needed the fire-clay lining of its opening replaced every season, a tedious and precise job that required patience rather than strength. He could wedge and set the clay segments with a level of careful attention that Senna's apprentice—an older boy who was talented at the blowing itself and indifferent to maintenance work—had demonstrated only inconsistently.

He had, therefore, become the person who maintained Senna's furnace lining. In exchange, he stood at the bench beside her during the slower sessions and learned.

Glass was, as he had suspected on his birthday, a craft of entirely different principles from everything he knew. Metal was persuaded by force. Leather was persuaded by tension. Wood was persuaded by patience and the grain's own preferences. Glass was persuaded by *breath*, by rotation, by the exact pace at which you allowed it to cool, and—most critically—by the willingness to commit completely to a shape before the material made the decision for you.

He had ruined his first fourteen pieces. This was not unusual for a beginner. What was unusual was how he ruined them: the first three were failures of strength—he simply didn't have enough lung-capacity to expand the gather before it cooled past workable. The next four were failures of geometry—he could expand it but couldn't maintain the symmetry of the rotation. The next seven were failures of decision—he had the gather at the right temperature and the right shape and then hesitated at the moment of committing to the final form, and the glass didn't forgive hesitation.

Senna had watched all fourteen failures and had said, at the end of the seventh session: "You know what's wrong before I tell you. What are you waiting for?"

"I'm trying to understand it before I commit to it," Tanner said.

"Glass doesn't work that way," Senna said. "Glass is already committed. You only decide whether to follow."

He had thought about this for a long time before his next session. He had turned it against the rope-maker's principle—controlled conflict, neither side winning—and against the dovetail's mutual hold, and against the thing his mother had told him about the running stitch that guides mana through a seam.

And then he had understood: glass was not persuaded at all. Glass was caught at the exact moment it was willing to be something, and in that moment you had to be the shape you wanted it to become. Not guide it there. *Be it.*

His fifteenth piece was a recognizable bowl. His twentieth was a closed-neck bottle, asymmetric but intentional. His twenty-eighth was a swan.

---

He had been making the swan for three sessions, carrying the shape of it in his mind between visits until it lived there as completely as the forms in the practice yard. He knew what a swan looked like—there were three on the mill-pond at the valley's edge, white and effortlessly imperious—and he had studied them extensively because Tanner Smith did not attempt to make something he had not studied.

The result was not elegant. The neck was slightly too thick, creating a study, heavy bird rather than the fine-boned grace of the real thing. The wings were asymmetric—the left one swept higher than the right. The tail-fan, which he had been most ambitious about, had partially collapsed during the transfer to the cooling cradle, giving the swan a slightly surprised expression that was almost certainly not intentional.

But it was a swan. Unmistakably. A serious, dense, slightly astonished swan with the weight of something made rather than found.

When he had lifted it from the cradle at the end of the session, his hands had been shaking—not from fear, but from the accumulated tension of three sessions of intention finally released. The Golden Resonance had come as he'd expected it would, the same clean, copper-edged warmth that came whenever his work recognized him as its origin. He felt it pour down through his hands and into the glass, settling into that arrested mid-liquid state the way water settles into stone.

He had not expected what happened next.

The swan had begun to glow.

Not light like fire—not orange or red. A light like the moment just before dawn, when the sky is still dark but very faintly, unaccountably luminous. The arrested mana-potential in the glass, meeting the Golden Resonance, had found the resolution it had been waiting for—not cracking back to liquid and not surrendering to dead solid, but arriving at a third state entirely: *held light.* The swan was storing the energy the same way it stored its shape: by being always almost something else, and in that constant almost, radiating.

Senna had not spoken for quite some time when she saw it.

"That doesn't happen," she had said finally.

"I know," Tanner said. He had wrapped it carefully in clean cloth.

---

He presented it on the morning of his fifth birthday, before training, before anyone had quite woken up properly.

His mother unwrapped it at the kitchen table and held it in both hands. In the early-morning light, the glow was faint—a nighttime phenomenon, primarily—but the warmth of it was present even in day, a radiating steadiness like a lamp-flame seen through heavy curtain.

Elara looked at the asymmetric wings. The sturdy neck. The slightly surprised tail.

Her eyes filled exactly the same way they had filled over the first doll, fourteen months ago.

"It's a swan," Tanner offered, in case this was not apparent.

"It's beautiful," she said.

He decided not to argue with her assessment, because she was looking at the thing inside it, not the shape of it, and she was right about the thing inside it.

That night, she put it on the windowsill of the kitchen. In the dark, the swan glowed with its quiet pre-dawn light, steady and self-contained, casting no shadow. It would glow every night for the rest of her life, and she would never move it from that window.

---

When the family asked what he wanted for his birthday—gathered at the table again, cake again, the brass lamp again—Tanner did not take fifteen minutes this time.

"Healing," he said. "I want to learn more about how that works."

He looked around the table—at his father's forge-scarred hands, at his grandfather's old injuries held quietly in the way he favored his left side in cold weather, at the world beyond the window where people broke and bent and came apart.

"I might not always be able to prevent harm," he said. "So I need to know how to heal it."

The table was quiet.

Kaelen reached across and put his hand over Tanner's.

"I know someone," he said.
