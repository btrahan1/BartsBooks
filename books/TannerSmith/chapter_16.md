# Chapter 16: Grandmother

The word *healing* had opened a door that none of the adults had been certain they were ready to walk through.

Elara was the first to speak, which was often how it went when the room needed someone to move it forward. "The Temple of the Radiant Hand has a healing order," she said carefully. "Brother Aldous has been good to the district. I've seen him work on the dockworkers after accidents. He's—"

"No," said Kaelen.

The word was quiet and absolute. Not unkind, but with the specific texture of something that has been considered before and decided.

Elara looked at her father. "He's five."

"I know how old he is." Kaelen set his cup down. He looked at Tanner, who was sitting at his end of the table with the birthday cake untouched before him and an expression of complete, undemonstrative attention. Then he looked at Elara. "What does Brother Aldous heal with?"

"Faith," she said. "Prayer. The light of—"

"The light of the gods," Kaelen said. "The power passes through him, Elara. It comes from above and he opens himself to receive it and it moves through his hands. He is a conduit. He has trained all his life to make himself a worthy one, and he is good at it—yes. But the healing is not his." He looked at his granddaughter. "Tell me I'm wrong."

Elara was quiet for a moment. She was not a woman who argued when she didn't have an argument. "You're not wrong," she said.

"Holy healing is borrowed power," Kaelen said. "It comes from the Heavens, not from within. Yes, it takes years of devotion, of study, of reshaping the self into something the divine will work through. I don't diminish that work. But the power at the end of it is not the priest's. It never was. And our Tanner—" he glanced down the table again—"would no sooner channel another's power through his hands than he would wear another man's armor into battle."

The table was quiet.

Tanner had not moved. He was listening in the particular way he listened when something important was being said—not just with his ears but with his whole posture, absolutely still, storing everything.

"Then what," Elara said, carefully, "are you thinking?"

Kaelen looked at the middle distance for a moment. His fingers turned his cup once, deliberately. When he spoke, he was looking at Elara.

"Your mother," he said.

The silence that followed was a different kind than the comfortable quiet between ideas. It had weight and edges. Elara's face changed—not dramatically, because she was her father's daughter and didn't scatter her reactions—but something moved behind her eyes that was old and complex and not fully resolved.

"How long has it been?" she asked. Her voice was even.

"Ten years," Kaelen said.

Valerius had not spoken since Kaelen had dismissed the Temple. He sat with his elbows on the table and his hands loosely folded, and the set of his shoulders held a particular quality that Tanner had learned to read over five years of close study: it was the way his father held himself when he was carrying something he had not put down.

Guilt. The posture of a man who knows exactly how a thing happened and knows that his hands were on it.

Elara's mother was a druid. Tanner had not known this—had not known, in fact, that he had another grandparent living. The information settled into his mind without drama, slotting into place beside everything else he was filing. A grandmother. A druid. Ten years of distance. And his father's shoulders holding that particular weight.

He was five years old and did not yet have all the words for what he was understanding, but the Sight worked on people the same way it worked on materials, in its quieter way, and what he saw in his father was not complicated once you looked at it properly: Valerius believed that he was the reason the ten years had happened. That by taking Elara away—to Elveron, to the Low District, to a human blacksmith's life—he had put distance between his wife and her family that had grown, in the wordless way these things do, into something that now required deliberate effort to cross.

*Will she accept him?* Valerius asked, and his voice was steady because he was a man who did not show what cost him unless he chose to. But the question was not really about Tanner. Or it was about Tanner and also about more than Tanner.

Kaelen looked at his son-in-law for a long moment.

"She is a druid," Kaelen said. "She lives in Thornwood, in the old grove chapel. She has spent ten years talking to trees and mending broken animals and teaching the young ones how to listen to what the earth says." He paused. "She is not angry, Valerius. She is waiting. There is a difference."

Valerius looked down at his hands. He turned them over once, looking at the hammer-calluses on his palms, the forge-scars across his knuckles. Tanner watched his father's face and saw him doing the particular kind of accounting that has nothing to do with numbers.

"I should go with you," Valerius said. "When you go."

"Yes," said Kaelen simply. "You should."

Elara had her eyes fixed on the swan on the windowsill—the one her son had made, glowing its steady pre-dawn light into the dark kitchen. Her expression held something that might have been anticipation and might have been the careful holding of an old hope she had not been certain she was permitted to have anymore.

"She'll love him," Elara said softly. It was not a question and it was not entirely addressed to anyone at the table.

---

Tanner had been quiet throughout all of this.

He had listened to every word of it with the comprehensive attention he brought to everything that mattered. He had filed: grandmother, druid, Thornwood, ten years, the grove chapel, the fault his father carried, the hope his mother was being careful about. He had cross-referenced: druids, from what he knew, worked with nature's own power—the living systems of root and rain and growing things—which meant their healing was not borrowed from above but drawn from the same world the patient inhabited. The power was not a conduit's. It was a conversation.

That was different. That was learnable. That was something he could, in principle, earn the understanding of rather than simply receive.

He had also noticed one other thing. His grandmother was a stranger. Not just to him—to this house, to this table, to the ten years of his father's life that had been built here. Walking into a stranger's world empty-handed was something Tanner had not done since before he could hold a tool.

He would not start now.

He pushed back his chair.

The three adults looked at him.

"I need to make something," he said. He was not agitated, not excited in the way children are excited by new information. He was simply clear, the way iron is clear when it has been heated and has decided what it's going to be. "I will be away for a week. I'll sleep at the woodworker's shop."

"Aldric's?" Valerius asked.

"Yes. I already know where everything is." He paused. "I'll leave in the morning. Before training."

"What are you making?" his mother asked.

He picked up his staff from beside his chair. He looked at the three of them—at the old estrangement and the guilt and the careful hope sitting at the table together—and he thought about a grandmother who had spent ten years in a grove chapel waiting, and who was a druid, and who did not yet know that her grandson existed as anything other than a name.

He thought about what you bring to a stranger who has been waiting.

"I'll know it better when it's done," he said. "But I know what it needs to be."

He looked at his mother last, and something passed between them that was too old for a five-year-old to be carrying and too real to deny—the particular understanding between Elara and Tanner that lived mostly in the space between words, where two people who see things very clearly sometimes find they have arrived at the same place without having to go together.

He nodded once. She nodded back.

"Tell her," Tanner said quietly, "that I'm coming."

He was up the stairs before anyone thought of a reply.

They sat at the table in the lamplight. The swan glowed in the window. The forge ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs, the sound of a book being moved—of pages being reviewed, of materials being considered—told them that whatever their son was making had already begun in his mind, and was further along than they might have expected.

Valerius reached across the table and found Elara's hand. She turned her palm up and held it.

"Ten years," he said. It was an apology and a statement of fact and a thing he had been carrying, all in two words.

"She knows," Elara said.

"That doesn't—"

"I know." She pressed his hand. "But she knows."

Outside, the night settled over Elveron and the valley beyond, and somewhere past the valley's edge, in a grove chapel among the old trees of Thornwood, a druid who had not seen her family in ten years was—or so the family chose to believe—beginning, in whatever way druids begin such things, to sense that the world was about to shift in the direction of something good.

Tanner was already making the thing that would carry them to her.
