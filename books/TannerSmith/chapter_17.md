# Chapter 17: The Druid's Gift

He arrived at Aldric Burne's door before dawn, which was not unusual. What was unusual was that he had his sleeping roll under one arm, his tool-roll under the other, and his staff strapped across his back like a soldier shipping out.

Aldric, who was already awake because woodwrights rise with the dark, opened the door and took in the entirety of this picture.

"Good morning," said Tanner.

"Come in," said Aldric.

He put the kettle on, because it seemed like the right response to a five-year-old appearing on your doorstep before sunrise with a week's worth of kit. Tanner set his sleeping roll in the corner of the workshop behind the chest rack—he had already identified this as the most practical corner: out of the main work path, close enough to the banked coals of the stove to be warm, and shelving enough for his tools—and began unpacking with the organized economy of someone who had thought about where everything would go before they arrived.

Aldric put tin cups on the table and waited.

Tanner sat across from him, folded his hands, and looked at the woodwright with the serious, preparatory expression that, by now, Aldric had learned to recognize as the precursor to something that was going to require concentration.

"I need your help making something," Tanner said.

"All right," said Aldric.

"It's a gift. For a druid."

Aldric poured the tea. "I've made furniture for the Thornwood chapel before. Some of their pieces. They like simplicity—natural finishes, honest joints. Not much decoration."

"This is different," Tanner said. "It's a presentation box. About—" he held his hands apart, indicating a volume roughly the size of a loaf of bread— "this large. Hinged lid. Interior lined with silk and velvet."

Aldric nodded slowly. This was within the realm of work he understood. "Good grain-matched oak would suit nicely. I have some in the—"

"The wood can't have been cut from a living tree," Tanner said.

Aldric paused with his cup halfway to his mouth.

"The wood must be gathered," Tanner continued. "Fallen branches. Deadfall. Wood the tree gave up on its own. No blade may have severed it from living wood. I need to find the right piece myself."

Aldric set his cup down. "You want to make a box out of deadfall."

"Yes."

"Deadfall is—" Aldric chose his words with the care of a man navigating a professional objection— "inconsistent. It can be punky. Dried unevenly. Some of it is half-rotted, some of it is split. Finding a piece structurally suitable for joinery work is—"

"I know," Tanner said. "That's why I need a week."

A pause. "Go on," said Aldric.

"The box itself needs to be shaped without any cut severing good material. I can use rasps, gouges, sand—removing material by the grain instead of cutting across it. The joinery should be done the same way. I was thinking of a fitted-rabbet lid rather than a dovetail, because I can work a rabbet without a saw if I use the shoulder-plane with its iron set very fine."

Aldric was quiet for a moment, running through the woodworking logic of this. The boy was not wrong. A rabbet joint—a stepped recess that the lid sat into—could technically be made with chisels and a shoulder-plane alone, avoiding the ripping saw entirely. It was an unusual method. It was also, if executed with sufficient precision, entirely sound.

"The interior," Tanner continued. "Silk cloth, then velvet over it. I'll need to ask my mother for the velvet. I have silk cord I made myself but not sheet silk." He paused. "I'll need to borrow from her. I'll pay it back."

"How?" Aldric asked, in the spirit of genuine inquiry.

"I'll make her three gusset panels for her autumn commission. She's been putting them off." He had clearly already worked this out. "And inside the velvet—" he paused for the first time, not because he was uncertain, but because he was being precise about sequencing— "I need to blow a glass rose. I'll go to Senna's shop on day three or four."

Aldric Burne stared at him. 

"You want to make what," he said.

It was the same question he'd started with, delivered in exactly the same tone, but now it contained a year's additional weight.

"A glass rose," Tanner said. "Petals that look like they're still opening. The stem should be a single pulled gather. I've been thinking about how to manage the petals—if I introduce a small amount of air at the base of each petal while it's still workable, they should curve outward naturally. Senna will know better than I do."

"You want a box," Aldric said slowly, "made from wood no blade has ever cut from a tree, with a velvet interior, and a glass rose inside."

"Yes."

"For a druid."

"Yes. She's my grandmother. I've never met her."

There it was. Aldric sat with his hands around his cup and looked at the five-year-old across his work table and felt the particular sensation—which he had experienced three times before in his life, all three involving exceptional craftspeople—of standing at the beginning of something that was going to outlast both of them.

"Tell me about the wood," he said finally. "What are you looking for?"

Tanner reached into his tool-roll and produced his small, cord-bound book. He had already written three pages of notes the night before—Aldric could see the careful, dense script without reading it—and now he turned to a rough sketch he had made. A plan view. Elevation. Material notes.

"I need wood with real age in it," Tanner said. "Not young. Old-growth if I can find it. Deadfall from something that stood for a long time. The grain will be tighter. The Might in it will be more settled—deeper. A druid will be able to feel the difference." He looked at the sketch. "Kaelen says there's a grove at the valley's eastern margin where the old oaks drop branches every autumn. Some of them are quite large and haven't rotted because the canopy keeps the rain off." 

"Fallow Grove," Aldric said. He'd taken some pieces from there himself, years ago. "The oak there is—" he searched for the word— "unusual. Very dense. The rings are tight enough that the grain almost looks like stone."

"That's what I need," Tanner said.

Aldric drank his tea. He thought about his week. He thought about a druid grandmother in Thornwood who had not, apparently, met her grandson yet, and about a five-year-old who had arrived at his door before dawn with a sleeping roll and a plan drawn in a hand-made book.

"You'll need someone to walk you to Fallow Grove," Aldric said. "It's three miles."

"Kaelen is coming at dawn," Tanner said.

Of course he was.

"And the velvet," Aldric said.

"My mother will bring it by this evening."

Aldric looked at the sketch a moment longer—the clean lines of the box plan, the rabbet joint indicated with a fine-drawn dotted line, the interior measurements already calculated to accommodate a glass rose of approximate dimensions. He thought of the marking gauge Tanner had made himself, now living in its own slot in the tool-roll across the room, from fruitwood and hand-forged iron, more precisely calibrated than half the tools in the professional supply cabinet.

"Right," said Aldric. He stood up. "Drink your tea. You'll need to be awake for Fallow Grove. That ground's uneven."

---

Kaelen arrived at first light. He said nothing when Aldric opened the door—just looked at the woodwright with the quiet eyes of a man who had been expecting to say something and found it had already been said.

He led Tanner out of town in the grey morning, east along the mill road and then off-path through the season-bare field margins until the ground dipped and the old trees closed in overhead. Fallow Grove was not a forest—it was more a memory of one, a stand of oaks so old that their trunks had grown wide enough to be rooms and their canopy had merged into a single dark ceiling three stories up.

The floor was deep in leaves, and in the leaves were branches. Some small and worthless. Some partially sunk and half-rotted. And some—the good ones, the ones that had fallen from significant height and impacted and split in ways that left interior wood exposed and seasoning beautifully in the dry air under the canopy—were worth the walk.

Tanner did not collect the first branch that looked right. He walked the grove for two hours, moving slowly, crouching to assess pieces, touching them and looking through the Sight at the mana settled in the grain. Kaelen followed, silent, hands clasped behind his back, watching the boy work.

At the end of the second hour, Tanner found it.

It was not large—a branch-scar piece, where a significant limb had broken away from one of the biggest oaks years or decades ago, leaving an angled, gnarled section of dense heartwood sitting in the leaf-litter. It was roughly the right size. It was dry through. And the grain—the Sight showed it as a deep, slow current, barely moving, the energy of something very old that had been still for a very long time.

Not passive. *Patient.*

Like his grandmother, he thought, and the thought arrived without intention and was more accurate than anything he could have constructed deliberately.

He picked it up with both hands. The Golden Resonance did not come—not yet, not until he worked it. But it recognized him, the way all honest materials recognized honest attention, and it was warm in his hands despite the cold of the morning.

"This one," he said.

Kaelen looked at it. He looked at his grandson. He did not say *good choice* or *well done,* because both would have been smaller than the moment.

He simply turned and led the way back to Bevel Street, and behind him Tanner Smith carried a piece of ancient fallen oak toward the workshop where he would spend a week turning it into something worthy of giving.

The glass rose could wait until Thursday. He had the box to think about first.
