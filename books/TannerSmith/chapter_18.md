# Chapter 18: The Gift Box

He set the heartwood on Aldric's largest work table and stood in front of it.

Aldric had cleared the surface without being asked, moving his current commission to the wall-bench with the wordless efficiency of a craftsman who understands that some work needs room. Kaelen had settled himself on the window bench with his tea and made himself part of the wall. Neither spoke. The morning was coming up blue and cold through the high windows, and the lamp on the corner post burned a steady amber against it.

Tanner looked at the heartwood.

It was, objectively, a piece of debris. An angled section of oak branch-scar, roughly fourteen inches at its widest point, irregular in cross-section, with a spiral figure running through the grain where the branch junction had been under years of wind-stress. The surface was weathered silver-grey on the outside and, where it had split at the point of its long-ago falling, dense amber-brown at the heart. It smelled of deep time and dry leaves.

He did not touch it.

He stood at the edge of the table with his hands at his sides and he looked.

A minute passed. Then another. Aldric found a piece of shaping to do on a stool-leg across the room and did it very quietly. Kaelen looked out the window. The lamp burned. The coals in the stove breathed.

Five minutes. Seven. Ten.

At the fifteen-minute mark, Tanner's posture changed fractionally—not that he moved, but something in the long line of his back and shoulders arrived at a different quality of stillness. He was not looking at the wood anymore in the way you look at a problem. He was looking at it the way you look at someone who is telling you something and you are waiting for the sentence to finish.

The Sight showed him everything.

The mana in the old heartwood was not like the mana he had seen in iron or in newer wood or in the gathered materials of his leatherwork. It was slow—glacially, geologically slow—the compressed patience of something that had grown for perhaps two hundred years and then spent another ten or twenty on the forest floor, settling further into itself, becoming more entirely what it was. The energy didn't flow through it. It resided in it, the way heat resides in hearthstone at midnight, present but still.

And in that stillness, through the Sight, he could see shapes.

Not the shape of the box he intended to make—not yet, not precisely. But the grain-lines and the figure and the way the wood's internal Might had organized itself around the old stresses of growth and wind told him where the material was densest, where it was most coherent, where a worked surface would close smooth and where it might feather. He was reading it the way his mother read hide before cutting, finding the natural drape and tension-lines before the needle ever touched it.

The box lived in there already. He could see approximately where it was. He just had to find it.

He nodded. Once, the quiet nod of a conclusion reached.

Then he reached out. His hands settled on the heartwood's surface—not gripping, just resting, the same way he rested his hands on his staff when he was thinking rather than training. He closed his eyes for a moment. The warmth of the old wood came through his palms, slow and settled and enormously certain of itself.

"Okay," he said quietly. "I see it."

He picked up the heartwood in both hands, turned it once, and set it back down in a slightly different orientation—rotated perhaps twenty degrees, which placed the densest section of the figure centrally where the box's bottom would be.

He looked at Aldric.

"Rasps first," he said. "And the large gouge. Can I use your carving vice?"

"It's set up by the window," Aldric said, as if they'd been in the middle of a conversation the whole time.

---

The first day was removal without decision.

This was a concept Aldric had heard described but rarely seen practiced—the working discipline of taking material away from the outside of the form rather than defining the inside. Tanner began with the coarsest rasp in the set, a half-round bastard with teeth that could each be felt individually, and went after the weathered exterior of the heartwood with a patience that had nothing tentative in it. He was not trying anything. He was revealing.

The grey surface came off in curled shavings and fine dust. Beneath it, the amber-brown of the heartwood emerged like a tide coming in—darker at first, with the grain close and tight where decades of compression had packed the rings to near-invisibility, and then lightening as he went deeper into the piece where the wood was still almost wet with its own resins.

He worked by touch as much as by sight. Every few passes of the rasp he stopped and ran his fingertips across the surface, assessing what the tool had done and whether it had done it in the right direction. The grain of the old heartwood had its own preferences—it wanted to close smooth in one direction and wanted to feather in the other—and learning those preferences was the first work, before any shaping could begin.

Aldric came to look twice during the morning. The first time he looked at the emerging form of the wood and said nothing. The second time he picked up one of the shavings from the bench—a long, tightly-curled one from a particularly well-directed pass—held it to the lamp, and set it back down.

"That's good heartwood," he said, which was all he said.

"I know," said Tanner, without looking up.

---

The second day, the box began to exist.

He had rough-established the exterior form by the end of the first day—the overall dimensions correct, the major surface planes present if not finished. On the second morning he switched to finer rasps and then to gouges, working the interior. This was the harder part.

Excavating the interior of a box without sawing it open required removing the lid-section as a single clean piece—which Tanner achieved by scoring a deep channel around the perimeter with the marking gauge he had made himself, then using a series of graduated gouges to deepen the channel until the lid could be separated by careful leveraged pressure rather than cutting. This left a ragged fit that had to be refined by long, careful passes with the shoulder-plane he had borrowed from Aldric's rack—the finest-set iron in the shop, adjusted so that each pass removed a shaving thin enough to read through.

He fitted the lid and unfit it seventeen times, checking the gap each time with the same light touch he used on forge work. Late on the afternoon of the second day, it seated with the low, decisive sound of two surfaces that have been persuaded to agree with each other.

The Golden Resonance came then, quiet and tentative—a thread of it along the joint line, not blazing as it had when the iron caps seated on the pine, but present. He was not done yet. It knew it was not done yet. But it was marking its intention.

---

The third day was the interior and the surface work.

He had spoken with his mother the evening before—she had brought the velvet in person, a square of deep forest green that she had been saving for something she hadn't yet found the right use for—and she had looked at the rough-shaped box for a long time before saying anything.

"The figure in the grain," she said finally.

"Yes," he said. He had been hoping she would see it too.

In the lamplight of the shop, with the grain opened up by the rasp-work, the spiral figure of the old branch-junction ran through the box exterior like a slow river seen from height. It was not straight. It was not symmetrical. It was unmistakably the record of a living thing that had grown the way it needed to, not the way it was told, and the spiral of it was beautiful in the specific way that only unselfconscious things are beautiful.

"She'll see this," Elara said, meaning her mother.

"That's why I chose it," Tanner said.

On the third day he scraped the exterior surfaces to their final smoothness using a card-scraper he had made from a piece of worn-out file steel and a fragment of leather for backing—a tool Aldric had taught him to use, and which in the right hands left a surface finer than sandpaper could achieve. He did not oil or wax the wood. He had decided, after thinking about it, that a druid should receive it bare—the wood as natural as he could make it, honest in its texture the way the forest floor was honest.

The interior he lined with the thin sheet-silk his mother had provided, cut and fitted without a single pin-hole through the wood, held instead by a woodworking adhesive he made from boiled hide-glue he had mixed himself, which he applied with a tiny brush to the bare interior surfaces before pressing the silk smooth with a curved piece of bone. Over the silk went the velvet, fitted into the corners with the same gusset-logic he used in leatherwork, so that it sat without puckers in the angles.

When the velvet was set and dry, he pressed his palm flat into the interior and closed his eyes. The forest green velvet was warm and dense. The old heartwood was warm around it. The Golden Resonance moved through both—finding the silk and the velvet as new things, not his in the way the wood was his, but borrowed from his mother whose work was half his origin, and settling with them with the same warmth it had found with the salamander-hide tunic two years ago.

*Given,* not *borrowed.* It accepted them.

He opened his eyes. 

On the fourth morning, he would go to Senna's.

The box was ready to receive its rose.

He set it on the corner of Aldric's work table and looked at it from a distance for a while — the silver-grey exterior, the spiral figure, the lid that seated perfectly silent. It was not decorated. It was not polished. It was clean and honest and old, and it was waiting.

Aldric stood beside him, arms folded, looking at the same thing.

"You know," the woodwright said, after a time, "in forty years I've had three people come through this shop who worked wood the way you do."

Tanner looked at him.

"The other two," Aldric said, "were sixty and seventy-three respectively."

He uncrossed his arms, picked up his own work, and went back to his station.

Tanner picked up his staff and went to find his sleeping roll. Tomorrow was the rose.
