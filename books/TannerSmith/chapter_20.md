# Chapter 20: Not Finished

The dinner table at the Smith house had witnessed some significant moments in the past year.

This was another one.

Tanner had been quiet through the whole meal—quiet in the deep, interior way rather than the polite social way—and the family had given him the silence he was clearly using. The lamb was eaten, the bread was finished, the lamp burned down an inch before he finally set down his cup and looked at the middle of the table.

"It's not finished," he said.

The silence that followed was complete—not the comfortable quiet of a family that had run out of things to say, but the alert quiet of three people who had recognized the particular weight in those three words.

His mother was the first to speak. She had seen the box that afternoon, had turned it in her hands and looked at the rose through its cotton wrapping and had spent a long time very still in the way she was still when something moved her beyond the scale of ordinary response.

"Tanner," she said, softly. "It is exquisite. She will love it."

He shook his head. Not dismissively—he did it with the sad precision of someone correcting something they wish were true.

"It doesn't show her everything," he said. "It doesn't tell her the complete story of who I am."

The table was quiet. Valerius had his hands around his cup. Kaelen was watching his grandson with an expression that had gone very still and very attentive, which usually meant he was several steps ahead and was waiting to see how far behind him the conversation would catch up.

"What else is there?" Kaelen asked carefully.

Tanner looked at his grandfather. Then he looked at the middle of the table again, which was where he looked when he was organizing something complex into the order he wanted to present it in.

"Runes," he said. "I want this box to last forever. And to preserve the rose forever." He paused. "Druids live a long time, from what I understand."

No one disputed this.

"I want this gift to last at least that long."

The lamp flickered once in a draft from the window crack and settled.

Valerius turned his cup slowly on the table. "Preservation inscriptions," he said. It was not really a question.

"Yes. Two runes on the box exterior—one for the wood, one for the interior and the rose. They should be written into the material, not surface-applied." He had clearly been thinking about this since before dinner, probably since before dinner yesterday. "The wood is old enough to hold deep inscription—the Might in the grain is settled enough to anchor the rune-lines without distortion. I worked out the placement this afternoon. Along the base-edge, where they won't interfere with the grain-figure."

Elara reached across and touched the back of his hand. "But that is Mary's work, Tanner. You've studied theory with her—"

"I'm not going to ask Mary to do it," he said.

The table was quiet again.

"No," Kaelen said, not disapproving—just confirming, with the tone of a man recognizing the internal logic of a position. "He's not."

"The box is mine," Tanner said. "Everything in it is mine. If Mary inscribes the runes, they are hers—her understanding, her mana-transfer, her intent locked into my wood." He looked at his mother. "You know what borrowed strength is."

Elara looked at her father.

"He's right," Kaelen said simply.

"He's five," Elara said, with the specific helplessness of a mother who is watching her child be completely correct about something that should be impossible.

"When has that stopped him?" Valerius said—not unkindly, and with the corner of his mouth doing something that wasn't quite a smile but was in the vicinity of one.

Tanner had been following this exchange with patience. When it came to its natural end, he continued.

"I've been studying inscription theory with Mary for almost a year. I can draft the rune-forms accurately. I understand the flow-path principles." He paused. "But I've never inscribed a live rune. I know the theory but I don't know the transition. Where the mark on the surface becomes the command in the material." He looked at Kaelen. "That crossing is what I don't know how to do yet."

"And you want Mary to teach you that crossing," Kaelen said.

"Yes. Not to do it for me. To teach me the technique. So that I can put my own understanding into my own work." He looked around the table. "That's different."

It was different. Everyone present understood that it was different, because they had all spent years living in the philosophy of a boy who had very clear ideas about where the line was between instruction—which made your own work better—and dependency—which replaced your own work with someone else's.

Teaching was the family business. In every sense.

---

He went to Mary the following morning.

He had thought about what to bring and had arrived, by the same calculation he had applied to the glassblower's basket, at the conclusion that what Mary would most appreciate was not food—she was well-supplied for that—but the thing she had mentioned wanting when they first met.

His letters were good.

He arrived at her door with a fresh quill, a bottle of his own preparation of iron-gall ink—he had made it himself from oak galls and iron vitriol and gum arabic, working from a recipe in one of Aldric's reference volumes—and twelve sheets of his finest paper, made by cutting and pressing a batch himself from cotton rags and dried flower-pulp the week prior, which produced a page with better tooth than the standard market stock for taking inscription ink.

Mary opened the door, looked at the ink and the paper, and stepped aside without a word.

Inside, he laid it out on her drafting table. She examined the ink by holding the bottle to the light, then touched the paper and rubbed her finger against the surface to assess its texture.

"You made the ink," she said.

"Yes."

"And the paper."

"Yes."

She set the paper down. She looked at him with the particular expression she had worn since their first meeting—the one that combined professional assessment with the faint, wry quality of someone who keeps being surprised by the same person and has decided to simply allow it.

"Inscription theory," she said. "You've learned it here. But you've never crossed to live inscription."

"No."

"Sit down," she said.

He sat. She pulled her own stool to the opposite side of the drafting table and looked at him directly—the full, considered attention of a teacher who has decided something.

"Crossing to live inscription is not more complicated than what you already know," she said. "In some ways it is simpler, because the rune is no longer a drawing—it becomes a question. You ask the material whether it will accept the command. If you have understood the material correctly, it will answer yes."

Tanner was listening with both the ears and the Sight.

"Your difficulty," Mary continued, "is that most students who come to me haven't spent years genuinely understanding the materials they want to inscribe. They've memorized rune-forms and they know the basic flow-paths and when they try to cross to live inscription they encounter the material's resistance, because the material knows it is not understood." She picked up one of his fresh sheets of paper and held it. "A material that is fully known has almost no resistance. It is already prepared to be something more than it is."

She set the paper down and met his eyes.

"You made that box," she said. "You found the wood. You worked it for three days with hand tools. You fitted the lid seventeen times. You lined it with your mother's materials that you know as extension of your own." She paused. "Tell me: when you pressed your palm into the finished velvet interior, what did you feel?"

He did not have to think about this. "The Golden Resonance came," he said. "Quiet. Just confirming."

"That confirmation," Mary said, "is the material telling you it's ready. It already accepted you. The inscription is not forcing a new relationship—it's deepening an existing one." She looked at him steadily. "You are, in my professional opinion, almost certainly the most prepared first-time inscriber I have ever had at this table. The theory is already in you. The relationship with the material is already there." She stood up. "The crossing is simply the moment you trust both of those things enough to press the stylus down and mean it."

She crossed to her tool-rack and selected a fine inscription stylus with a mithril tip—the kind used for dense hardwood, where the tip needed to carry the inscriber's mana-signature cleanly through high-resistance grain.

She set it on the table in front of him.

"We'll practice on scrap oak first," she said. "Until it feels like finishing rather than starting."

Tanner picked up the stylus. It was the right weight. He turned it once in his fingers and felt the mithril tip—warmer than steel, with the faint, ready hum of a material that existed specifically to carry intent from one place to another.

He looked at Mary.

"How many tries?" he asked.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she went and got the scrap oak from the bin beside her bench, set a piece on the table, mixed a small amount of inscription ink in a ceramic well, and sat back down.

"With a normal student," she said, "I would say: as many as it takes."

She looked at him. "With you, I am genuinely curious."

He dipped the stylus. He looked at the scrap oak—at its Might through the Sight, at the settled, hospitable grain of a material that had already agreed to be worked by human hands. He thought about the box waiting at Aldric's. He thought about a grove chapel in Thornwood where a druid with long years of patience had been waiting a different kind of time.

He pressed the tip to the wood.

He meant it.

The rune-line went in clean.
