# Chapter 22: The Journey

They left before dawn on a morning that smelled of wet stone and the last exhale of autumn.

Tanner had packed his own kit the night before — sleeping roll, tool-roll, water-skin, the fire-steel he had made himself, three days of trail bread he had baked with his mother the previous afternoon. His staff was strapped across his back in a carry-harness of braided hemp he had twisted and stitched specifically for the purpose, which freed both hands for walking and left the iron caps pointing skyward where they wouldn't catch on undergrowth.

The gift was wrapped in three layers: the linen, then a padding of soft cotton batting, then a fitted wooden carrying-box that Aldric had produced without being asked, planed smooth and fitted with a leather strap. Tanner had accepted this without protest — the carrying-box was not the gift, it was packaging, and packaging was a practical matter rather than a philosophical one. He carried it himself. No one offered to carry it for him, because no one in the family made that kind of mistake anymore.

Valerius walked with the particular gait of a large man who had not been on a forest trail since he was a young ironworker doing delivery runs to the hill-country, which was to say: heavily, but with more endurance than you'd expect. Elara walked the way she did everything — quietly, precisely, with an awareness of the ground under her feet that was half-elven instinct and half a lifetime of working with materials that punished inattention. She had packed light. She had also packed a second dress, which was the only concession to concern about appearing before her mother that she had permitted herself.

Kaelen walked the way the forest walked — which is to say, without appearing to make any particular effort, without any particular sound, and with the kind of ground-covering pace that seemed leisurely until you looked back after an hour and realized you were four miles from where you'd started.

The road out of Elveron followed the valley floor for the first half-day, past the mill and the farms and the stone bridge over the Cut River where the water ran clear and fast over slate. Then it rose into the hill-country, and the trees began, and by mid-afternoon they had passed beyond the territory Tanner had ever seen.

He looked at everything.

---

Kaelen began teaching on the first morning the way he began everything — without announcement, simply changing the texture of what he was already doing until it became instruction.

"Look at the trail ahead," he said, as they passed through a stand of birch where the path narrowed. "What do you see?"

Tanner looked. Leaf-litter, a half-covered stone, the impression of something in the soft earth beside the path.

"A track," he said. "Something with a cloven hoof. Deer?"

"Size?"

Tanner crouched and looked at the depth and the spread. "Young doe. Moving left to right. Not running — the prints are evenly spaced."

"When?"

This was harder. He looked at the edges of the print — how much the leaf-litter had fallen into it since it was made, how much the moisture had softened the detail. "Yesterday? Before the dew fell."

Kaelen nodded once. That was how Kaelen praised — a single nod, given when the answer was correct and no further commentary was required.

They walked on. By noon, Tanner was reading the forest floor the way he read the surface of iron in the forge — as a continuous record of events written in a language that became legible once you knew the alphabet. Fox scat at the base of a holly. A badger's claw-marks on a fallen log. The scrape of antler-velvet on bark, high up, which meant a full-grown stag passing through and not afraid.

"Your feet," Kaelen said, sometime in the early afternoon.

Tanner looked down at his own movement. He had been walking the way humans walk — heel-strike, roll forward, toe-off. It was efficient on flat ground but on the trail it was loud, each step announced by the compression of leaves and the snapping of small twigs.

"Watch," Kaelen said. He walked ten paces ahead and came back. The forest had not registered his passing. No leaves compressed. No twigs surrendered.

"Toe first," Tanner said, watching. "Weight forward. You're testing the ground before you commit to it."

"The ground will tell you what it can hold if you ask before you step," Kaelen said. "Most people step first and listen after. By then the sound is already made."

Tanner adjusted. His first twenty steps were clumsy — toe-leading on uneven ground required a different kind of balance, the weight held higher, the knees more bent. By the fiftieth step he had smoothed the transition. By the hundredth he was not silent the way Kaelen was silent — that would take years — but he was notably quieter, each footfall a question rather than a statement.

Valerius, behind them, continued to walk like a man shaped by anvils and was entirely unapologetic about it.

---

The evening lessons were fire.

First fire was the work of the experienced — Kaelen built it on the first night, talking through every decision as he made it. The clearing of ground. The selection of tinder: dry birch bark, shredded fine, because birch bark carried its own oils and would catch from a single spark. The architecture of kindling — stacked not in a heap but in a graded cone, thinnest at the center, each layer slightly thicker, creating a chimney-draft that drew the flame upward through progressively larger fuel.

The fire-steel. The angle of the strike. The placement of the spark.

"Fire is not made," Kaelen said, as the tinder caught and the first thread of smoke climbed into the evening air. "Fire is invited. You prepare the conditions for it and then you offer the spark. Whether it comes is between the spark and the tinder."

Tanner, who had spent a year tending the forge, understood this principle at a level deeper than Kaelen perhaps expected. The forge ran hot because the conditions were maintained — the bellows fed air at the right rate, the fuel was graded, the draw was managed. Take any element away and the fire sulked or died. It was not obedient. It was cooperative, and only when the conditions justified cooperation.

Second fire was Tanner's, on the second night. He built it carefully, applying Kaelen's principles with the same systematic precision he applied to every new skill. The fire caught on the first spark. Kaelen said nothing, which was the nod's equivalent in verbal form.

By the third evening, Tanner was in charge of everything.

He selected the campsite — a depression between two fallen logs with natural wind-break and dry ground beneath a dense canopy that would keep the dew off. He gathered tinder and fuel, grading it and stacking it in reach-order. He built the fire with a single strike. He strung the cook-pot from a green-wood crossbar over forked sticks he had cut and seated with the small hatchet Kaelen had lent him.

He prepared dinner — trail stew from dried meat and the wild onion and wood-sorrel he had identified along the afternoon's walk, following the herbalism instruction Kaelen had woven into the day's lessons. He knew yarrow for wound-cleaning, now. He knew comfrey-leaf for bruises and sprains. He knew the broad, fuzzy leaves of mullein that could be steeped into a tea for coughs, and the dark berries of elder that were medicine when cooked and poison when raw.

He knew which mushrooms to leave alone entirely, because Kaelen's instruction on that topic had been the only time in the journey his grandfather's voice had carried genuine sharpness.

After dinner on the third night, Tanner banked the fire the way Kaelen had shown him — buried in its own ash with a core of slow-burning hardwood that would hold coals until morning — and then sat for a while looking at the stars through the canopy gaps.

"You're a natural in the woods," Kaelen said, from across the fire. It was the longest compliment Tanner had received from him in five years.

"I like solving things," Tanner said. "The forest is just a different kind of workshop."

His grandfather made the sound that had graduated, over the course of five years, from a mystery to a medium-distance laugh to what it actually was: the sound of a man who loved something very much and did not know how to say so except sideways.

---

The days settled into a rhythm. Walk. Learn. Make camp. Sleep. Walk again.

Valerius and Elara fell into step together in the middle of the line, and something happened between them during the long walking hours that was quiet and significant — the kind of conversation that happens in glances and adjusted pace and the occasional touch of a hand to a shoulder, two people preparing themselves for something they had postponed for ten years.

Tanner noticed. He noticed most things. He gave them the privacy of not commenting.

The forest changed as they went deeper. The birch gave way to ash, the ash to old oak, and the old oak eventually — on the sixth day — to something older still. The trees here were enormous. Their trunks were wider than Aldric's workshop. Their canopy was so dense that the forest floor was open and cathedral-quiet, the undergrowth unable to compete for light and simply surrendering the ground to deep moss and ancient fern.

Through the Sight, the Might of this forest was unlike anything Tanner had seen. It was not the settled patience of the deadfall heartwood or the slow rivers of Aldric's working oak. It was *alive* — massively, anciently, overwhelmingly alive — a continuous, interconnected system of root and trunk and canopy that pulsed with the deep, slow breathing of something that had been growing since before Elveron existed.

Tanner walked more quietly here. Not because Kaelen told him to, but because the forest itself seemed to ask for it.

---

On the morning of the eighth day, the path — which had been narrowing for two days — opened without warning into a wide clearing bordered by standing stones that were so covered in moss they looked like green pillars. The light came through differently here, filtered by the canopy into long, shifting columns.

They had taken perhaps twenty steps into the clearing when the scouts appeared.

*Appeared* was the operative word. They did not emerge from behind trees or step out from cover. They were simply not there, and then they were — two figures in mottled green and brown, longbows drawn to half-tension, arrows nocked and pointed at the approximate center of the approaching group.

They were elves. Both of them had the angular precision of feature and the unsettling stillness that Tanner recognized from his mother and grandfather — the quality of being very present while appearing to not quite be there at all.

They saw the family. Their bows lowered by half an inch.

They saw Kaelen. The bows came down entirely.

"It has been a while, sir," the taller one said. Her voice held the particular texture of respect mixed with surprise — the sound of someone greeting a person they had not expected to see again but had stories about.

"It has," Kaelen said. His voice was different here — or rather, the same voice Tanner had always known, but in a place where it fit differently, the way a tool fits differently in the hand that made it than in the hand that borrowed it. He was home. Or close to home. Close enough that the forest's enormous, slow-breathing Might seemed to recognize him the way the heartwood had recognized Tanner.

"We are here to see Sylara," Kaelen said.

The scouts looked at each other. The taller one looked at Valerius — at his human build and his forge shoulders and his hammer-calluses — and her expression held a calculation that was swift and professional and entirely without hostility.

Then she looked at Tanner.

He was standing between his parents with his staff across his back and the carrying-box held against his chest in both hands. He was looking back at her with flat, calm, interested eyes. He did not flinch from the bow. He did not hide behind his father. He stood and looked, and filed her, and waited.

"The boy," the scout said.

"My grandson," Kaelen said.

Something passed between the two elves — not words, just a glance — and the taller one stepped aside and gestured toward the path beyond the clearing, which led deeper into the ancient wood toward a place Tanner could not yet see but could feel, through the Sight, as a gathering of enormous green energy that pulsed in the forest like a second heartbeat.

"She's expecting you," the scout said.

Kaelen did not look surprised. He walked forward into the grove.

The family followed.
