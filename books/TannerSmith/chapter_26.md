# Chapter 26: Anatomy

The classroom was not a room of stone and desks, but a hollowed section of a root-node, high enough in the Great Tree that the air felt thinner and tasted of sunlight. 

The walls were living wood, the grain smoothed by decades of gentle mana-shaping into natural shelves and benches. Sunlight filtered through translucent patches in the bark, Casting long, soft-edged shadows across the floor. In the center of the space stood a tripod made of dark, polished ironwood. It held a man-sized diagram.

It was not a drawing of a person. It was a Venn Diagram.

Three massive circles overlapped in a complex geometry of intersections. One was the deep, pulsing red of *Might*; another was the cool, iridescent blue of *Magic*; and the third, which Tanner had not seen represented so clearly before, was a shimmering, translucent gold—the circle of *Structure*, or what the druids called the *Life-Bind*.

"Before you can learn how to heal, you have to learn anatomy," Sylara said. She stood beside the tripod, her hands tucked into the generous sleeves of her green robes. Her voice was steady, projecting the authority of the teacher she had been for sixty years. "In Thornwood, we do not view the body as a collection of parts. We view it as a collision of systems."

She gestured toward the woman standing on the other side of the diagram. Parmera was shorter than Sylara, with skin the color of dark earth and hair that had been braided with silver wire and tiny, dried medicinal flowers. Her eyes were sharp, analytical, the kind of eyes that didn't just see a person but estimated their bone-density and cardiovascular health.

"Parmera is our resident expert on humanoid anatomy," Sylara continued. "She will be your primary guide through the physical—"

Tanner didn't hear the rest of the sentence.

He had moved closer to the diagram, his head tilted slightly to the left. His eyes—the "Sight" that saw the *Flow* and the *Bind*—had already dismissed the ink and the parchment. He was looking at the way the mana in the room was being pulled into the shape of the circles. 

The diagram wasn't just a visual aid; it was a projection.

In his mind, the three circles began to rotate. He saw the place where *Might* (the physical muscle and bone) ground against *Magic* (the internal mana channels). He saw the central intersection, the tiny sliver where all three circles met—the place where a person's *Origin* resided. 

He didn't just see the overlapping. He saw the *tension*. 

He saw how the blue mana thread didn't just sit beside the red cord of muscle; it threaded through it like a needle, and where they crossed, the gold of the *Life-Bind* acted as the knot. If the knot was loose, the muscle failed. If the blue thread snapped, the red cord went cold.

The world around him faded. The sound of Sylara’s voice became a distant, muffled hum, like the sound of wind through a different tree. He moved a step forward, then another, until his nose was inches from the central intersection of the diagram. 

He wasn't "zoning out." He was analyzing. 

He was comparing the diagram's perfect geometry to the messy, vibrating reality he had seen in his father's thick-muscled arms and his mother’s delicate, mana-sensitive fingers. He started tracing the lines of the diagram in the air with his own fingers, his movements microscopic and incredibly fast.

Parmera raised an eyebrow, her hand going to the hilt of a small pruning knife at her belt. She looked at Sylara, a question in her sharp eyes.

Sylara, however, merely frowned for a fleeting second before her expression smoothed into one of quiet observation. She remembered her daughter's warning: *He doesn't learn by listening. He learns by watching the invisible.*

She raised her hand toward Parmera in a "wait" signal. 

The three of them stood there. Parmera was tense, her weight shifted onto the balls of her feet. Sylara was still, a slight smile beginning to touch the corners of her mouth. Tanner was a statue, his breathing so shallow it didn't even disturb the dust motes in the sunlight.

An hour passed.

The light in the root-hollow shifted from gold to amber. A bird landed on a branch outside, whistled a three-note melody, and flew away. The Great Tree breathed beneath them, a low-frequency vibration that most people never noticed but which Tanner’s body had been subconsciously mapping for the entire time.

Finally, Tanner took a long, heavy breath—the kind of breath someone takes when they’ve just finished a three-mile run. He blinked, the intensity fading from his eyes, and he stepped back from the diagram.

"Wow," he whispered. "I had no idea."

He looked at the two women, as if realizing for the first time that they were still there. He didn't look tired. He looked hungry—not for food, but for the next realization.

"The way the *Bind* holds the *Might* together during a mana-spike..." He shook his head, his small face illuminated by a sudden, fierce clarity. "I thought it was just friction. I didn't realize it was a deliberate knot."

He looked at Parmera, then at Sylara. He adjusted his posture, rooting himself into the floor as Kaelen had taught him.

"Shall we begin?" he asked. 

Parmera looked at Sylara. The Arch-Druid’s smile was no longer hidden.

"We have already begun, Tanner," Sylara said. She took a step back, her robes rustling softly on the moss-smoothed floor. "I will leave you with Parmera for the remainder of the evening. She will help you learn the foundations for healing. I had originally lined up another novice healer to teach you the basics—someone closer to your age and level."

She paused, looking at the diagram, and then at the boy who had just decoded its heart in an hour.

"Instead," Sylara said, her voice dropping into a register of profound seriousness, "I will teach you, personally."

Tanner bowed. He bowed first to his Grandmother, the deep, respectful bow of the Sunshadow lineage. Then he turned and bowed to Parmera, his new Teacher.

"I cannot allow that," Tanner said simply. 

The silence that followed was different from the one that had preceded it. This was the silence of a hammer strike that hasn't yet been answered by the metal. Parmera went very still. Sylara’s eyebrows climbed toward her hairline. 

Tanner didn't look at his grandmother. He looked at Parmera. He stared at her for a long time, his eyes wide and unblinking, the "Sight" still active, tracing the lines of her own Life-Bind. 

Parmera felt a sudden, inexplicable chill. It wasn't predatory, but it was relentless. It felt as though he were looking "through" her, as though her skin and muscle and bone were merely layers of a particularly complex piece of wood he was preparing to work.

Tanner sat on the floor, his movements efficient. He unslung his pack—the leather one he had stitched in his mother’s shop—and started taking out tools. A small forging hammer, a set of fine needles, a roll of dark, treated cloth.

"Teacher," he said, addressed to Parmera. "Please return tomorrow. I will have something for you. A small gift in exchange for lessons."

Sylara looked at Kaelen, who had been standing in the shadows of the doorway. The old ranger merely shrugged, a faint, 'I-told-you-so' glimmer in his eyes.

"He does not take what he hasn't earned," Kaelen said from the door. "Even from family. Especially from family."

Tanner didn't look up. He had already spread the cloth on the floor and was examining a small, dull-gray ingot of metal he had pulled from the bottom of his pack. 

"Until tomorrow, Teacher," Tanner said.

Sylara watched him for a moment longer, then nodded toward Parmera. They walked out together, leaving the five-year-old on the floor of the root-hollow, the light of the setting sun catching the edge of his hammer as he began to work—not to study a book, but to create the payment for the knowledge he had yet to receive.
