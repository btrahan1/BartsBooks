# Chapter 27: A Gift for a Teacher

The root-hollow did not just feel larger; it felt as though it were expanding with the coming of night. 

In the absence of the Arch-Druid’s presence, the ambient mana of the Great Tree seemed to settle, losing its directive edge and becoming something more primal—a heavy, verdant tide that pulsed with the rhythm of deep roots and slow centuries. Tanner sat in the center of the hollow, cross-legged on the dark, treated cloth he used to protect his work from the forest's dampness. 

He was five years old, and he was currently negotiating with a ghost.

The ghost was the memory of the "Venn Diagram"—the three overlapping circles of *Might*, *Magic*, and *Structure*. It sat behind his eyes like a lingering afterimage after a bright light, refusing to fade. Every time he blinked, he saw the central gold of the Life-Bind, the tiny, critical knot that prevented a person from simply dissolving into a puddle of biological impulse and raw energy.

"Balance," he whispered, his voice small against the hum of the wood. "It’s not just about hold. It’s about the tension."

He reached into his pack, his fingers brushing against his leather-wrapped tools before finding the small, drawstring pouch of heartwood. He didn't just pull the wood out; he invited it. He tipped the contents onto the cloth, and a dozen small, irregular shards of heartwood clattered softly. These were the scraps from the Gift Box—the off-cuts from the three days he had spent shaping the Arch-Druid’s tribute. 

They were pieces of a tree that had fallen on its own, wood that had decided to be still before any axe could demand it. To any other crafter, they were tinder. To Tanner, they were a specific resonance of patience.

He selected a piece that was nearly a sphere already—a dense, knotty junction where a minor branch had once fought for light. It was dark, almost charcoal in color, and so dense it felt more like stone than cellulose. He held it in his palm, feeling its weight. He closed his eyes and let his "Sight" bleed into it.

The wood was quiet. It had no internal mana-flow left of its own, but it was hungry. It was waiting for a directive.

"I will give you a heart," he told the wood.

He took up his father’s hammer—a small, precisely balanced tool with a head of case-hardened iron and a handle of mountain-ash. Then he reached for his carving knife. It was a tool he had built for himself in the quiet hours of his fourth year, the blade forged from a broken file he’d salvaged from the Low District and the handle wrapped in the dragon-hide silk his mother had taught him to stitch.

He began to carve.

The first few strokes were not about shape, but about stripping away the distractions. He shaved the outer bark-scars with a surgical precision, his "Sight" guiding the blade through the microscopic grain-lines. He didn't fight the wood; he looked for the places where the grain wanted to part. 

As the sphere emerged, Tanner’s mind moved back to Mary’s shop in Elveron. He remembered the scent of ozone and the way the ink seemed to vibrate on the parchment before she even touched it with her pen. He remembered her face—analytical, slightly impatient, yet filled with a fierce love for the logic of command strings.

"An Internal Mirror," he thought, his knife-tip tracing the equator of the wooden apple. "It has to reflect what it touches. But it can't just be a surface. It has to be a cavity."

He didn't hollow out the apple physically. He hollowed it out *magically*.

He began the first inscription. He used his smallest needle-chisel, a tool no thicker than a sewing needle, and he began to tap. 

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

The sound was rhythmic, syncing with the low-frequency thrum of the Great Tree. Each tap was a tiny invitation for mana to settle into the wood. He was carving the *Stabilize* rune into the core—a complex, spiraling geometry that Mary had once demonstrated to show how to keep a liquid from spilling. Here, it served a different purpose: it created a "well" in the center of the heartwood, a place where mana could pool without escaping.

By the second hour, the wood felt different. It was getting colder. It was starting to draw in the green mana of the room, acting like a tiny, wooden vacuum.

"Now the Mirror," Tanner whispered.

This was the advanced work. The *Internal Mirror* wasn't a single rune; it was a conversation. It required three interlocking symbols to be carved in a specific sequence: the *Receive*, the *Echo*, and the *Release*. 

He didn't just carve them on the surface. He used the *Sight* to project the runes into the grain itself, "etching" them with his own intent through the metal of the chisel. He saw the red threads of his own *Might* flowing down his arm, through the iron hammer, and into the chisel point. He saw the blue sparks of his *Magic* meeting the red at the wood’s surface, and where they collided, the golden spark of the *Bind* flared.

He was replicating the diagram.

He carved the *Might* lines in the deep grain—the structural support of the apple. He carved the *Magic* lines in the surface polish—the sensitive layer. And at the stem, the place where a real apple would draw its life from the branch, he carved the *Life-Bind*. 

It was a representation of a human system, but it was trapped in the permanence of heartwood.

The night deepened. The orange light faded into a pre-dawn gray, and then into the first chilly sapphire of the morning. Tanner’s eyes were bloodshot, his hands were trembling with the fine-motor strain of a thousand microscopic taps, but the apple was finally finished.

He didn't wait to test it. He *knew* it was correct. He could feel it in the way the air in the root-hollow seemed to bend toward his hand.

He held the apple up to the first pale light of dawn. 

It was a masterwork of minimalism. It was perfectly spherical, about the size of a large plum, polished to a mirror-shine that caught the light of the forest in a deep, amber-trapped glow. But as he turned it, the runes became visible—not as dark lines, but as *glimmers*. They were like the veins in a leaf or the grain in a piece of marble, appearing only when the light hit them at exactly the right angle.

Across the top, near the stem, was the *Life-Bind* geometry—the golden knot he had seen on the Venn Diagram. It looked less like a drawing and more like a physical wire that had been woven into the wood itself.

Tanner took a breath and pushed a tiny, measured thread of his own mana into the stem.

The response was immediate. 

The apple didn't just vibrate; it *sang*. It wasn't a sound, but a feeling—a honey-like warmth that radiated from the center of the wood and flowed out through the runes. As it touched Tanner’s skin, the golden knot flashed a steady, rhythmic pulse. Two fast, one slow. Two fast, one slow.

It was his own heartbeat.

The *Internal Mirror* was working. The apple was reflecting the state of his current *Bind*. It felt strong, steady, but slightly frayed from the night’s work—a subtle, vibrating discordance in the golden pulse that told him he needed sleep.

"Payment," Tanner said. His voice was husky from disuse.

He wrapped the apple in the scrap of forest-green velvet Sylara had given them for their luggage. He tucked it into the side pocket of his pack, next to his father’s hammer and his carving knives. 

He didn't lie down. He sat there for a few minutes more, watching the sun finally crest the distant canopy of Thornwood. He watched the light hit the diagram on the tripod, turning the red and blue circles into something that looked like a fading dream.

He had learned the map. Now, he had built the compass. 

As he finally leaned back against the bark, his eyes half-closing, he thought about Parmera. He thought about the sharp, analytical way she moved, and the way her own *Bind* had looked through his Sight—steady as stone, but with a tiny, persistent flicker near her left shoulder where an old injury had never quite settled.

"Tomorrow," he whispered. "I'll show her the shoulder."

The hammer dropped from his hand, landing softly on the moss with a dull, muffled thud. The boy who was a Smith, a Carver, and now a Student of the Life-Bind, fell into a sleep so deep that even the Great Tree seemed to lower its voice in respect.
