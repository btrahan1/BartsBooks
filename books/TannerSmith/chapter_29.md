# Chapter 29: The Walk of Nature

"Let's take a walk, Tanner," Sylara said. 

Her voice was like the sound of wind through high branches—calm, inevitable, and carrying a weight that the boy could feel even before he processed the words. She had already turned toward the living archway, her robes trailing across the moss with a soft, rhythmic shush. 

Tanner didn't hesitate. He took up his pack, ensuring the diagnostic apple was safely tucked away in its velvet sleeve. He didn't want to bring it—this was a walk, not a lab session—but his "self-made" vow made it difficult for him to leave his own creations behind. They were part of his system, just as his breathing was part of his body.

As they stepped out of the root-hollow and onto the sweeping, bark-clad balcony of the Great Tree, the air changed. It lost the heavy, concentrated sweetness of the living chamber and became something sharper, filled with the scent of wet earth, pine-needle decay, and the electric ozone of a thousand different mana-flows interacting at once.

"For most, healing lessons would come in a classroom," Sylara said as they began their descent. They weren't using stairs; the Great Tree had grown a series of gentle, sloping ramps that spiraled around its trunk, wide enough for two people to walk side-by-side with room to spare. "But I think you may learn a bit differently than others."

Tanner followed along, his hands resting on the straps of his pack. He was already "on." He couldn't help it. The moment they had left the chamber, his "Sight" had expanded. In the root-hollow, the complexity had been manageable—the diagram, the apple, the three women. But here, in the open air of Thornwood, the world was a cacophony of Binds.

"It's loud," Tanner whispered.

Sylara slowed her pace, looking down at him. "The forest?"

"The flows," Tanner clarified. He pointed toward a massive, ancient fern growing from a crevice in the bark beside the ramp. Through his Sight, the fern wasn't green. It was a vibrating lattice of deep blue and bright gold. "That fern... it's not just sitting there. It's screaming at the light. The Blue is pulling so hard against the Gold that it’s almost tearing."

Sylara stopped. She didn't look at the fern; she looked at Tanner. 

"The Walk of Nature," she said softly, a new intensity in her eyes. "No questions today, Tanner. No lectures. We are just going to walk about our village and look around. You can just tell me what you see. We will make this an opportunity to understand 'Life', in general."

They continued their walk, moving further down into the heart of the village. Thornwood was unlike Elveron. In Elveron, things were built—hammers on iron, needles through leather. Here, things were *negotiated*. 

The homes were hollows within living trees, the bridges were woven from still-growing vines, and even the paths were maintained by the selective growth of moss. To the average eye, it was a paradise of harmony. To Tanner, it was a battlefield of intent.

"There," Tanner said, pointing to a group of young elves practicing forms in a clearing below. 

They weren't fighting; they were moving in a synchronized dance, their wooden staves trailing faint arcs of green mana.

"What do you see?" Sylara asked.

"Fragile knots," Tanner said, his voice analytical and slightly distant. "They’re trying to move the Magic before the Might is ready. Their Red circles are too soft. Every time they swing, the Blue thread tries to pull the Gold knot out of place. If they hit something hard, they’ll break."

He paused, his eyes narrowing as he watched a particularly tall elf-boy. 

"That one," Tanner noted. "His left knee. The Gold knot is already frayed. He’s hiding it with the Blue, but the Red is starting to go cold."

Sylara made a small, mental note. The boy he was pointing to was the son of one of her senior druids, a boy renowned for his "effortless" grace. Effortless, it seemed, because he was bypassing his own structural limits with raw mana-projection—a recipe for catastrophic failure in a decade’s time.

They moved past the training clearing and into the "Market of the Growing." 

Here, the trade wasn't in coins or iron, but in seeds, specialized mosses, and vials of concentrated sunlight. The air was thick with the scent of a hundred different blossoms.

"The Binds here are different," Tanner said, stopping before a table covered in small, glowing jars. Inside the jars were bioluminescent fungi. "They’re... looping. The Magic goes into the fungus, turns into Might, then the Might feeds the Magic back into the jar. It’s a closed system."

He reached out a finger, almost touching one of the jars. 

"It’s very efficient," he admitted. "But it's lonely. It doesn't want to talk to the forest anymore. It’s decided it's enough by itself."

Sylara watched him. For most students, she would have had to explain the concept of *Enclaved Mana* and the dangers of *Isolation-Blight*. Tanner had reached the conclusion by looking at the geometry of the light.

"Is that a bad thing?" she asked.

"I think so," Tanner said. "If my hammer decided it was enough by itself, it wouldn't care about the iron. And if the iron didn't care about the hammer, nothing would ever change."

They walked for hours. They visited the "Garden of the Fallen," where ancient trees were allowed to return to the earth, their Binds slowly unraveling in a slow-motion explosion of golden sparks that fed the surrounding soil. Tanner watched this for a long time, his body perfectly still.

"They aren't dying," he said at last. "They’re just changing the shape of their intent. They’re giving their Gold back to the tree that’s growing through them."

They visited the village well—a natural basin formed by the largest surface-root of the Great Tree. A crowd of elves had gathered there, laughing and talking as they drew water that tasted of minerals and magic.

"The people," Sylara prompted.

Tanner looked at the crowd. For a long time, he said nothing. He saw the shimmering overlaps of the Red, Blue, and Gold in dozens of different configurations. Some were bright and aggressive, others were soft and steady. He saw the way the Binds of children were chaotic, shifting with every laugh and every sudden movement. He saw the way the elders had Binds that looked like polished stone—dense, quiet, and deeply rooted.

"They're all trying to be something," Tanner said. "But most of them are using the Blue to hide the Red. They want the Magic to do the work that the Might is supposed to handle." He looked at his grandmother. "Is that why they use borrowed power? Because it's easier than strengthening the Red?"

"In part," Sylara said. "Borrowed power is a shortcut, Tanner. It allows you to reach a peak before you have built the mountain. But as your grandfather says..."

"The peak will eventually realize there's nothing beneath it," Tanner finished.

The sun reached its zenith, casting the forest floor into a dappled mosaic of light and shadow. They had walked the entire loop of the upper village, and Tanner felt a strange kind of exhaustion—one that wasn't in his muscles, but in the Sight itself. The constant analysis of thousands of different systems was starting to blur the edges of his own world.

They found themselves back at the base of the Great Tree, near the Great Arch of the Reception.

"What was the most important thing you saw today?" Sylara asked.

Tanner looked out over Thornwood. He looked at the trees, the bridges, the people, and the invisible threads of mana that stitched them all together. He thought about his heartwood apple, sitting in its velvet sleeve. He thought about Parmera’s shoulder, and the way the "knot" there was waiting for him.

"The Binds aren't static," Tanner said. "I thought they were like dovetails—set in the wood once and finished. But they're more like... breathing. They're always moving, always trying to find a new balance." 

He looked at his hands, open and calloused. 

"To heal someone isn't to fix them," he said, his voice gaining a sudden, profound clarity. "It's to help them find the rhythm again. If the Red and the Blue are fighting, you don't just force them to stop. You find the Golden knot and you remind it why it’s there."

Sylara stood unmoving for a full minute, her eyes locked on the five-year-old child standing before her. She had spent a lifetime in the forest, and she had never heard the core philosophy of Druidic Healing stated with such clinical, uncompromising precision.

"The Walk is over for today," she said, her voice carrying a trace of wonder. "Go back to the hollow, Tanner. Sleep. Your mind has walked further than your feet."

Tanner nodded, bowed, and began the climb back up the spiraling ramps. He was tired, yes, but as he walked, his Sight picked out one last thing—a tiny, struggling seedling growing between the roots of a giant. 

Its Bind was small, barely a glimmer of gold, but it was fierce. It was holding its own against the weight of the mountain above it.

"I see you," Tanner whispered to the seedling.

Then he turned the corner and headed for home, the rhythm of the forest finally settling into a melody he could understand.
