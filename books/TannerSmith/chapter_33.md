# Chapter 33: Personal Walkabout

The morning after the Communion brought a light that felt different—clearer, more intentional.

Tanner woke before the sun-berry lamp had even begun to dim. His well wasn't just full; it felt *dense*, like a piece of iron that had been folded a hundred times. The golden pulse of the Great Tree was a constant, low-frequency hum in the floorboards of the hollow, and as he stood up, he felt his own heartbeat shift, falling into that perfect, resonant cadence. 

*Two fast, one slow. Two fast, one slow.*

He didn't feel like a five-year-old boy. He felt like an extension of the forest’s own intent.

Sylara was already at the central table, preparing a bowl of steamed lichen and berries. She looked up as he emerged from his corner, her analytical gaze immediately sweeping over him. She paused, her spoon hovering mid-air.

"Tanner," she said, her voice careful. "Your well... it’s not just recovered. It’s stabilized."

Tanner nodded, picking up his pack. "The Tree was very generous, Grandmother."

"And you?" she asked, her eyes narrowing slightly. "Did you follow the instructions?"

"I did," Tanner said. He didn't mention the Perspective Exchange. Not yet. It felt like a private conversation between him and the wood, something that didn't need to be translated into Elven or Human. 

He reached for his staff—the one he had carved from the ironwood branch during his fourth year, capped with the steel he had forged in his father's smithy. The moment his fingers closed around the leather-wrapped handle, the staff *sang*. It didn't make a sound, but the resonance was so sharp it made his teeth ache for a second. It felt less like a tool and more like an extra limb, a bridge between his own internal well and the external world.

"I'd like to stroll alone today, Grandmother," Tanner said. "There are things I need to do."

Sylara was surprised. She set the spoon down, the lichen forgotten. "Things to do? Tanner, you have lessons with Parmera at noon. And your parents will be expecting you for the evening meal at the nursery."

Tanner adjusted the straps of his pack, his face set in that quiet, borderline-obsessive focus that Sylara had seen in the forge. 

"I need to get to know the forest a bit better," he said. "I need to... it's hard to describe. I need to become more of a part of it. I think there is much to learn, and I can't learn it all from a diagram."

Sylara studied him for a long moment. Usually, the idea of a five-year-old child—even a half-elf—wandering the upper canopy of Thornwood alone was a recipe for catastrophe. The Great Tree was a beautiful place, but it was also a vertical wilderness filled with predators, unstable vine-bridges, and mana-pockets that could daze an unwary mind.

But she looked at the way he held his staff. She looked at the absolute, grounded stability of his Life-Bind. He didn't look like a child playing adventurer. He looked like a smith headed to his own forge.

"Go," she said softly. "But stay within the boundaries of the Verdant Tier. If the Great Tree’s rhythm changes, you return immediately. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Tanner said. He bowed to her—the deep, respectful bow of someone who understands the weight of a trust given. "I'll be back before dark."

He turned and walked out of the archway, his staff clicking rhythmically against the floor.

---

The "Personal Walkabout" began not as a journey, but as an interrogation.

Tanner didn't walk for the sake of moving. He walked for the sake of *connecting*. With every step, he pushed a tiny thread of his newly-filled mana into the bark beneath his feet. He wasn't taking; he was checking the line. 

*Two fast, one slow.*

The Tree answered him. It guided him away from the busy markets and the training clearings, leading him instead toward the "Silent Gallery"—a section of the Great Tree where the trunk split into three massive pillars, creating a sheltered, windless canyon high above the forest floor.

Here, the mana was different. It was older, more contemplative. The Binds of the moss and the lichens here weren't frantic; they were so slow they were almost static.

Tanner stopped before a massive, bulbous growth on the side of the trunk. Through his Sight, it looked like a tumor—a chaotic knot of Red and Blue spheres that were fighting for dominance over a Golden Bind that was stretched to the point of snapping. 

It was a *Canker-Blight*, a magical infection that affected the Great Tree’s ability to move nutrients. 

"System friction," Tanner whispered.

He didn't reach for the apple. He didn't need a diagnostic tool when the problem was this obvious. He sat before the growth, resting his staff across his knees. 

He closed his eyes and expanded his awareness. He didn't just see the blight; he saw the *why* of it. The Tree was fighting a localized imbalance—a pocket of stagnant mana that had been trapped by a falling branch decades ago. The "intent" of the wood had been broken, and the tree had simply tried to grow around it, creating a knot.

Tanner thought about his father’s forge. When a piece of iron had a cold-shut—a place where the metal hadn't fused correctly—you didn't just hammer it. You had to bring it back up to heat, flux it, and then work the flaw out with a series of gentle, persuasive strikes.

He stood up. He didn't use a hammer. He used his staff.

He tapped the steel cap against the center of the blight. *Clink.*

The resonance of his own well, anchored as it was to the Great Tree’s rhythm, flowed through the ironwood staff and into the canker. It was a precise, focused "Reminding." 

*Two fast, one slow.*

The blight resisted at first. It was a stubborn, selfish system that wanted to stay as it was. But Tanner didn't force it. He simply *held* the rhythm. He became a fixed point of intentionality. He showed the canker what it looked like to be a part of the loom again.

He stayed there for an hour, his small hand wrapped tight around the leather of the staff, his brow beaded with sweat despite the cool morning air. He was exercising the muscle Sylara had spoken of, but today, he felt the Great Tree’s vast lake of mana sitting right behind him, supporting him. He wasn't a cup anymore; he was a tap.

Slowly, the golden knot of the blight began to unravel. It didn't disappear, but it moved. The Red spheres began to align with the Blue flows. The "kink" in the Life-Bind straightened, smoothed out by the persistent, unyielding rhythm of the boy’s resonance.

The canker-blight softened. The bark, once gray and brittle, began to shift back toward a healthy, vibrant brown. The Great Tree breathed a long, subterranean sigh that Tanner felt in the soles of his feet.

"You're welcome," he whispered.

He moved on, deeper into the Silent Gallery. He met a *Cloud-Squirrel*, a small, iridescent creature with wings made of gossamer-fine skin. It was trapped in a tangle of *Binding-Ivy*, its frantically beating heart throwing its Bind into a chaotic, spinning mess. 

Tanner didn't cut the ivy. He simply touched the main vine with his staff and "reminded" it that its intent was to grow upward, not inward. The ivy relaxed, the loops opening like a bored cobra, and the squirrel vanished into the canopy in a blur of rainbow light.

He spent the rest of the morning like this—correcting small discords, smoothing out minor frictions, and listening to the way the forest changed around him. He wasn't just walking "alone"; he was walking with a billion different companions.

He realized that his "Protector" goal was much larger than he had thought. It wasn't just about holding a line during a battle. It was about maintaining the *integrity of the system*. A Protector was the one who ensured that the Binds stayed true, whether in a sword, a person, or a forest.

As the sun began to dip toward the horizon, casting long, emerald shadows across the Verdant Tier, Tanner found himself back at the Great Arch. 

He was tired, his legs aching from the miles of climbing, his mana-well feeling pleasantly used but not empty. He looked at his staff. The steel cap was slightly dulled, and the ironwood handle felt warmer, more alive. 

He had become more of a part of the forest. Not by merging with it, but by *working* with it. He had treated the Great Tree like a collaborative project, and the tree had rewarded him with its trust.

He walked back into the living hollow. Sylara was there, waiting for him. She looked at his face, then at his staff, and then at the way he stood—more rooted than ever.

"Did you find what you were looking for, Tanner?" she asked.

"I think so," Tanner said. He set his staff by the door and unslung his pack. "I think the forest is just a very big smithy. And I think I'm starting to understand how the tools are supposed to fit together."

Sylara didn't ask what he had done. She didn't need to. She could feel the way the Great Tree was still leaning toward him, a silent, golden acknowledgment of the small boy who had decided that even a god deserved a fair exchange.

"Get some rest," she said. "Parmera is coming tomorrow. And she’s bringing her medical kits."

Tanner smiled—a real, wide, five-year-old smile. 

"I'll be ready," he said. 

He fell asleep that night before the sun-berry lamp even flickered, his dreams filled with golden threads, ironwood hammers, and a forest that was finally beginning to understand the name of its newest smith.
