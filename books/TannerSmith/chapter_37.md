# Chapter 37: The Enquiry

The "Chamber of Voices" was a circular hollow carved from the very heartwood of the Great Tree, miles below the Crowned Canopy. 

The air here was ancient. It didn't smell like pine or rosemary; it smelled like rain that had fallen four centuries ago and fossils that were still forming. There were no chairs, only a series of high, natural terraces where the Elder Druids and the Arch-Druids sat in silent, emerald-clad judgment. In the center of the room, standing on a disc of polished amber, was Tanner Smith. 

Through the Sight, the chamber was a forest of old, deep-set Binds. The ambient mana didn't swirl here; it drifted like heavy, invisible fog, every molecule laden with the weight of tradition. Tanner felt the pressure of those thousands of years resting on his shoulders. He looked very small. His staff, which had felt like a legendary tool in the Silent Gallery, now looked like exactly what it was: a child’s first attempt at woodcraft, capped with a rough-forged steel tip. 

The silence lasted for several minutes, broken only by the faint, distant rushing of sap through the massive arteries of the trunk. Then, a voice spoke from the highest terrace—a woman whose skin was the color of dark loam and whose eyes were twin sparks of white mana. 

"Who are you, Tanner Smith?" she asked. The question wasn't about his name. It was about his *type*. She wanted to know if he was a fluke, a threat, or a prophet. 

Tanner gripped the staff a little tighter, feeling the grounding resonance of the Great Tree beneath the amber. "I'm a smith," he said. "And I'm a student of my Grandmother, and Teacher Parmera."

"A smith," another voice, a male Elf with a voice like cracking dry leaves, repeated with a sneer. He leaned forward, his emerald robes catching the dim sun-berry light. "A smith of iron and leather. Yet you have performed a restoration that our master healers have labeled a medical impossibility. You have used a 'Mana Chain' to superimpose a mirror image onto a chronic injury. This is not smithing. This is heresy... or it is something we do not understand. *How* did you do it?"

Tanner looked up at the tiers of elders. He didn't see judge or jury; he saw a set of complex Binds that were currently vibrating with the resonance of "Skepticism." It was a jagged, defensive frequency that reminded him of the way a cold ingot resists the hammer.

"It’s not an impossibility," Tanner said, his voice quiet but carrying clearly in the hollow. "It’s just a construction problem. Parmera’s shoulder had a cold-shut in the Bind. The bone thought it was still breaking because the mana-channels were circling the shadow of the injury. All I did was flux the stagnation and remind the Golden knot what its symmetry looked like."

He used his staff to draw a circle in the air, the steel cap catching a glint of the white-mana light. 

"There are Three Spheres," he continued, slipping into the technical tone he used in the forge. "Red for the Might, Blue for the Magic. If the Red and the Blue are out of alignment, the Gold knot that holds them together—the Life-Bind—gets a kink in it. If you leave a kink in a wire for twenty years, it becomes a habit. I just used the Apple to provide a blueprint, and the other students provided the heat to weld it back in place. It wasn’t magic; it was engineering."

The Elder with the leaf-voice stood up, his robes rustling like a dead forest. "Heat? Welds? Blueprint? This is nonsense! You are speaking of the sacred Life-Bind as if it were a common hinge in a farmer’s gate. To heal is to commune with the Mother's Breath, not to... to 'flux' her creations! You are reducing the miracle of life to the mechanics of a tool-shed!"

A murmur of frustration moved through the upper terraces, a wave of emerald and silver shifting as the skeptical druids leaned forward. Their individual mana-flows were starting to spike in jagged, aggressive patterns. To Tanner’s Sight, the room was beginning to look like a sea of lightning. The skepticism was turning into a demand for conformity, a collective intent to "correct" this five-year-old heresy.

"You are a child," the loam-skinned woman said, her white-mana eyes burning like cold stars. "You have played with forces you do not respect. To take the mana of others and channel it through a piece of wood... you could have shattered their cores. You have no title, no rank. You are a half-blood guest who has overstepped—"

*Grumble.*

The sound didn't come from a throat. It came from the building. 

The floor of the Chamber of Voices vibrated—a deep, subsonic frequency that traveled up through Tanner’s boots, through the amber disc, and into the very bones of the elders. It was the sound of a mountain shifting, or a giant turning in its sleep. Dust motes shook loose from the high, dark ceiling, and the sun-berry lamps flickered from their steady orange into a violent, protective emerald green.

The Great Tree was grumbling. 

The loam-skinned woman went silent, her mouth still open mid-sentence. The leaf-voiced Elder sat back as if he had been struck physically by the weight of the sound. To those with the Sight, the chamber’s ambient fog didn't just swirl; it *bowed*. The mana-threads of the room aligned themselves toward Tanner, a golden current that acknowledged him as part of the loom.

The Grumble was a directive. It was the Great Tree’s way of saying "Silence." And more importantly, it was the Tree’s way of saying "He's with me. Do not question the smith."

Tanner didn't look surprised. He simply closed his eyes and felt the vibration, matching it with his own heartbeat, which was steady and calm. *Two fast, one slow.*

A heavy, wooden door at the base of the chamber groaned open—a natural seam in the bark that Tanner hadn't even noticed. It opened with a slow, deliberate dignity, as if the wood were inviting the world in.

An older Elf entered the room. He didn't wear the emerald robes of the druids. He wore a heavy apron of black, reinforced leather that was stained with the residue of heartwood oils and iron filings. His arms were corded with the kind of muscle that only comes from a thousand years of working with resistant wood—dense, slow-grown power. He looked like the elven version of Tanner’s father, but with eyes that had seen the birth of the forest.

This was Elder Thorne, the Master Weaponsmith of Thornwood.

"Are you Tanner?" Thorne asked, his voice a low, gravelly rumble that perfectly matched the Great Tree’s recent interruption. He ignored the Arch-Druids entirely, his focus locked on the child in the center.

Tanner bowed—a low, respectful smith’s bow. "I am."

Thorne looked at the child, his Sight lingering on the heartwood staff in Tanner’s hand. He didn't sneer. He looked at the child’s work with the critical, respectful eye of a master looking at an apprentice’s first project. He saw the steel, the leather wrap, the ironwood grain. He saw the "honesty" in the tool.

"You need to come with me," Thorne said. He turned his head slightly toward the Arch-Druids, and for a second, his gaze was like a splash of cold water. They might as well have been the moss on the wall for all the attention he paid them. "I have been instructed to make something for you."

Tanner blinked, his heart starting to race. "A gift? I'm sorry, Master Thorne, but I cannot—"

"It is not a gift," Thorne interrupted, his eyes meeting Tanner’s with a sharpness that was like a steel blade being drawn. "I have no interest in charity, and neither does the forest. It is a tool that The Great Tree says it *needs* you to have. She has a project for you, and your current hammer is too light."

Tanner paused, his "Self-Made" oath clashing with the gravity of the smith’s statement. If the Tree needed him to have it, then it wasn't a favor; it was a commission.

"The Tree knows more about the future than you or I," Thorne continued. "Your current staff... it’s a fine piece of work for a four-year-old. The steel is well-folded, and the ironwood is honest. But it is insufficient for what is coming. It is a cup trying to hold a river. She wants to upgrade it. And she wants me to teach you how to do the upgrade yourself. You will not be a consumer; you will be the maker."

The Chamber of Voices was still silent, but the resonance had changed. The frustration of the Arch-Druids had been replaced by a stunned, heavy recognition. For Elder Thorne to be called—and for the Great Tree to command an "upgrade"—was a social shift of tectonic proportions. The "half-blood guest" was suddenly the Great Tree’s apprentice.

Tanner looked at his staff. He felt its limits now, the way it had groaned under the mana-chain of the day before. Thorne was right; it was a cup. And he had always wanted a bigger hammer.

"I have to do the work?" Tanner asked.

"You will supply the intent," Thorne promised. "And the sweat. I will merely provide the forge and the ancient heart. We will use your steel, but the Tree will provide the bone. It is a fair exchange, Tanner Smith. Your perspective for the Tree’s strength."

Tanner looked at Sylara. She was watching him from her terrace, her expression unreadable, but there was a faint, proud sparkle in her eyes that spoke of old secrets being revealed. Then he looked back at the Master Smith.

"I'm ready," Tanner said.

Thorne turned and led the way out of the chamber, his leather apron creaking with every heavy step. Tanner followed him, his own staff clicking one last time on the amber disc before he entered the dark, deep-root corridors of the Weaponsmith’s forge. 

Behind them, the Arch-Druids remained in their terraces, staring at the empty space where a five-year-old boy had just rewritten the rules of their world. The Great Tree gave one last, soft hum of approval—a vibration that settled into Tanner’s well and told him that the "Protector" was finally going to get his shield.

The Enquiry was over. The Forge was waiting. And Tanner Smith was about to learn how to smith a god.
