# Chapter 2: A Parasite's Game

### []{#anchor}Chapter 2: A Parasite's Game

The thing about poker, Nate knew, was that the house never loses. They
get a "rake," a small percentage of every pot, skimmed off the top by
the dealer with the mechanical disinterest of a bee gathering pollen. It
didn't matter who won the hand---Barry, Mr. Chen, or himself---The
Sovereign got its cut. Nate was a good poker player, very good, but the
game required constant migration. He had to move from casino to casino
to find "new blood," fresh faces whose tells he hadn't yet memorized,
whose patterns were yet to be charted.

The part that bothered him, ironically, was the rake. It felt...
inefficient. It was a tax on his skill, a leakage in a system he was
trying to perfect. The house "took" money from his winnings. Even when
he came out ahead, it just irritated him.

The other thing that bothered Nate was that he didn't really enjoy
taking "other people's money"---usually. He knew most of the people that
came into the casino shouldn't be there. They violated the first and
only sacred rule: "Never bet what you can't afford to lose." Barry's
temple-rub wasn't just a tell; it was a broadcast of the mortgage
payment he was gambling away. That was noise Nate preferred not to hear.

Now, the casinos themselves, they had hundreds of millions of dollars.
They were, in fact, a parasite. A beautifully designed, mathematically
perfect parasite that fed on flawed human psychology: hope, greed, and
the inability to comprehend long-term probability. It was a rigged game
from the start, not through cheating, but through pure, unassailable
math. And that, Nate felt, was a system worth exploiting.

It was time to go play some blackjack at another casino. It would be a
grind, but the odds, if managed correctly, were with him. He would visit
a place he had never been, keeping to his low-key strategy, win a grand,
then leave.

He cashed out his chips, tipping the dealer a crisp hundred-dollar
bill---precisely 2.7% of his net winnings, a figure calculated to be
generous but not memorable---and walked out into the perpetual twilight
of the casino floor. The Sovereign was all dark woods and muted golds.
Twenty minutes later, after a silent cab ride down the Strip, he walked
into The Stardust Flamingo.

This place was the opposite: a riot of neon pink and electric blue, with
a soundtrack of chirping slot machines and 80s pop music. It was
designed to overwhelm the senses, to short-circuit the logical part of
the brain. It smelled of cheap coconut air freshener and desperation, a
much less refined vintage than The Sovereign's. Perfect. Low-end casinos
often had sloppier dealers and less sophisticated surveillance.

Nate found what he was looking for: a six-deck shoe game with a \$25
minimum. The dealer was a man named George, according to his name tag.
George looked tired. His tie was slightly askew, and his movements,
while practiced, lacked the crisp precision of a dealer at a high-end
room.

(Internal Observation: Dealer fatigue is a quantifiable variable. A
tired dealer is 12% more likely to have a shallow deck penetration
before shuffling, and 7% more likely to deal with a consistent rhythm.
Both are exploitable.)

Nate bought in for two thousand dollars, exchanging cash for four neat
stacks of black hundred-dollar chips. He sat at third base, the last
seat before the dealer, giving him the maximum amount of time to see the
cards as they were played. For the first two shoes, he flat-betted the
minimum, \$25 a hand. He wasn't playing to win. He was playing to count.

His mind became a silent, flawless abacus. Each card that emerged from
the shoe was assigned a value. Low cards, 2 through 6, were +1. High
cards, 10 through Ace, were -1. The 7s, 8s, and 9s were neutral. He
wasn't just keeping the running count; he was tracking the ratio of high
cards to low cards left in the deck, calculating the "true count" by
dividing his running total by the number of decks remaining.

Running count: +2. Decks remaining: 4.5. True count: +0.44. The deck is
still neutral. Bet the minimum.

He lost the hand. It didn't matter. Individual hands were irrelevant
noise. He was listening for the signal.

Another twenty minutes passed. The chatter at the table was a low hum he
filtered out. A couple from Ohio celebrating an anniversary. A young man
on his first Vegas trip, making every wrong play with a goofy grin. Nate
nodded, smiled politely, and kept counting.

Running count: +8. Decks remaining: 2. True count: +4. The signal is
getting stronger.

The deck was now rich with tens and aces. The parasite's own math was
turning against it. The probability had shifted, however slightly, into
his favor.

Nate slid out a bet of \$200---eight times his minimum. The couple from
Ohio gasped. The young man whistled. Nate's face remained impassive.

He was dealt a ten and a six. Sixteen. The single worst hand in
blackjack. The dealer was showing a seven.

The young man groaned sympathetically. "Oof, tough break, man."

Basic strategy, the chart memorized by every amateur, said to hit. But
basic strategy was for a neutral deck. This was not a neutral deck.

(Internal Calculation: The deck is saturated with high cards.
Probability of drawing a ten and busting: 61%. Probability of drawing a
low card (Ace-5) and improving: 39%. The dealer's seven is also likely
to draw a ten, giving him seventeen. My sixteen is a dog. But the count
is the count. The math is absolute. Standing is the statistically
negative play. Hitting is also statistically negative, but it is less
negative. The only variable is the dealer's hole card. Based on his
dealing rhythm and the slight hesitation before checking for
blackjack... no, that's noise. Stick to the math.)

He tapped the table. "Hit me."

The young man shook his head. "No way, dude."

George slid a card from the shoe. It glided across the green felt and
landed beside Nate's other two cards.

The five of clubs.

Twenty-one.

The table let out a collective sigh of relief. Nate just gave a small,
almost imperceptible nod. He felt nothing. No thrill, no rush. It was
simply the correct outcome of a well-executed equation.

The dealer turned over his hole card. A ten. He had seventeen.

George paid out Nate's bet. Nate now had two hundred dollars more than
he started the hand with. He left the \$200 bet on the table. The true
count was still high.

He would do this four more times, win or lose. His goal was a thousand
dollars. A small enough figure to fly under the radar. A drop in the
ocean for The Stardust Flamingo, but for Nate, it was another successful
extraction. Another small victory against the parasite. Then he would
walk away, disappearing back into the city, another tourist with a lucky
streak, the signal fading back into the noise.
