# Chapter 3: The Physics of Chance

### []{#anchor}Chapter 3: The Physics of Chance

He won the next hand, a simple twenty against the dealer's bust,
bringing his session total to a neat \$1000 profit. But the win felt
hollow, overshadowed by the signal his peripheral vision had just
screamed at him.

A suit. Dark grey, ill-fitting, moving with the slow, deliberate pace of
a shark that knows the fish aren't going anywhere. He wasn't a player.
He wasn't a tourist. He was a Pit Boss, or worse, casino surveillance
taking a stroll. And he was moving towards Nate's table.

(Internal Observation: Subject is male, approximately 50 years old. Gait
indicates he spends 8+ hours a day on his feet. The subtle bulge under
his left armpit is not a wallet; 82% probability it's a holstered radio.
His gaze is not fixed on any single player, but is scanning the
aggregate betting patterns of the table. They've spotted the bet
spread.)

Nate hadn't hit his self-imposed forty-minute time limit, and he was
technically still a few hands away from his monetary goal after the last
loss. But the rules were flexible. The primary directive was always:
*Don't get noticed.* The suit was a blaring alarm.

It was time to go.

He waited for the hand to finish, stacking his winnings calmly. He was
up \$800 after a small loss on the previous hand. Close enough.
Profitability was secondary to longevity.

As the dealer pushed the black chips his way, Nate discreetly palmed a
green \$25 chip. He stood, pushing his chair in.

"Thanks, George. It's been a pleasure," he said, his voice casual. He
tossed the green chip to the dealer, a gesture of goodwill that also
served to break the dealer's focus.

He didn't even glance toward the cashier's cage. In Vegas, for a man
like Nate, chips were a universal currency, and cashing out was an
unnecessary data point. Cashing out created a paper trail, a transaction
that could be logged and cross-referenced. Walking out with chips in his
pocket was cleaner. It left a question mark where there should have been
a period.

He walked away from the table at a tourist's pace, hands in his pockets,
making a beeline for the nearest exit. He could feel the suit's eyes on
his back. He didn't turn. In his mind's eye, he could see the man pause,
scratch his head in mild confusion as he watched a mid-level winner
simply abandon his post. *An amateur who got scared,* the suit would
conclude. *Good.* Let them think he was noise.

Outside, the dry desert air was a shock after the recycled chill of the
casino. Nate hailed the first cab he saw.

"The Horseshoe."

The driver grunted and pulled into traffic.

*Time for some craps! A real man's game,* Nate thought with a sliver of
dark humor. He hated craps. The game was a cacophony of superstition and
bad math, a hurricane of high-fives and shouted slang. Players blew on
dice, begged for numbers, and performed elaborate rituals that had a
0.00% effect on the outcome.

But for Nate, it was a different kind of table. It wasn't about the
cards; it was about the physics.

Binion's Horseshoe was old Vegas. The ceilings were low, the air was
thick with smoke, and the felt on the tables was worn smooth by a
million desperate prayers. It had history. It had grit. And most
importantly, it had 12-foot craps tables with diamond-patterned back
walls that were, according to Nate's extensive research, slightly softer
than the modern, unforgiving walls of the mega-resorts. This introduced
another variable. A variable he could control.

He found a table with a decent crowd and bought in for two thousand,
converting a portion of his Stardust Flamingo chips. He waited through
two other shooters, making minimum pass-line bets, just observing. He
wasn't watching the players. He was watching the dice.

He studied their trajectory as they left the shooter's hand. The angle
of release. The number of rotations in the air. The way they hit the
felt, the way they skittered, the way they struck the pyramidal rubber
of the back wall. Most players just flung the dice. It was a chaotic,
random system. But a controlled throw was different. A controlled throw
was a problem in applied physics.

When the dice came to him, he picked them up. They felt cool and solid
in his palm. The stickman droned, "New shooter, coming out. Get your
bets down."

Nate set the dice. He turned them so the three-V shape was on
top---sixes on the outside, threes facing up. This "hardway set"
minimized the chances of a seven appearing on the axis he was about to
control.

(Internal Analysis: Grip pressure: 8.7 psi. Angle of release: 42
degrees. Rotational velocity required: 2.2 rotations per second. Target
impact zone: three inches from the seam, fourth row of pyramids up. The
goal is not to guarantee an outcome, but to skew the probability.
Standard odds of rolling a seven: 16.67%. With a controlled throw, I can
reduce that to an estimated 13.5%. Over time, that 3% edge is a
mathematical certainty.)

His arm moved in a smooth, practiced pendulum swing. It was the same
motion he'd practiced tens of thousands of times in a custom-built rig
in his apartment. The dice flew low and soft, staying on the same axis,
spinning like tiny gyroscopes. They landed gently on the felt, traveled
the last few feet, and kissed the back wall with a soft *tock-tock*.

They tumbled for a moment, the chaos factor introduced by the pyramids,
and settled.

Five and one. Six. The point is six.

The table cheered. Nate felt nothing. He had just run a successful
experiment. The stickman pushed the dice back to him. He set them again.
Same grip. Same V-shape. Same fluid motion.

He was no longer gambling. He was working.
