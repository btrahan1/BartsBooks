# Chapter 4: The House Edge

### []{#anchor}Chapter 4: The House Edge

The thing about applied physics is something called Chaos Theory. Some
things were just unpredictable. It was the Universe's way of saying,
"You're not as smart as you think you are."

And Nate knew this. There was no such thing as a perfect system. The
Universe made certain of that. A stray air current from a vent, an
infinitesimal flaw in the dice's plastic, a microscopic piece of grit on
the felt---any of these could send his carefully controlled throw into a
chaotic tumble. He wasn't eliminating randomness; he was just nudging
the bell curve of probability a few crucial percentage points in his
favor.

However, not even the Universe could bend probability over time. An edge
was an edge.

"Two-way hard six!" Nate called out, tossing a green \$25 chip and a red
\$5 chip to the stickman. "One for you, one for the boys." A tip for the
dealers on a specific bet. It kept them happy. It made him look like
just another gambler sharing the wealth.

"Bet's on, shooter!" the stickman acknowledged, placing the chips in
their designated spots.

It took three more throws---a four, a nine, an eleven---without rolling
a seven, and then the dice came to rest, sitting side-by-side like a
pair of red eyes. Three dots on each.

The table erupted. "Six! Hard six! Winner, winner, chicken dinner!" a
man in a Hawaiian shirt screamed, clapping Nate on the back.

Nate groaned internally. *He'd never heard THAT one before. Zero points
for originality.*

He smiled a thin, practiced smile and watched the dealer pay out his
bets. But his focus wasn't on the chips. From the corner of his eye, he
saw him. The Shift Manager, flanked by two uniformed security guards.
They weren't moving toward him, not yet. They were moving to a position
of observation, standing casually by a pillar twenty feet away. They
were trying to look nonchalant and failing spectacularly.

Damn.

He still had the dice. It would be infinitely more suspicious to walk
away mid-roll, a "hot" shooter leaving a winning table. It was a red
flag the size of a billboard. Bad luck. *Thanks, Universe.*

Nate accepted the dice back. The game was over, but he had to play it
out. He abandoned his precise, controlled throw. He picked up the dice
and gave them a loose, random shake, flinging them down the table with
the sloppy abandon of a tourist.

*(Internal Analysis: The objective has changed from* '*maximize profit*'
*to* '*simulate amateur.*' *A chaotic system is now the desired outcome.
Abandon control.)*

The next roll was a nine. The roll after that, a five. And on the third
careless throw, the dice bounced off the back wall, spun wildly, and
settled.

Four and three. Seven out.

The table let out a collective groan. Nate shook his head with a
believable sigh of disappointment. "Well, it was fun while it lasted."

He tossed another green chip to the dealers. "Thanks, boys." He began to
gather his remaining chips, stuffing them into his blazer pockets as he
started to make his way toward the main aisle, toward the exit. He
almost made it.

A firm hand touched his elbow. It wasn't the clumsy grip of a drunk or
the iron fist of a security guard. It was a manager's grip---polite,
non-negotiable, and backed by absolute authority.

"Mr. Weber, a moment of your time, please."

Nate stopped. He didn't tense up. He didn't pull away. He simply turned,
his face a mask of mild, inquisitive confusion. The man was the Shift
Manager he'd spotted. He was older, with silver hair and the weary eyes
of a man who had seen every angle and every scam.

"I think you have me mistaken for someone else," Nate said, his voice
even.

"I don't think so," the manager replied, his tone equally calm. "Your
name is Nathaniel Weber. You stayed at the Mirage last April for three
nights. You won \$7,200 at their tables. You hit Caesars for five grand
the month before that. And tonight, you've had profitable sessions at
The Sovereign, The Stardust Flamingo, and now here. My name is Arthur
Sterling. Will you walk with me, or will my colleagues here escort you?"

Nate's internal calculator went silent. The numbers vanished. There was
nothing left to compute. This wasn't a game of probability anymore. This
was a conclusion.

"I can walk," Nate said.

The journey was a quiet one. Sterling led him from the vibrant chaos of
the casino floor, through a set of unmarked double doors, and into the
stark, fluorescent-lit world of the backstage. The symphony of slot
machines was replaced by the low hum of ventilation and the squeak of
their shoes on polished concrete floors.

They entered a small, windowless office. It contained a metal desk,
three chairs, and a bank of monitors showing various angles of the
casino floor. On one screen, frozen in time, was a high-definition image
of Nate's face as he'd bought in at the craps table.

Sterling sat behind the desk. Nate and the two guards remained standing.

"You're not in any legal trouble, Mr. Weber," Sterling began, folding
his hands on the desk. "We are not accusing you of cheating. In fact,
that's the problem. Our analysts have been tracking your play for six
months. Your card counting is technically flawless. Your dice control
is... well, it's the best we've ever seen. You are what we in the
industry call an 'advantage player.' A very, very good one."

He slid a thin file across the desk. Nate didn't touch it.

"You're too good," Sterling said, the politeness in his voice replaced
by a cold finality. "We're a business, designed to provide entertainment
while maintaining a statistical advantage. You are reversing that
advantage. You are bad for business."

He nodded to one of the guards, who stepped forward holding a clear
plastic bag with Nate's chips from the Horseshoe and a thick envelope.
"We've taken the liberty of cashing you out. It's all there, minus the
tips you gave the dealers. Eighteen hundred and fifty dollars."

The guard placed the bag and envelope on the desk.

"This is a formal notice of trespass," Sterling said. "Your photograph
and profile have been distributed to every casino in the state of
Nevada, and to our partners across the country. If you set foot in this
establishment, or any other major gaming establishment, again, you will
be arrested. Do you understand?"

Nate gave a single, slow nod.

"Good," Sterling said, standing up. "This way, please."

He didn't lead Nate back to the casino floor. He led him to a heavy
steel door at the back of a service hallway. One of the guards opened
it, revealing a grimy alley that smelled of stale beer and garbage.

"Have a pleasant evening, Mr. Weber," Sterling said, and gently closed
the door.

The lock clicked shut, the sound echoing in the sudden silence of the
alley. Nate stood there for a long moment, the noise of the Strip a
distant roar. In his hand, he held an envelope full of cash. He had won.
He had beaten the house.

And he had been permanently, irrevocably, kicked out of the game.

Nate looked at the closed steel door, in stunned silence. The deadbolt's
click still hung in the greasy air of the alley. He was alone, cast out
into the city's grimy backstage. This was it. The backroom, the
ejection, the end of the line. This was the moment he had calculated as
an inevitable outcome, the final variable in a long and complex
equation.

Then he started to chuckle.

It was a low sound at first, a tremor in his chest. It grew into a
full-throated laugh, bouncing off the brick walls and dumpsters. It was
loud and free, completely devoid of the control and placid stillness he
cultivated at the tables. He laughed until his eyes watered, leaning
against the rough brick for support.

He knew this day would come. The odds demanded it. You couldn't sustain
a positive expectation against the house indefinitely without the house
eventually changing the rules. It was a statistical certainty. But he
never, in all his scenarios, expected it to be so damn *polite*.

There was no rough handling, no veiled threats, no tough guys taking his
winnings. It was a corporate HR meeting. A "formal notice of trespass."
They had complimented his skill, called him the best they'd ever seen.
It was the most respectful firing squad imaginable.

And the icing on the cake, the absolute chef's kiss, was the envelope
clutched in his hand.

He pulled it out and tore it open under the dim glow of a service light.
Inside was a cashier's check from Binion's Horseshoe for \$1,850 and a
single sheet of paper. It was a receipt for his cash-out. His name was
printed at the top: NATHANIEL WEBER.

This wasn't just money. This was a trophy. This was a diploma. This was
a signed, sealed, and delivered admission of defeat from an industry
that defined itself by its invincibility. It was written proof.

He, Nathaniel Weber, a single man armed with nothing but his mind, had
beaten them. Not just one casino, not just for one night. He had beaten
the entire system. He had played their game so perfectly, so
efficiently, that their only recourse was to ban him from the board
entirely. The casinos, all of them, had just bowed down to his
superiority.

Oh, this felt good.

This felt better than any jackpot. It was a feeling of pure,
intellectual victory. He hadn't just taken their money; he had broken
their model. He held the proof in his hand.

The laughter subsided, replaced by a wide, genuine grin. He looked up at
the sliver of starless, neon-polluted sky visible between the buildings.
For the first time in a decade, he had no next move to calculate, no
casino to case, no odds to compute. The game was over. He had won.

He tucked the envelope and the receipt carefully into his inside breast
pocket. He straightened his blazer, brushed the brick dust from his
shoulder, and walked out of the alley and onto the glittering, chaotic
street.

The roar of the Las Vegas Strip hit him, but it sounded different now.
It wasn't the sound of a thousand tiny battles of chance he needed to
navigate. It was just noise. Beautiful, irrelevant noise.

The question, for the first time in a very long time, was stunningly,
terrifyingly, wonderfully simple:

What next?
