# Chapter 5: The Final Tally

### []{#anchor}Chapter 5: The Final Tally

Nate sat in an all-night diner, one of a dozen anonymous,
fluorescent-lit sanctuaries scattered just off the glittering arteries
of Vegas. The vinyl on the booth was cracked like a dry riverbed, and
the air smelled of stale coffee and bacon grease---an aroma of pure,
unvarnished reality. He picked up the plastic-coated menu, its corners
softened by the hands of a thousand other souls seeking refuge from the
night.

He scanned the options, his mind instinctively beginning to calculate
the food-cost-to-menu-price ratio, the probability of the fish being
fresh versus frozen... and then he stopped. He consciously shut the
calculator off.

When the waitress came, a woman with tired eyes and a pen tucked into
her beehive of hair, he simply pointed to a line on the menu. "I'll have
the 'Sunrise Special'."

"You got it, hon," she said, scribbling on her pad.

He had no idea what it was. For the first time in his adult life, he had
willingly embraced a completely unknown variable. It could be awful. It
could be great. It was a blind bet, a flip of a coin, and the freedom of
it felt so intoxicatingly good.

While he waited, he pulled out his phone, the sleek black rectangle a
stark contrast to the worn formica tabletop. He bypassed the news
alerts, the emails, the noise. He opened a single, secure app.

The icing on the cake, the secret punchline to the joke he'd been
playing for years, was what the casinos *thought* they knew about him.
Arthur Sterling's file probably had him pegged for winnings in the low
six figures, a hundred thousand, maybe two hundred thousand tops. A
significant bleed, enough to warrant an excommunication.

What they didn't know was that they were off by a factor of ten.

They tracked the prime-time player, the man who made a few grand on a
Saturday night. They didn't see the ghost. The one who haunted the
tables during the graveyard shift, in the wee hours between 4 and 7 a.m.
It was the "dead" zone in Vegas, a time of profound systemic weakness.
The A-team surveillance crews were gone, replaced by night-shifters
fighting off sleep. The dealers were exhausted, their movements looser,
their shuffles shallower. The pit bosses' attention to detail was lower
by a statistically significant margin.

For years, Nate had treated the casinos not as a source of income, but
as a source of *seed capital*. He was a venture capitalist exploiting
market inefficiencies. And every week, he had funneled his winnings into
the true statistical battleground, the biggest casino in the world, the
one with no pit bosses and no back rooms. The Stock Market.

The app finished loading, displaying a single number under his
portfolio's heading.

"Three million, one hundred twelve thousand dollars, and some change,"
Nate murmured to himself, the words barely a whisper.

"What's that, hon?"

Nate looked up. The waitress was back, a heavy ceramic plate in her
hand. She was a Vegas native, he guessed. Or near enough. The type who
probably came here on vacation twenty years ago and never left,
acclimated to the city's strange rhythms.

She placed his "special" on the table. It was a glorious, chaotic pile
of waffles, two strips of bacon, a mound of golden hashbrowns, and a
side of buttered toast. Perfect. A breakfast built with no regard for
elegance, only for sustenance.

Nate smiled, a real smile, not the placid mask he wore at the tables. He
pulled a folded twenty from his pocket and slid it onto the table beside
his untouched water glass.

"Keep the coffee coming and keep the change, Margaret." He'd noticed her
nametag the moment he walked in, a reflex he couldn't turn off. But now,
he used it not for analysis, but for connection.

Margaret's tired eyes widened slightly at the bill. "Well, thank you,
sugar. You got it." She topped off his coffee, the dark liquid steaming
in the thick mug.

He picked up his fork and looked out the diner's large plate-glass
window at the blurry, distant lights of the Strip. It was a different
planet out there. A fantasy world he had conquered and been exiled from
in the same night. He felt no bitterness. No regret. Just a profound
sense of peace. The numbers on his phone were the victory, but
this---this simple, uncalculated moment---was the prize.

"It's a beautiful night, isn't it?" he said, mostly to himself, but loud
enough for Margaret to hear.

She paused her retreat, looked out the same window, and a genuine smile
touched her lips. "You know," she said. "It really is."
