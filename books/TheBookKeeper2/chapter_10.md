# Chapter 10: The Unaccounted Variable

### []{#anchor}Chapter 10: The Unaccounted Variable

The bell over the door dinged again, a bright, cheerful sound that cut
through the quiet of the shop. Nate turned from Alistair, his tea mug
halfway to his lips.

A young woman stood in the doorway, shaking a light rain from her
jacket. She had dark, curly hair pulled back in a messy bun, a smudge of
what looked like ink on her cheek, and bright, intelligent eyes that
were taking in the scene with quiet curiosity. She was holding a canvas
tote bag overflowing with books.

"Ah, right on time, Elizabeth!" Alistair's face lit up with a
grandfatherly warmth. "Come, come. Say hello to your new boss!"

Nate froze.

For a full three seconds, his brain went into a state of catastrophic
system failure. The stream of calculations, observations, and
probabilities that usually ran in the background simply... stopped. The
gears seized.

An employee?

He had read the purchase agreement three times. He had memorized every
clause, every addendum, every line item regarding assets and
liabilities. The inventory, the fixtures, the lease, the business
name---it was all there. There was no mention of staff. No payroll
records. No line item for "Elizabeth."

This was an unaccounted-for variable. A card appearing from a second
deck.

Alistair saw the flicker of confusion---the infinitesimal widening of
Nate's eyes, the sudden stillness of his posture. He stepped closer, his
cheerful demeanor momentarily sharpening into something more serious. He
stared hard into Nate's eyes, and in a low voice, almost a whisper, he
said, "That's not a problem, right, Nate?"

It wasn't a question. It was a statement with a question mark tacked on
at the end out of politeness. Nate's mind, rebooting from its temporary
crash, immediately processed the subtext.

*(Internal Analysis: Alistair's tone has shifted from* '*amiable
seller*' *to* '*protective guardian.*' *His posture is defensive.
Probability that Elizabeth's continued employment is a non-negotiable,
albeit unwritten, term of this deal: 99.8%. To refuse would be to
scuttle the entire transaction.)*

This was not a business negotiation. This was a test of character.
Alistair hadn't put her in the contract because he wasn't selling her
contract. He was entrusting her to the new owner. It was part of the
"responsibility" he'd just mentioned.

The young woman---Elizabeth---had walked over, a slightly hesitant smile
on her face. "Hi," she said, her voice soft but clear. "It's nice to
meet you. I'm Lizzie." She shifted the heavy tote bag. "I just picked up
the special orders from the post office."

Nate's brain processed the new data. She wasn't just a clerk; she
handled logistics. She was integrated into the store's messy,
inefficient, and apparently functional system.

He had two options.

Option A: The Vegas Play. Point out the contractual omission. State that
he had not agreed to take on staff. Renegotiate the terms or walk away.
It was the logical, financially prudent move. It was also, he realized
with sudden clarity, the wrong move.

Option B: The Port Blossom Play. Adapt. Accept the new variable. Rerun
the equation.

He looked from Alistair's intensely watchful gaze to Lizzie's curious,
open face. He was in their world now, and he had to play by their rules.

Nate extended his free hand, forcing a calm he didn't quite feel. "Nate
Weber. It's a pleasure to meet you, Lizzie."

Alistair's shoulders relaxed. The tension in the air evaporated as
quickly as it had formed. The old man beamed, his faith in Nate visibly
restored. "Excellent! Lizzie here is the real brains of the operation.
She knows where every single book is, don't you, my dear? A walking,
talking card catalog."

"I try," Lizzie said with a modest smile, her eyes flicking around the
chaotic stacks. "It's mostly organized by genre, then by a system of
'what feels right'."

Nate took a sip of his now-lukewarm tea. Organized by 'what feels
right.'

He had just inherited a business with an intuitive inventory system, a
pet dragon tea, and a surprise employee who was the "brains of the
operation."

The odds of this venture succeeding, by any logical metric, had just
plummeted. And yet, for the first time, he felt like he was exactly
where he was supposed to be.
