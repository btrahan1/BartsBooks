# Chapter 1: The Sandbox

### []{#anchor}Chapter One: The Sandbox

The Pacific Ocean, two hundred feet below the surface, does not care
about borders. It does not care about treaties, sanctions, or the
posturing of old men in warm offices. It is cold, it is crushing, and it
is silent.

Captain Valery Korolenko---who had instructed his crew three weeks ago
to address him only as *Nemo*---stood over the plot table. The digital
glow of the bathymetry washed his face in a spectral green.

"Range," he said. His voice was soft, barely louder than the hum of the
air scrubbers.

"Target distance, two hundred and twelve miles," the Weapons Officer,
Lieutenant Volkov (no relation to the thinkers of the past, simply a man
good at math), replied. "Downtown Los Angeles. The Wilshire Grand Center
is the designated zero point."

Nemo stared at the dot on the screen.

Two hundred miles. A stone's throw for the Zircon-class hypersonic
cruise missile resting in Tube 4. In the old days, this would have been
an act of war. But there was no war anymore. Nemo knew this better than
anyone. War implied a conflict between two sides with a chance of
victory.

What was happening back home, in the mud of the Donbas and the ruins of
Kharkiv, wasn't war. It was a trade show.

He closed his eyes for a second and saw the images that had broken him.
Not the dead bodies---a submarine commander is trained to ignore the
cost of life---but the *drones*. He remembered reading the intelligence
briefings before he stole the ship. The Americans were sending
switchblade drones to test their guidance chips. The Iranians were
sending Shaheds to test their range. The Chinese were watching the
satellite feeds to calibrate their AI targeting algorithms.

The Ukraine was just a sandbox. A petri dish where the superpowers could
play with their toys without scratching the paint on their own cities.
They were monetizing the slaughter, gathering data points from every
crater to improve their next fiscal quarter's defense contracts.

*I am done with the simulation,* Nemo thought. *It is time to bring the
experiment to the control group.*

"Captain," his XO, Antonov, stepped up beside him. Antonov was a bear of
a man, his face scarred from a reactor fire years ago. He was the only
one who knew the full scope of the payload. "We are approaching the
thermal layer. If we go up to launch depth, the Americans will hear us.
The sonar net off California is dense."

"Let them hear," Nemo said, eyes opening. They were grey, flat, and
devoid of fear. "Stealth is for thieves. We are not stealing anything
today, Antonov. We are making a delivery."

He turned to the bridge. The crew of the *K-329 Belgorod*---or what was
left of it after Nemo had offloaded the loyalists in a lifeboat three
hundred miles off Kamchatka---watched him. These were the disillusioned.
The men who had lost brothers to faulty equipment and sons to pointless
offensives. They didn't defect to America. They defected to Nemo.

"Comrades," Nemo said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to.
"For two years, we have watched the world leaders treat the threat of
nuclear fire like a poker bluff. The President of the Federation
threatens it on Mondays. The President of the West dismisses it on
Tuesdays. They have forgotten the fear. They believe the atom is just a
bargaining chip."

He walked over to the Weapons Control console. He took the red key from
around his neck.

"They believe they are safe because they fight their wars by proxy. They
think the blood stays on the other side of the ocean."

Nemo inserted the key into the slot. Antonov, without hesitation,
inserted his.

"We are going to remind them," Nemo whispered, "that the world is very,
very small."

"Tube 4 flooded," Volkov reported, his hand hovering over the trigger.
"Hatch open."

Nemo looked at the timer. It was 8:00 AM in Los Angeles. The morning
rush hour. Millions of people sitting in traffic, drinking coffee,
worrying about emails, completely unaware that a phantom from the deep
was about to stop time.

"Launch," Nemo ordered.

The submarine shuddered. It was a heavy, metallic groan that vibrated
through the deck plates and into the soles of their feet.

On the sonar screen, a new contact appeared. It moved fast. Mach 9.

"Bird is away," Volkov said. "Flight time to impact... four minutes."

Nemo walked back to the periscope stand, though he didn't raise it. He
imagined the missile breaching the surface, shedding the water, the
booster igniting and turning the Pacific sky into a canvas of white
smoke. He imagined the frantic calls in the Pentagon. The confusion. The
denial. *Is it a drill? Is it real?*

"Take us deep, Antonov," Nemo said, clasping his hands behind his back.
"Six hundred meters. Rig for silent running."

"And the radio?" Antonov asked. "Do we broadcast the manifesto?"

"No," Nemo said, a thin, cruel smile touching his lips. "Let them
wonder. Silence is heavier than words."

He checked his watch. Three minutes and fifty seconds left.

The missile wasn't armed with a nuclear warhead. It wasn't even armed
with high explosives. The nose cone was filled with concrete and a
densely packed casing of solid tungsten.

It wouldn't incinerate Los Angeles. But when a kinetic object strikes
the center of a city at nine times the speed of sound, the shockwave
alone will shatter every window for ten miles. The sonic boom will sound
like the trumpet of Gabriel. It will smash a crater into the asphalt
deep enough to bury a skyscraper.

It would land, and it would not explode. It would sit there, smoking and
hot, a tombstone in the middle of the freeway.

And the world would look at it and realize that if Nemo had chosen
differently, they would all be ash.

"Two minutes," Volkov whispered.

Nemo closed his eyes. He waited for the screams he couldn't hear, and
the panic that would feed his soul.

"Let the chaos begin."
