# Chapter 2: The Dust and the Dashing

### []{#anchor}Chapter Two: The Dust and the Dashing

Victoria Winters spat out a mouthful of grit. It tasted like pulverized
drywall and copper.

Thirty seconds ago, she had been standing on the corner of Figueroa and
Wilshire, checking her reflection in the lens of the heavy broadcast
camera. She had been preparing to deliver a ninety-second filler segment
on the delayed construction of a new metro line. She had been bored. She
had been worrying that her blazer---a sharp, electric blue that popped
on HD---was too warm for the unseasonably hot Los Angeles morning.

Now, she was on her hands and knees, and the world had turned grey.

"Marcus!" she screamed, the sound of her own voice muffled by the
ringing in her ears. "Marcus, talk to me!"

Her cameraman, a heavy-set veteran who had covered the Gulf War before
retiring to local news, was groaning a few feet away. He was covered in
white dust, looking like a baker who'd lost a fight with a flour sack.
But he was holding the camera like a baby.

"I'm good," Marcus coughed, hauling himself up. He wiped blood from a
cut on his forehead with his sleeve. He looked at the camera. The tally
light was dead. He smacked the battery pack. The red light blinked on.
"We're rolling. Vic, we are rolling."

Victoria scrambled to her feet. She didn't check her hair. She didn't
check her makeup. The instinct that had been dormant for five years of
covering cat shows and celebrity DUIs suddenly roared to life.

"Live?" she asked.

"Live. Feed is choppy, but we're punching through."

Victoria stepped over a pile of shattered safety glass. The air was
thick, swirling with debris. Car alarms were blaring in a discordant,
deafening symphony. People were running---some screaming, some silent,
eyes wide with the shock of sudden violence.

But there was no fire.

That was the first thing Victoria's brain registered. There was no heat.
No smell of cordite or gasoline. Just the smell of crushed stone.

She looked toward the Wilshire Grand Center. Or where the street in
front of it used to be.

"Point it there, Marcus," she commanded, her voice dropping an octave,
becoming the voice of authority. She walked toward the devastation, the
camera tracking her.

"This is Victoria Winters, reporting live from Downtown Los Angeles
where... where something has just fallen from the sky."

She moved forward. The street was gone. In its place was a furrow, a
trench torn through the asphalt like a giant plough had been dragged by
an angry god. Cars had been tossed aside like toys, flipped onto their
roofs, flattened against building facades.

"We felt the impact at 8:02 AM," she said, walking faster, stepping over
a twisted parking meter. "There was no warning. No sirens. Just a sound
like... like the sky tearing apart."

She reached the edge of the crater. It was massive. It had swallowed the
entire intersection. A bus was teetering on the edge, its front wheels
hanging over the abyss.

Victoria looked down.

The dust was settling, revealing the object.

It wasn't a meteor. It wasn't a plane engine.

It was a cylinder. Sleek, black, and terrible. It was buried deep, only
the tail section visible, jutting out of the ruined earth like a dark
monolith. The metal was hissing, steam rising where it touched a
ruptured water main. The heat radiating from it distorted the air,
making the scene ripple.

Victoria froze. She knew what a missile looked like. She had seen the
diagrams on the news every night for two years, watching the footage
from Ukraine and Gaza.

"Marcus," she whispered, forgetting the microphone for a split second.
"Zoom in. On the fins."

The camera lens whirred.

The object was perfectly intact. It hadn't detonated. It had simply
arrived.

Victoria looked directly into the lens. This was it. The moment the
world changed. The moment she changed.

"If you are just joining us," she said, her voice trembling but clear,
cutting through the chaos. "We are looking at a projectile embedded in
the center of Los Angeles. It appears to be military in origin."

She took a breath. She needed to say it before the National Guard
arrived and cut the feed. Before the censors woke up.

"It didn't explode," Victoria said, staring at the black metal
tombstone. "It struck with enough force to shatter windows for ten
blocks, but the warhead... the warhead is dormant."

She stepped closer to the edge, the heat of the friction-burned metal
hitting her face.

"This wasn't an attack," she said, the realization hitting her at the
same time the words left her mouth. "If this thing had gone off, I
wouldn't be speaking to you. Downtown Los Angeles would be gone."

She looked up at the skyscraper above her, the glass stripped away, the
steel skeleton exposed.

"Someone just put a gun to the head of America," Victoria Winters said,
her eyes fierce, "and they decided not to pull the trigger. The question
is... why?"

Behind her, the first sirens began to wail, a mournful cry that rose up
to meet the dust. Victoria didn't flinch. She just kept staring at the
black fin protruding from the earth, waiting for it to open.
