# Chapter 13: The Hexagon Army

### []{#anchor}Chapter Thirteen: The Hexagon Army

It was the impossible mission, executed by the impossible team.

History would call it "Operation Broken God."

At 0200 hours local time, the power grid of Pyongyang failed. This
wasn't a standard brownout; it was a localized, hard-kill cyber-attack
launched by Unit 61398 of the People's Liberation Army of China.

Thirty seconds later, the radar shadows appeared.

They came in low over the mountains. American MH-60 Black Hawks flying
in formation with Russian Mi-17 transport choppers. On the ground, South
Korean 707th Special Mission Group operators laser-painted the perimeter
defenses of the Ryongsong Residence.

Inside the palace, the lights died.

When the breach occurred, it was a symphony of lethal efficiency.
British SAS blew the doors. Russian Spetsnaz Vympel units cleared the
corridors with flash-bangs and silenced subsonic rounds. US DEVGRU (SEAL
Team 6) moved directly to the Master Bedroom.

There was resistance---fanatical Imperial Guards who refused to
surrender---but they were fighting the apex predators of four
superpowers. It wasn't a battle; it was a deletion.

At 0214, the code word was spoken into the encrypted comms: *" The
Statue has fallen."*

The Little Fat Man was no more.

By sunrise, the convoys arrived. Not tanks, but food trucks. Thousands
of them, pouring across the Yalu River from China and the DMZ from the
South. They brought rice, medicine, and truth.

The Hexagon seized the state media apparatus. For the first time in
seventy years, the televisions in North Korea didn't broadcast praise
for the Dear Leader. They broadcast the truth: *He is gone. You are
free. The world is here to help.*

There were no riots. The populace was too stunned, too malnourished, and
too conditioned to obey authority. When the authority shifted, they
simply waited.

The "Election" was a frantic, miraculous administrative feat managed by
the UN. It wasn't a standard ballot. It was a gathering of the
subterranean dissident movement---the intellectuals, the reformist
generals, the hidden voices who had been waiting for the dynasty to
crack.

They nominated **Dr. Moon So-yi**.

She was a former nuclear physicist who had been under house arrest for a
decade for refusing to accelerate the weapons program. She was frail,
her hair grey and pulled back in a severe bun, but her eyes were made of
tungsten.

Forty-seven hours and twelve minutes after Nemo's deadline, she stood on
the balcony of the Grand People's Study House. The square below was
packed with a million silent, terrified, hopeful souls.

She looked into the cameras. She knew the whole world was watching. She
knew Nemo was watching.

"Citizens of the North," her voice was raspy, amplified by the speakers
that used to blare propaganda. "And citizens of the Earth."

She didn't read from a script.

"For three generations, we have lived in the dark. We were told the
world wanted to destroy us. We were told we needed monsters to protect
us from monsters."

She gripped the podium.

"Yesterday, the walls fell. Not by war, but by the united will of a
world that refused to let us die. The tyrant is gone."

She looked up at the sky, towards the East Sea, addressing the invisible
submarine.

"To the Captain in the deep," Dr. Moon said, her voice steeling. "You
threatened to unify us with fire. You threatened to turn our mountains
into glass to solve a puzzle."

"Do not fire. We have solved the puzzle ourselves."

"We are not South Korea yet. We are not unified yet. We are a people
waking up from a long nightmare. We are hungry. We are confused. But we
are alive."

She paused, and for the first time, a small, genuine smile touched her
lips.

"The war you feared is gone, Captain. There is no one left here to
fight. Go back to the dark. We will handle the light."

In the square below, a single person began to clap. Then another. Then a
thousand. Then a million. It wasn't the rhythmic, forced applause of the
regime. It was chaotic, loud, and real.

In the Situation Room in Washington, the President let out a breath he
didn't know he was holding.

"Forty-eight hours," he muttered. "He actually did it."

"Transmission from the *Belgorod*," the Aide said.

"Put it on."

The screen flickered. No voice this time. Just a text message, scrolling
across the sonar display in green block letters.

PROPOSAL ACCEPTED. KOREAN PENINSULA STATUS: STABLE.

CONGRATULATIONS ON YOUR COOPERATION. IT IS AMAZING WHAT YOU CAN ACHIEVE
WHEN YOU HAVE NO CHOICE.

MOVING TO NEXT OBJECTIVE.

The President stared at the screen. "Next objective? What's left? We
ended the war in Europe. We neutralized Taiwan. We regime-changed North
Korea."

"Sir," the Aide said, looking at the tracking data. "He's not heading to
a country this time."

"Where is he going?"

"He's heading into the Atlantic, sir. But he's turning south. Deep
south."

"South America?"

"No, sir. He's heading for the coordinates... 38.8977° N, 77.0365° W."

The President froze. He knew those coordinates. Every man in the room
knew those coordinates.

"That's... that's Washington DC," the Chief of Staff whispered.

"No," the President corrected, his face pale. "It's not a strike vector.
He's sending a message."

"Sir, the sonar," the Admiral interrupted. "He's pinging. But it's not a
target lock. It's... Morse code."

"Read it."

The Admiral listened to the rhythmic pulses.

"**THE KINGS ARE CHECKMATED. NOW FOR THE BANKERS.**"

The President slumped in his chair.

"Wall Street," he whispered. "He's coming for the money."
