# Chapter 7: The Infiltration

The hardest part wasn't the lock. It wasn't the laser grid. It, surprisingly, wasn't even the armed patrol unit circling the block every twelve minutes.

The hardest part was tamping down the happiness.

Beth lay flat on the cool tar-paper roof of *Mendel & Sons*, her heart hammering a frantic, joyous rhythm against her ribs. It felt like she had just injected pure caffeine into her veins. Every nerve ending was singing.

*Focus,* she commanded herself. *Save the party for the after-party.*

She closed her eyes for three seconds. *Inhale count of four. Hold count of four. Exhale count of four.*

When she opened her eyes, the world was sharper. The giddy "I'm doing it!" energy was shoved into a mental box, labeled **LATER**.

She crawled toward the HVAC unit. The schematics had shown a service hatch. It was secured with a high-end padlock, but a padlock was just a suggestion to someone with the right tools.

She slid her tension wrench into the keyway. *Click. Click. Click.* The shackle popped open in six seconds.

"Trick or treat," she whispered into her mask.

She lifted the hatch. The hum of the fan was loud, which was good; it covered the sound of her descent. She dropped into the shaft, moving like a spider, bracing her arms and legs against the metal walls to control her slide.

She reached the vent grille at the bottom. Through the slats, she could see the back office.

Empty.

She used a customized magnetic tool to unscrew the bolts from the inside, catching them before they could fall and clatter on the floor. She removed the grille, lowered it silently to the carpet, and dropped into the room.

She was in.

She paused, waiting for the silence to settle around her. The air smelled of stale coffee and old paper. It was mundane. It was perfect.

She moved to the door. This was the tricky part. The hallway had motion sensors.

She pulled the *Digital Key* from her pocket. The small device hummed with a faint blue light. She held it near the alarm panel on the wall. The code she’d written—the "ghost loop"—began to broadcast. It told the sensors that everything was normal. It told the system that the air was still, the temperature was constant, and absolutely nobody was walking down the hallway.

She stepped out.

The red LED on the motion detector stared at her. It didn't blink.

She suppressed a giggle. It was absurd. It was so easy when you knew how the world worked.

She reached the showroom floor. The streetlights outside cast long, prison-bar shadows through the iron gates. The glass cases glinted in the semi-darkness.

Gold. Diamonds. Platinum watches. There were millions of dollars in this room.

Beth walked past a case filled with Rolexes without even glancing at them. She wasn't here to empty the place. Greed was sloppy. Greed got you caught.

She went to the register first. The cash drawer was locked, but it was a joke of a lock compared to the front door. A simple rake pick opened it in two seconds.

She took the stacks of bills—twenties and hundreds. Maybe four grand. Just enough to justify the break-in to the cops. A "robbery gone right."

But she needed her souvenir.

She moved to the central display case. This was the good stuff. The vintage pieces.

She cut a perfect circle in the glass with her diamond-tipped cutter, attached the suction cup, and popped the disc out.

She reached in.

Her gloved fingers hovered over a heavy gold bracelet. *Too gaudy.*
A diamond ring. *Too cliché.*

Then she saw it.

It was a necklace. Silver chain, delicate as a spiderweb. The pendant was a single, teardrop-shaped sapphire, so dark it looked black in the dim light. It was elegant. It was understated. It was beautiful.

It was exactly the kind of thing Boring Beth would never wear. Boring Beth wore sensible pearls or nothing at all.

"Hello, pretty," she murmured.

She lifted it out. The weight of it was cool and substantial in her hand. She slipped it into her pouch, separate from the cash.

That was for her. The memento. The crystallized feeling of this exact moment.

She checked her time. Six minutes in.

"Time to vanish," she thought.

She didn't run. She walked back to the office, retracing her steps with the same fluid grace. She climbed back up the vent, replaced the grille (leaving the screws loose—why make it hard for the repair guy?), and shimmied up the shaft.

She emerged onto the roof, the cold city air hitting her face.

She locked the padlock back onto the hatch.

She stood up and looked out over the city. The lights of Manhattan twinkled like a sea of electric stars.

Now. She could feel it now.

She let the breath out in a long, shuddering sigh, and with it, the box opened. The rush hit her—dopamine, adrenaline, triumph. She wanted to scream. She wanted to dance.

She had walked into the lion's den, tickled its nose, and walked out with a prize.

She tapped the sapphire in her pocket through the fabric of her suit.

*Trick or treat, indeed.*

She turned and sprinted toward the edge of the roof, launching herself into the night, a shadow returning to the dark.

***

Most thieves went to a fence. Beth went to church.

Saint Jude’s Cathedral loomed out of the darkness like a gothic mountain. It was closed for the night, the massive oak doors barred, but Beth knew the back way.

She scaled the rectory wall, moving silently through the shadows of the expertly manicured garden. She approached the side door—the one used by the cleaning staff.

It wasn't locked. It never was. A small act of faith, or maybe just negligence.

She didn’t go inside. She just slipped into the alcove where the Poor Box was mounted into the stone wall. It was an old, heavy iron box with a slot that looked like a hungry mouth.

She pulled the bands of cash from her pouch. Four thousand dollars.

She dropped the bills in. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

She didn't do it for absolution. She didn't believe in sin, not really. She did it because the money was dirty, and she was clean. The money was the excuse; the skill was the point. Keeping the cash made you a criminal. Giving it away made you a phantom.

She patted the iron box once, affectionately. "Buy something nice," she whispered.

Then she was gone, dissolving into the city night as if she had never been there at all.

***

Forty minutes later, Beth was home.

The black bodysuit was hidden away in the false bottom of the wardrobe. The tools were cleaned and stowed.

She sat in her favorite armchair, wrapped in a plush, oversized robe. Her feet were bare.

On the small side table, a bottle of expensive Cabernet breathed in a crystal decanter. She poured a glass, the red liquid swirling rich and dark.

She took a sip, letting the complex notes of oak and blackberry wash over her tongue.

Then, she reached out and picked up the necklace.

She held it up to the light. The sapphire drank the illumination, glowing with a deep, inner fire. It was cold perfection.

She didn't put it on. She just held it, letting the stone warm against her palm.

This was the trophy. This was the proof that for one hour tonight, she hadn't been a cog in a machine. She hadn't been a Compliance Auditor. She had been a god.

She leaned back, closing her eyes, savoring the burn of the wine and the lingering electric hum in her blood.

"Happy Halloween, Beth," she whispered to the empty room.

She smiled definitively. It was good to be alive.
