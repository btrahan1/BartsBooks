# Chapter 8: The Anomaly

Detective Jack Thorne’s desk was a geologic formation of paper.

There were strata of open cases (active), substrata of cold cases (forgotten), and a bedrock of takeout menus (essential). Somewhere in the middle, buried under a report on a stolen shipment of catalytic converters, was his keyboard.

Jack took a sip of his coffee. It was lukewarm and tasted like despair.

"Thorne," Lieutenant miller barked, walking past. "New file. 402 West Broadway. The jewelry store."

Jack didn't look up. "Mendel's? That’s barely my jurisdiction. That’s a beat cop problem. Broken window, grab-and-dash?"

"That’s the thing," Miller said, dropping a folder onto the pile. "They claim nothing was taken."

Jack’s interest piqued. He set the coffee down. "Nothing?"

"Owner says a window was improperly secured. Said the alarm malfunctioned. Said it was probably just kids looking for a thrill who got scared off."

Jack opened the folder. The photos were standard crime scene shots. A rooftop HVAC hatch. A pristine showroom.

He picked up the photo of the hatch. Specifically, the padlock shackle.

He squinted.

"That's not a bolt cutter mark," he muttered. "That’s a shim. And that service grille... the screws were left in place, but loose."

He flipped to the interior shots. The glass circle on the display case. It was perfect. A geometric impossibility for a "kid looking for a thrill." A kid with a brick makes a mess. This was surgical.

"Why lie?" Jack asked the empty room. "Why tell the cops nothing was taken when clearly someone was in there?"

*Because you’re laundering money,* he answered himself. *Or you’re moving something you don't want us to know about.*

Mendel & Sons was a Mob front. Everyone in Property Crimes knew it, but nobody could prove it. If a thief broke in, they wouldn't report it unless they had to.

So, someone broke into a fortress, bypassed a silent alarm that shouldn't be bypassable, cut a perfect hole in reinforced glass... and took nothing?

"It doesn't fit," Jack said. He rubbed his temples. He hated when things didn't fit. It made his brain itch.

His phone rang. It was the desk sergeant.

"Thorne," Jack answered.

"Hey, Jack. Weird one for you. We got a call from Father O’Malley over at St. Jude's."

Jack sighed. "Is he complaining about the homeless encampment again? Tell him to call Community Affairs."

"No, it’s not that. He found something in the Poor Box this morning. A lot of something."

"How much?"

"Four grand. In twenties and hundreds. Wrapped in rubber bands that smell like... well, O'Malley says they smell like 'old money'."

Jack froze.

He looked at the Mendel file again. The register would have held a cash float. A typical retail float for a high-end store over a weekend... maybe three, four grand?

"What time did he find it?" Jack asked.

"Early morning. But the overnight security camera—which is grainy as hell, by the way—shows a figure in the garden around 1:45 AM."

The Mendel alarm—the one the owner claimed malfunctioned—had tripped a silent internal log at 1:12 AM.

Jack hung up the phone.

He stood up and walked to the whiteboard on the wall, shoving aside a poster about mandatory HR training.

He drew a circle and wrote: *MENDEL'S*.
He drew a line to another circle: *ST. JUDE'S*.
He wrote: *$$$*.

"Okay," Jack whispered.

You break into a Mob front. You have the skills to ghost a top-tier security system. You have the balls to walk into a room full of diamonds.

And you steal the cash... just to give it to the church?

It wasn't a robbery. It was a statement.

"You're not doing this for the money," Jack said to the invisible thief. "So what are you doing it for?"

He looked at the photo of the perfectly cut glass again. It was arrogant. It was precise. It was... beautiful.

Jack smiled. It was the first time he’d smiled in a month.

"I'm going to find you," he promised.

And for the first time in a long time, the hunt felt interesting.
