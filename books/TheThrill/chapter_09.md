# Chapter 9: The Reaction

Beth sat on the edge of her bed, one running shoe laced, the other dangling from her hand.

The television was on, tuned to the local morning news.

"...and in a heartwarming story coming out of St. Jude's this morning," the anchor was saying, her plastered-on smile softening into something almost genuine. "Father O'Malley reports a miraculous discovery."

The screen cut to the steps of the cathedral. Father O'Malley, a man who usually looked like he carried the weight of the world on his sloping shoulders, was practically glowing. He held his hands up to the sky.

"It is a sign," the priest told the reporter, tears glistening in his eyes. "Four thousand dollars. Found in the poor box before dawn. No note. No name. Just a gift from an angel."

Beth smiled. She finished tying her shoe.

"An angel in black spandex," she whispered to the screen.

"The funds will go directly to the soup kitchen renovations," O'Malley continued. "God bless you, whoever you are."

Beth stood up and stretched, feeling the pleasant ache in her quads from the climb the night before. It was a good ache. It was a secret ache.

She turned off the TV.

The city was waking up. The commuters were starting their grind. The compliance reports were waiting for her at the office.

But for now, she wasn't just Beth Sanders, Auditor. She was an anonymous benefactor. She was a ghost story.

She grabbed her keys and headed out the door. The morning air felt lighter than usual.

***

Five miles away, the air was significantly heavier.

The back room of *Odessa Imports* smelled of cigar smoke and expensive cologne. It was a dark, wood-paneled office that looked respectable until you noticed the pistol on the desk next to the espresso machine.

Viktor Volkov did not look like an angel. He looked like a cinder block with a goatee.

He was watching the same news broadcast on a mute flat-screen mounted in the corner. He watched Father O'Malley weeping with joy.

He took a slow drag from his cigar and turned his head to the man standing nervously by the door.

"The jewelry store," Viktor rumbled. His voice sounded like gravel being crushed.

"Yes, Boss. Mendel says the window was... bypassed. Glass cut."

"And he said nothing was taken."

"That's what he told the police, Boss. To keep the heat off."

Viktor pointed a thick finger at the television screen. "The priest. How much did he say he found?"

The henchman swallowed. "Four thousand, Boss."

Viktor stared at him. "Call Mendel. Ask him exactly how much was in the cash drawer last night."

The henchman fumbled for his phone, dialed, and whispered furiously for a moment. He paled. He hung up.

"Well?" Viktor asked.

"Three thousand nine hundred and eighty dollars in the till. Plus... he had a twenty on the counter he was using for a coffee run."

Viktor exhaled a cloud of gray smoke.

"Four thousand," he said.

The room went very quiet.

"Someone breaks into *my* store," Viktor said, his voice dropping to a dangerous whisper. "They bypass a fifty-thousand-dollar security system. They ignore a million dollars in diamonds."

He stood up, towering over the desk.

"And they take my pocket change to give to a priest?"

He slammed his hand down on the mahogany surface. The espresso cup rattled.

"This is not a robbery," Viktor snarled. "This is a joke. Someone is laughing at us."

He walked to the window and looked out at the gray Brooklyn street.

"Find out who did this," he ordered. "I don't care about the money. I want the comedian."
