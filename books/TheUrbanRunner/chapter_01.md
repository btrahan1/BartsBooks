# Chapter 1: The Urban Runner

The Urban Runner

Bart Trahan

### 

### []{#anchor}Chapter 1

"Bye Mom, I'm off to work! I fed Tabitha! Get some rest!"

The words were a gust of wind, already halfway down the hall by the time
they reached Sarah's ears. Tyler was a creature of constant motion, and
the slam of their apartment door was the starting pistol for his
evening. Sarah heard the faint, rhythmic thud of his footsteps
descending the three flights of stairs, a beat too quick for a walk, too
controlled for a run. It was his purpose-built tempo.

From the worn floral-print couch, she sighed, pulling the afghan her own
mother had knitted over her legs. "Her name is Maya," she murmured to
the empty room, a tired smile touching her lips. He'd been calling his
ten-year-old sister 'Tabitha' for a week, a running joke whose origin
she couldn't even remember. It was just Tyler being Tyler.

He hit the cracked pavement of Oakley Avenue already in a light jog, the
empty delivery pack a comfortable, familiar weight against his spine.
The air was thick with the coming evening, a soupy August humidity that
promised a good sweat. Most people hated it. Tyler welcomed it. It felt
like resistance, something to push against.

The ten-block journey to Saito's Noodle House was his warm-up. He didn't
take the straight shot down the main drag. That was for cars and
cyclists, amateurs who thought in straight lines. Tyler's map of the
neighborhood was a three-dimensional spiderweb of shortcuts and secrets.

He cut left into a narrow alley between a laundromat and a pawn shop,
the air instantly smelling of bleach and forgotten things. His sneakers,
worn smooth at the balls of his feet, made barely a whisper on the grimy
concrete. A chain-link fence, topped with a sagging strand of barbed
wire, blocked the end. A dead end for anyone else. For Tyler, it was a
door. He took three quick steps, planted a foot on a protruding brick,
and vaulted over the top, landing in a cushioned crouch on the other
side without breaking rhythm.

He emerged onto Chestnut Street, a river of slow-moving traffic and
honking horns. He flowed through the gaps in the crowd of pedestrians, a
shoulder dipped here, a slight sidestep there, never losing momentum. He
saw the game in it, the puzzle. He wasn't just moving through the city;
he was solving it.

Saito's was a splash of red and white tucked between a gray office
building and a vape shop. Steam fogged the front window, inside which
Mr. Saito, a man whose age was impossible to guess, moved with the
precise, deliberate grace of a surgeon. He looked up as the bell above
the door chimed, a rare smile crinkling the corners of his eyes.

"Tyler-san. You are on time." In Mr. Saito's world, this was the highest
form of praise. "On time is late. You are early."

"Ready to fly, Mr. Saito," Tyler said, swinging his pack onto the
counter. It was a standard hiking daypack, but its guts were all his own
creation. He'd used foam insulation, corrugated plastic, and a series of
adjustable bungee cords to create four secure, heat-retaining pods. A
marvel of low-budget engineering.

The first order was already sealed and waiting. A Double Miso Ramen and
two orders of Gyoza for the law office on the 12th floor of the Bremmer
Building.

"Six minutes," Mr. Saito said, his eyes on the clock. It was their
nightly ritual. A challenge.

"Five," Tyler countered, a grin flashing across his face. He slotted the
containers into their pods, cinched the straps, and swung the pack onto
his back.

He was out the door before the bell had finished its jingle. The Bremmer
Building was eight blocks away by car. A maze of one-way streets and
traffic lights. Tyler's route was different. He sprinted down the
sidewalk for half a block before veering sharply into the covered
parking garage of a supermarket. He ran up the spiraling ramp to the
third level, his breathing deep and even. At the far end, a low concrete
wall separated the garage from the rooftop of the adjacent building. He
cleared it with a single, powerful leap.

This was his highway. A series of interconnected, flat-topped commercial
roofs that stretched for three blocks. He ran across the sticky
tar-paper surfaces, his footfalls sure and certain. He knew every vent,
every skylight, every loose patch of gravel. From this vantage point,
the city spread out below him, a tapestry of lights and noise. Down
there, people were trapped in steel boxes, inching along at the mercy of
a red light. Up here, he was free.

He reached the end of the rooftop expressway and descended via a fire
escape, the metal groaning under his weight. He dropped the last ten
feet, rolling as he hit the alley below, and came up running. The
Bremmer Building's service entrance was right there. He slipped inside,
nodding to the security guard who knew him by sight, and took the
service elevator to the 12th floor.

He delivered the noodles to a grateful paralegal, collected the payment
and a generous tip, and was back in the elevator in under thirty
seconds.

As the elevator descended, he checked his phone. Four minutes and
forty-two seconds. He allowed himself a small, private smile.

He burst back out into the humid night, the thrill of the run still
singing in his veins. As he cut across the plaza in front of the
building, his eyes caught a familiar sight. The gleaming, royal blue bus
of the Northgate High Stallions was parked by the curb. The entire
varsity soccer team was filing off, every one of them dressed in the
same gray team-issued sweatsuit. They looked like an army. An exhausted,
grim-faced army.

He saw Marco, his best friend since kindergarten, his shoulders slumped
as he hauled his gear bag. And there, by the bus door, was Coach Renner,
his arms crossed, his jaw set like granite. His gaze swept over his
players, a drill sergeant inspecting his troops. Renner's eyes flickered
past Tyler without a hint of recognition. To him, Tyler was just another
townie, another obstacle on the sidewalk.

A month ago, Tyler would have been on that bus. He would have been
wearing that sweatsuit, dreading the three-hour practice Renner had
scheduled for them, even on a game day. He felt a ghost of the pressure,
the suffocating weight of Renner's "Total Commitment" philosophy. The
demand that soccer be their god, and he its only prophet.

Tyler stopped for a second, hidden by the shadow of a large oak tree,
and watched them. Marco caught his eye from across the street. There was
a flicker of something in his friend's expression---resentment, maybe,
or was it longing? Marco gave a short, almost imperceptible shake of his
head and turned away, disappearing into the line of gray-clad players.

Tyler felt a pang, not of regret, but of a strange, sharp clarity. He
looked at the bus, at the rigid formation of the team, and then down at
his own worn-out sneakers and the custom pack on his back. They were
heading into a locker room for a lecture. He was heading back to Saito's
for his next run.

He turned and broke into a jog, leaving the Stallions behind. He had
deliveries to make. He had rent to help pay. He had his own race to run.
