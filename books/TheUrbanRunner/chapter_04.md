# Chapter 4: The Heart of the Run

The dinner rush was hectic, but it was a blast. A controlled chaos that
Tyler thrived on. For three straight hours, he was a blur, a human
pinball ricocheting through his ten-block territory. Mr. Saito worked in
a silent, focused fury, and Tyler matched his rhythm, transforming the
chef's culinary art into a logistical masterpiece. It was a symphony of
steam, shouted addresses, and the steady slap of his sneakers on
pavement.

By nine o'clock, the flood of orders had slowed to a trickle. Tyler
leaned against the counter, muscles humming with a satisfying fatigue,
while Mr. Saito wiped down his station with meticulous care.

"Good night, Tyler-san," the older man said, not looking up. "You were
very fast. Broke the Bremmer Building record."

"Had a good tailwind," Tyler replied with a grin.

Mr. Saito laid out Tyler's earnings on the counter. A stack of crisp
twenties and a thick, rumpled wad of ones and fives from tips. The tips
had been amazing tonight. Enough, maybe, for his mom to give up her
Sunday shift at the diner. The thought sparked a warmth in his chest
that had nothing to do with the residual heat from the kitchen.

As Mr. Saito turned to tally the register, Tyler performed a small,
practiced ritual. With a quick flick of his thumb, he separated roughly
ten percent of the cash, folded it into a tight square, and slid it into
the hidden zippered pocket inside his jeans. It was a second nature
motion, invisible to the casual eye. Tomorrow, before school, he'd
deposit it. The emergency fund. The 'Get Us Out of Here Someday' fund.
No one, not even his mom, knew it existed. Tyler had dreams,
too---quiet, hazy ones about a life with less running---but for now,
they were just data points in a long-term plan. Family first.

The walk home was a slow, welcome cool-down. The humidity had broken,
and a breeze ghosted through the alleys, carrying the distant sound of a
siren. He took the stairs to their third-floor apartment two at a time,
the day's last burst of energy.

He unlocked the door and slipped inside. The small living room was
bathed in the blue-gray light of the television. His mom was on the
couch, half-asleep, a textbook open on her lap. She was taking online
classes, trying to get a paralegal certificate.

"Here you go, Mom," he said softly, placing the stack of bills on the
coffee table next to a stack of mail. "I think we've got rent."

Sarah's eyes fluttered open. She looked at the money, then at him. "Oh,
Ty. You didn't have to---"

"And..." he added, swinging his backpack around and pulling out a white
takeout container. "You guessed it. Noodles."

She smiled at him. It was the same smile she always had for him, the one
he knew better than his own reflection. It lit up her tired eyes but
never quite smoothed the worry lines etched between them. Ninety percent
pure, uncut pride that her son was so strong, so responsible. And ten
percent a quiet, piercing sorrow that he had to be.

She brushed it away with a practiced motion, sitting up straighter.
"Thank you, honey. You're a lifesaver." She looked from his face to the
cash on the table, a dozen unspoken worries and thanks passing between
them in a single glance. She finally pushed a stray strand of hair from
her face, her expression shifting from exhaustion to a gentle,
deliberate brightness.

"So, this Saturday," she began, her voice casual, but he could hear the
careful thought behind it. "Soccer game at The Club?"

Tyler paused, surprised. She knew he'd joined the community league, but
they hadn't really talked about it. To him, it was just a way to keep
playing without the drama.

"Yeah, over at Northwood Park. Why?"

Her smile became real, losing that ten percent of sorrow for a moment.

"Maya wants to see you play."
