# Chapter 17: The Cathedral of Sport

Three hours later, the world had transformed. The sun was gone, replaced
by the brilliant, artificial glare of the stadium lights. They turned
the green turf into a hyper-real emerald stage and cast long, dramatic
shadows. The air was cool and crisp. This was the cathedral of sport,
and it was packed.

The two teams were on opposite sides of the field, doing their final
warm-ups. The contrast was a story in itself.

The Stallions moved with the synchronous, intimidating grace of a
military drill team. Every stretch was held for the same count, every
sprint ended on the same line. They were a single entity, clad in
immaculate royal blue, their faces grim and focused. They were here to
execute a plan.

On the other side, the Portside Wanderers looked like they were at a
backyard barbecue. They were laughing, juggling the ball in a circle,
trying audacious trick shots on their backup goalie. In the center of it
all was "Fake-out Franklin," the newly-minted folk hero, all smiles and
clumsy confidence. He tried a fancy back-heel pass, missed completely,
and the whole team erupted in good-natured cheers.

And then there was Tyler. He was in the middle of it, laughing with the
rest, his movements fluid and easy.

Rogers stood next to his coach on the sideline, watching the spectacle.
"He doesn't look injured, Coach. He looks brand new."

Renner grunted, a low, guttural sound. That he did. From this distance,
the limp was gone. The hesitation was gone. The kid was practically
glowing under the lights. It had to be some sort of mind-over-matter
bullshit. Adrenaline and youthful ignorance. Pain was a physical
reality; you couldn't just *will* it away. Could you?

But there he was. And to be honest, a feeling Renner hadn't experienced
in years, a feeling he refused to name, was coiling in his gut.

He was terrified.

It wasn't a fear of losing. He had lost games before. It was a cold,
analytical process of identifying failure points and correcting them.
This was different. This was a fear of the unknown, of a variable he
couldn't quantify.

He had a plan for Tyler, the player. He had hours of film. He knew Tyler
favored his right foot, that he preferred a quick pass over a long
dribble, that he could be physically pushed off the ball. He had a cage
designed, and Marco was the key.

But he had no plan for Tyler, the idea.

He had just watched a team of nobodies, a team he had personally
dismissed as a joke, win a semi-final on the foot of their worst player.
They had won not with skill or strategy, but with a psychological gambit
so audacious, so contrary to every rule of competitive sport, that it
shouldn't have worked. But it did.

That was Tyler's real power. It wasn't his speed or his vision. It was
his ability to make others believe. It was a virus of confidence, and it
had infected his entire team. How do you defend against that? How do you
create a drill to counter a feeling?

Renner looked at his own team. They were disciplined. They were strong.
They were obedient. They would follow his every command. But were they
having fun? Did they believe in anything other than his system?

He blew his whistle, a sharp, angry sound that cut through the night
air. "Bring it in! Now!"

The Stallions sprinted to him, their faces set. Renner looked at each of
them, his gaze finally settling on Marco.

"The plan is the same," he said, his voice cold iron. "Marco, you are
his shadow. I don't care if he goes to the water bucket, you are there
to offer him a drink. He is not to be allowed a single touch in our half
of the field. You get a yellow card, I don't care. You stop him. Is that
understood?"

Marco just nodded, his face pale under the bright lights.

Renner looked back across the field at Tyler, who was now juggling a
ball with his little sister on the sideline, a wide, easy grin on his
face. He looked free. He looked unbeatable. And the cold knot in
Renner's gut tightened.
