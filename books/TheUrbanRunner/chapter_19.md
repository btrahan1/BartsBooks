# Chapter 19: The Wall

Turns out it wasn't some Art-of-War fakeout.

On the sideline, a trainer was working on the Wanderers' actual goalie,
a kid named Peterson, who was lying on a table, his face a mask of pain
as the trainer stretched his lower back. The injury was real.

Renner saw it, and the fury on his face curdled into a kind of grim,
tactical focus. This wasn't a trick; it was a desperate, insane
improvisation. The plan had to change. "SHOOT!" he roared, his voice
carrying across the field. "SHOOT FROM EVERYWHERE! TEST HIM! NOW!"

The Stallions, a team built on precision, were now given a simple,
brutal command: bombard the goal.

In the box, Tyler was jumping up and down, a bundle of nervous energy in
a ridiculously large yellow shirt. He looked less like a goalkeeper and
more like a kid who had been dared to stand in front of the net.

It didn't take long for the first test to arrive. The Stallions'
superior midfield took control, and a perfectly threaded pass sent their
star forward, a kid with scholarship offers from three different
colleges, on a fast break.

It was one-on-one. A striker of this caliber, against a novelist
goalkeeper, from 15 yards out. It was a foregone conclusion. The crowd
held its breath.

The forward bore down on the goal. He didn't need to look up; he knew
where the corners were. He dropped his shoulder to fake right, then cut
the ball back to his left foot to curl it into the far post. It was his
signature move.

But Tyler wasn't watching the ball. He was watching the player's hips.
The angle of the plant foot. The dip of the head. It was the same data
set he used to predict which way a pedestrian would dodge on a crowded
sidewalk. It was a route, a probability. He seemed to know where the
ball was going before the player even shot.

He didn't make a spectacular, diving save. He didn't need to. As the
striker shot, Tyler was already moving, taking two quick, explosive
lateral steps to his right. He dropped to one knee and scooped the ball
up as if the striker had passed it directly to him, the shot nestling
comfortably in his midsection.

Silence. Then a roar from the Wanderers' fans.

Tyler calmly rolled the ball out to his defender and started organizing
his back line, pointing and shouting instructions as if he'd been
playing the position his whole life.

The Stallions were stunned, but they followed their coach's orders. They
attacked in waves.

A 30-yard blast from Marco. Tyler, instead of trying to catch the
swerving shot, punched it decisively over the crossbar. Safe. Efficient.

A corner kick led to a chaotic scramble in the box. Players were
crashing, feet were flying. A traditional goalie might have stayed on
his line. Tyler didn't wait. He charged into the fray, a flash of
yellow, and leaped, not to catch, but to punch the ball 40 yards back
down the field, clearing the danger in a single, aggressive move.

The Stallions were dominating possession, outshooting the Wanderers ten
to one. But they couldn't score. Their precise, practiced plays were
being met by something they couldn't prepare for: a keeper who didn't
play by the rules. He wasn't in the right position, but he was always in
the *effective* position. He read every pass, anticipated every shot.
His defense, inspired by his impossible confidence, threw their bodies
in front of everything, blocking shots with a reckless bravery they
didn't know they possessed.

As the half-time whistle blew, the massive stadium scoreboard told an
impossible story: Northgate Stallions 0, Portside Wanderers 0.

The Stallions trudged off the field, a storm of frustration and
disbelief on their faces. They had thrown everything at him. It was like
trying to score on a psychic.

Tyler, on the other hand, jogged toward the locker room, pulling off his
gloves. He was mobbed by his euphoric teammates, who were patting his
back and ruffling his hair. He was laughing, not with the relief of
someone who had survived a siege, but with the pure, unadulterated joy
of someone who was having the time of his life. He was a wall, and he
had built it with mind-reading capabilities honed on the streets of his
neighborhood, one noodle delivery at a time.
