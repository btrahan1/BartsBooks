# Chapter 8: The Observer

The sun had begun its descent when Lady Elara approached the same
factor\'s stall, her attendant Kael a few paces behind her. She placed a
single, gleaming gold crown on the counter.

\"I require some information, Master Factor.\"

\"Of course, my Lady. Anything.\"

\"A boy was here, not long ago. Dusty tunic, short, dark staff. Called
Tim.\"

\"Ah, the boy,\" the factor chuckled, a dismissive wave in his hand.
\"He bought\... get this\... a whole five silvers of rice futures.\" He
said it as if it were the punchline to a joke.

Lady Elara did not smile. She stood in silence for a long moment,
considering the boy. It wasn\'t a gamble. It was a survivor\'s
calculation. Simple, direct, and almost certainly correct. He was
betting on the turning of the seasons and the hunger of the common man.
He was betting on a fundamental truth. \"Interesting,\" she said softly.

She pushed the gold crown forward. \"Keep that for your time.\" She then
produced a small purse and counted out five silver crowns, placing them
neatly beside the gold one. \"I think I will also buy five silver
worth.\"

The factor was stunned into silence. He couldn\'t fathom the logic. He
quickly masked his confusion and wrote out a new contract. As she turned
to leave, he finally found his voice. \"My Lady, if I may be so bold\...
our analysts predict a surplus in the wheat harvest. Rice is\... a poor
man\'s speculation.\"

Lady Elara paused. \"The analysts of House Vance eat cake and spiced
meats in a warm manor,\" she said, without turning around. \"That boy
has known hunger. I will trust his analysis over theirs.\" And with
that, she swept out of the square, leaving a very rich, and very
confused, factor staring after her.

From the shadowed archway of a textile merchant\'s shop, Elara watched
Tim. She watched as he bought a pair of simple, sturdy leather boots.
She watched as he chose a thick, dark grey wool tunic and a proper
leather belt.

Then he paused before a vendor selling sizzling kebabs on a stick. He
stood there for a long time, the battle raging within him. But with a
small, sharp shake of his head, he broke the spell. He turned his back
on the feast and bought a small bowl of steamed rice and pickled
vegetables. He sat on an overturned barrel and ate slowly, methodically.

He finished, returned the bowl, and paid. Then he did something that
made Elara\'s carefully composed expression falter. From his dwindling
supply of coin, he took out a single copper penny and placed it on the
counter. A tip.

A boy who had just denied himself a feast had willingly given a coin
away. It was not the act of a desperate survivor. It was the act of
someone who believed in a future. \"How very interesting,\" she murmured
to the empty air.
