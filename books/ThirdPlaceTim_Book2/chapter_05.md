# Chapter 4: The First Lesson

They took the servants' stairs.

While the rest of the novice class jostled and shoved their way up the
wide marble Grand Staircase, trying to secure prime positions for their
entrance, Tim and Christopher slipped through a narrow oak door behind a
tapestry of a hunting hound.

The stairs were steep, unlit, and smelled faintly of lye soap. Tim
climbed them with a silent, rhythmic grace. Christopher wheezed behind
him, clutching his chest, but he kept pace.

"Third landing," Christopher gasped. "Left turn. Through the linen
storage."

They emerged into a brightly lit corridor, completely empty, just ten
paces from the heavy double doors of the Hall of Ancestors. They were
the first ones there.

"Efficient," Tim noted, adjusting his collar.

"Survival," Christopher corrected, wiping sweat from his forehead with a
handkerchief. "My brother went here three years ago. He told me the
Grand Staircase is a bottleneck. Cassian and his lot will be elbowing
people into the banisters."

As if on cue, a muted roar of angry voices and the clatter of scabbards
echoed from down the main hall. A moment later, the double doors swung
open, not by students, but by a gust of wind generated by the woman
standing inside.

Master Elspeth was a woman made of sharp angles. Her grey hair was
pulled back so tight it looked painful, and her robes were a severe,
unadorned charcoal. She stood behind a lectern that looked like a
pulpit.

"Enter," she commanded. Her voice was quiet, yet it cut through the air
like a paper slice.

Tim and Christopher walked in. The Hall of Ancestors was a semi-circular
theater. Rows of tiered wooden benches looked down upon the lecturer's
pit. The walls were lined with oil paintings of severe-looking men and
women holding glowing balls of fire or lightning.

"Back row," Tim whispered. "Corner."

"The worst seats?" Christopher hissed. "We won't be seen."

"Exactly," Tim said.

They slid into the highest bench, nestled in the shadows of a stone
column. Moments later, the rest of the class spilled in. They were
flushed, irritated, and smoothing ruffled feathers. Cassian Redgrave
strode in, looking like he'd just shoved someone down a flight of stairs
(which he likely had). He took a seat in the front row, center,
spreading his arms to reserve the spaces on either side for his
sycophants.

Master Elspeth waited for the silence to become uncomfortable. Then she
waited ten seconds longer.

"History," she began, "is not a record of what happened. It is a record
of who had the power to write it down."

She picked up a piece of chalk and turned to the massive slate board
behind her. She wrote a single, complex sentence in the swirling High
Script. The chalk screeched.

She turned back to the class. Her eyes scanned the room, hunting.

"The First Maxim of the Academy," she stated. "Who can translate and
interpret?"

Tim squinted. From this distance, the white swirls on the black slate
looked like a tangle of yarn. He couldn't distinguish a single letter.
He felt a cold sweat prickle his neck. This was it. Day one, minute
five, and he was blind.

Elspeth's gaze bypassed the eager hands in the front row. She looked up,
past the middle tiers, directly into the shadows of the back corner.

"You," she said. "The boy in the wool. Novice 94."

The room turned. A hundred pairs of eyes focused on Tim. Cassian twisted
in his seat, a cruel smirk playing on his lips.

Tim stood up. His mind raced. He could say nothing and look like a fool.
He could guess and look like an idiot.

Next to him, Christopher didn't look up. He was staring intensely at his
own desk, terrified. But Tim saw Christopher's lips moving. A barely
audible whisper.

"*Power is a river...*" Christopher breathed, terrified but keeping his
end of the bargain. "*It flows from the Core...*"

Tim projected his voice, keeping his face impassive. "Power is a river,"
he repeated.

Christopher's whisper continued, rapid-fire. "*But without the banks of
Duty, it becomes a flood that destroys the land.*"

"It flows from the Core," Tim continued, pacing his words to match the
rhythm of his own breathing. "But without the banks of Duty, it becomes
a flood that destroys the land."

Master Elspeth stared at him. The answer was correct. It was the literal
translation. But she wasn't done.

"And the interpretation?" she challenged. "What does it mean for *you*,
Novice?"

Christopher went silent. This wasn't in the text. This was the trap.

Tim looked at the teacher. He thought about the quote. A river. Banks.
Floods. He didn't know about noble duty, but he knew about rivers. He
knew that when the river flooded the slums in the lower city, the rich
houses on the hill stayed dry while the poor drowned in the mud.

"It means," Tim said slowly, "that power without control is just a
disaster waiting to happen. The 'banks of Duty' aren't there to protect
the river. They are there to protect the people standing next to it."

A murmur rippled through the room.

"An... interesting perspective," Master Elspeth said, her eyes
narrowing. "Most students argue that the banks exist to channel the
river's force for greater impact. You argue they exist for containment."

"A flood destroys the crops, Master," Tim said simply. "You can't eat
glory."

A few stifled giggles erupted from the middle rows---the students from
the merchant families who understood the value of crops. Cassian
Redgrave scowled.

"Sit down, Novice 94," Elspeth said. She didn't praise him, but she
didn't flay him either. She turned back to the board. "We will begin
with the Era of the Three Kings..."

Tim sat down. He let out a breath he didn't realize he'd been holding.
Under the desk, he nudged Christopher's foot with his own.

Christopher looked up, his face pale but his eyes shining behind his
glasses. He pushed a piece of parchment toward Tim. On it, he had
written the High Script alphabet, and next to each letter, the Common
equivalent.

*Lesson One,* Christopher had scrawled at the top.

Tim looked at the cheat sheet. Then he looked at the board. Slowly,
painfully, the tangle of yarn began to untangle. The loop was an 'A'.
The sharp spike was a 'K'.

The class dragged on for two hours. Master Elspeth droned on about
lineages and treaties. Tim didn't take notes on the history. Instead, he
watched the room.

He noticed that Cassian Redgrave didn't take notes; he had a smaller boy
doing it for him. He noticed that the girl in the second row, wearing
the green of House Vane, was shivering, despite the heavy velvet of her
dress. The heating in the hall was uneven. He noticed that Christopher
wrote with a frenetic energy, his quill scratching so hard it threatened
to tear the paper.

When the bell finally tolled, signaling the end of the lecture, the room
erupted into chaos as students rushed for the exit.

"That was terrifying," Christopher whispered as they packed their bags.
"I thought you were dead."

"We made a deal," Tim said, tucking Christopher's alphabet chart into
his tunic. "You provide the words. I provide the delivery."

As they moved toward the aisle, Cassian stepped into their path. He
loomed over Christopher, ignoring Tim entirely.

"Pendergast," Cassian sneered. "I didn't know they let paper-pushers
into the Academy. Shouldn't you be back in your father's mill, pulping
trees?"

Christopher shrank back, clutching his books to his chest. "I... I..."

Tim stepped smoothly between them. He didn't look aggressive. He looked
bored.

"Cassian," Tim said pleasantly. "You have ink on your ear."

Cassian recoiled, his hand flying to his ear. "What? Where?"

"Right there," Tim pointed vaguely. "Probably happened when you were
having your scribe take your notes. It's very hard to stay clean when
you don't do the work yourself."

Several students nearby laughed. Cassian scrubbed at his ear furiously,
his face flushing red. There was no ink, of course.

"Come on, Christopher," Tim said, guiding his roommate past the
distracted bully. "We don't want to be late for Etiquette. I hear the
forks are very sharp."

As they walked out of the hall, Christopher looked at Tim with something
approaching awe.

"There was no ink, was there?" Christopher asked.

"Power is a river," Tim said with a small grin. "And sometimes, you just
have to muddy the water."
