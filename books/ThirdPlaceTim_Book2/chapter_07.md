# Chapter 6: The Ethics of Salad

The Hall of Protocol was not a classroom. It was a dining hall designed
by a sadist.

In place of desks, there were fifty round tables, each set with a
tablecloth so white it hurt the eyes to look at it directly. And upon
each table lay an armory.

Tim stood behind his assigned chair, staring down at the spread in
horror. There were five forks on the left. Five knives and three spoons
on the right. Above the plate lay two smaller, strange instruments---one
looked like a trident for a mouse, the other like a pair of tweezers
designed to torture insects.

"It's a trap," Tim whispered to Christopher, who stood at the adjacent
setting. "It has to be. No one needs this much metal to eat a chicken."

"It's not for chicken," Christopher whispered back, his voice trembling
slightly but filled with the authority of a boy raised in high society.
"It's a progression. From the outside in. Soup, fish, salad, main,
palate cleanser, dessert. The placement dictates the pacing."

At the front of the room, a man who looked like a well-groomed ferret
paced back and forth. He wore a suit of plum-colored silk and a monocle
that seemed glued to his eye. This was Master Percival.

"Etiquette," Percival announced, his voice high and nasally, "is not
merely about manners. It is about *Ethics*. It is the physical
manifestation of the Noble Soul."

He stopped and picked up a spoon, holding it like a holy relic.

"A peasant eats to fill his belly," Percival sneered. "A beast tears at
flesh to survive. But a Noble? A Noble eats to demonstrate *Restraint*.
To use the wrong fork is to admit that you lack the discipline to govern
your own appetites. And if you cannot govern your appetites, how can you
govern a province?"

Tim blinked. *They think using a salad fork makes them qualified to
rule,* he thought. *This place is insane.*

"Novices!" Percival barked. "Assume the position of readiness. The First
Course: Potage Parmentier."

Forty-nine students smoothly pulled out their chairs, sat down, placed
napkins in their laps, and reached for the soup spoon (far right, round
bowl).

Tim sat down. He looked at the array. He reached for a spoon.

"Psst," Christopher hissed without moving his lips. "Not that one.
That's for bouillon. You want the round one. Outside right."

Tim switched targets. He grabbed the round spoon. He gripped it like a
shovel.

"No," Christopher whispered, panic rising. "Not a fist. Pinch it. Like a
quill. Balance it on your middle finger."

Tim adjusted his grip. It felt flimsy. If he had to stab someone with
this spoon, he'd break his finger. But he mimicked Christopher's dainty
hold.

"Novice 94!"

Tim froze. Master Percival was looming over him. The monocle magnified
the instructor's judgmental eye to terrifying proportions.

"Stand," Percival commanded.

Tim stood, careful not to knock over the precarious tower of silverware.

"You look confused," Percival said, sniffing the air near Tim as if
poverty had a scent. "Perhaps the spread is... intimidating?"

"It is... extensive, Master," Tim admitted.

"Let us test your *Ethics*," Percival said, smiling cruelly. He pointed
to the strange, tweezer-like tool above the plate. "Identify this
instrument."

Tim looked at it. He looked at Christopher. Christopher couldn't whisper
now; Percival was too close. But Christopher did something subtle. He
puffed out his cheeks and made a tiny, clamping motion with his hand.

Puffed cheeks. Clamping.

Tim's mind raced. What food puffed up? Bread? No, you use hands for
bread. Pufferfish? Too dangerous.

Then he remembered the market stalls in the port district. The barrels
of slimy, shelled creatures.

"It is a snail tong, Master," Tim said.

Percival raised an eyebrow. "Correct. And why, pray tell, do we require
a specific tool for the snail?"

"Because," Tim said, feeling his way through the logic, "the shell is
hard. If you use a hammer, you crush the meat. If you use your fingers,
you look... hungry."

"Hungry," Percival repeated with distaste. "Yes. But what is the
*Ethical* reason?"

Tim was stumped. What did ethics have to do with snails? Did the snail
deserve a fair trial?

He looked at Christopher again. Christopher tapped his own chest, right
over the heart, and then made a slow, sweeping gesture outward.
*Protection? Generosity?*

No. Christopher tapped his chest again. *Self.* Then he pointed to the
imaginary person sitting across from him. *Others.*

He mimed a snail shell flying out of the tongs and hitting the invisible
person in the eye.

Tim understood. It wasn't about the snail. It was about collateral
damage.

"Because," Tim said, his voice gaining confidence, "a snail shell is
round and slippery. If one were to try and extract the meat with a
standard fork, one risks the shell slipping. It could become a
projectile."

He looked Percival in the eye. "The tong secures the hazard. It protects
one's dinner companions from flying debris. It is an act of
consideration for the safety of others."

Percival stared at him. The monocle seemed to fog up slightly.

"That is..." Percival paused. He looked for a flaw in the logic. He
wanted to say it was about tradition, or style. But Tim had framed it as
a safety issue. As a *duty*.

"...an acceptable answer," Percival finally conceded, sounding
disappointed. "It demonstrates a rudimentary understanding of the
Noble's burden to protect the weak. Even from appetizers."

He spun on his heel. "Sit down, Novice. And fix your napkin. It looks
like a bib."

Tim sat down. He adjusted the napkin. Under the table, he nudged
Christopher's foot.

"Projectiles?" Christopher whispered, barely suppressing a giggle. "You
turned Escargot into a siege weapon."

"I told you," Tim whispered back, picking up the soup spoon with his
new, dainty grip. "Everything is a fight. You just have to know which
weapon to use."

He dipped the spoon into the imaginary soup, tilting the bowl away from
him as he saw the other students doing. It felt ridiculous. It felt
inefficient. But as he looked around the room, he realized something.

No one was looking at him. For the first time all day, he blended in.

He took a sip of air from the empty spoon. It tasted like victory. And
slightly like silver polish.
