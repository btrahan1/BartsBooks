# Chapter 19: The Mud and the Merit

The afternoon sun beat down on the Training Grounds, turning the watered
earth into a thick, sucking quagmire. Master Kincaid stood on a dry
wooden platform, looking down at the chaos with a vein throbbing in his
temple.

"Pathetic!" Kincaid roared. "You are the future leaders of the Kingdom?
You look like pigs fighting over a trough!"

The exercise was simple in theory, brutal in execution: The Siege Drag.

The class was divided into squads of eight. Each squad had to drag a
weighted wooden sled---simulating a supply wagon---across two hundred
yards of knee-deep mud.

The problem was ego.

In the center lane, Cassian Redgrave's team was a disaster. Cassian was
at the front, screaming orders, pulling on the lead rope. But the three
boys behind him were also trying to be at the front, jockeying for the
"leadership" position. The four at the back were slipping in the churned
earth, resentful of doing the pushing. The sled hadn't moved ten yards.

To their left, a team of minor nobles led by a frantic boy named Roland
was faring no better. They were exhausted, shouting contradictory
instructions.

"Pull left!" "No, push right!" "Heave on three!" "I thought *you* were
counting!"

In the far right lane, however, there was silence.

The Squad---reinforced by Mara and a few other Draft Zone recruits---was
moving. It wasn't fast, but it was rhythmic.

"Hup... slide," Gareth rumbled from the back, where he acted as the
engine. "Hup... slide," Tim called from the front.

They moved like a caterpillar. Tim had arranged them by height and
leverage. Gareth and the heavy hitters pushed. Lyssandra and the smaller
students were on the sides, clearing debris. Tim and Christopher were at
the front, guiding the ropes.

They passed Roland's stalled team. Roland was red-faced, screaming at a
boy from House Miller who had fallen face-first into the muck.

"Get up, you oaf! You're dragging us down!" Roland shouted.

The Miller boy scrambled, slipping again. The rest of the team glared at
Roland, their morale shattered.

Tim signaled his team. "Halt."

"Tim," Christopher panted, wiping mud from his glasses. "We have
momentum. If we stop, the suction sets in."

"We have time," Tim said. He dropped his rope.

He waded across the lane divider toward Roland's team.

"What are you doing, peasant?" Roland snapped, wiping sweat from his
eyes. "Coming to gloat? Go on, pass us."

Tim looked at the sled. It was dug in deep. He looked at the team. They
were strong enough, physically. But they were broken.

"You're fighting the sled," Tim said calmly. "And you're fighting each
other. The sled is winning."

Roland bristled. "We are resolving a command structure dispute."

"You're shouting at a boy who is already face-down in the mud," Tim
pointed out. "That's not command. That's noise."

Tim looked at the Miller boy, who was wiping sludge from his eyes. "He
fell because he's pulling from a high angle. The rope is choking him. He
can't get traction."

Tim turned to the rest of the noble squad. They were looking at him with
a mix of hostility and exhaustion. They expected mockery. They expected
the "stick."

Tim gave them the carrot.

"You guys are strong," Tim said. "Stronger than my team. Individually,
you have more power. But look at my team."

He pointed. Gareth, Christopher, Lyssandra---they were waiting
patiently. No one was shouting. No one was jockeying for position.

"We aren't stronger," Tim said. "We're just... connected. A chain only
works if the links trust each other not to break."

He looked at Roland. "You want to lead? Then stop pulling the rope. Go
to the back."

"The back?" Roland sputtered. "The back is for... for the grunts."

"The back is where you can see the whole team," Tim corrected. "It's
where you can see who is slipping. It's where you can push when they
need help. A driver doesn't sit in front of the horses, Roland. He sits
behind them."

Roland stared at him. It went against every instinct of glory he had
been taught. Front was best. Front was First.

"Try it," Tim urged gently. "Just for ten yards. If it fails, you can go
back to shouting."

Tim stepped back. "Miller, shorten your grip. Get low. Use your legs,
not your back."

Roland hesitated. He looked at his exhausted, angry teammates. Then he
looked at Tim's calm, waiting squad.

Slowly, Roland let go of the lead rope. He waded through the mud to the
back of the sled. He placed his hands on the heavy timber frame.

"Alright," Roland said, his voice less of a shout and more of a call.
"Miller, get set. Brace."

The team braced.

"Ready..." Roland called from the back. "Heave!"

The team surged. Roland pushed from the rear. The Miller boy, with his
better grip, found traction.

The sled groaned. Then, with a sucking sound, it broke free. It slid
forward three feet. Then another three.

"Keep the rhythm!" Roland called, seeing the line moving. "One, two,
HEAVE!"

They were moving.

Tim watched them go for a moment. He saw the Miller boy smile through
the mud. He saw Roland looking not at the finish line, but at his
teammates, calling out adjustments.

Tim waded back to his own team.

"Why?" Christopher asked quietly. "They're the competition."

"They're students, Christopher," Tim said, picking up his rope. "And we
have to live with them for a year. It's better if they don't hate us."

He looked at Gareth. "Engine ready?"

"Engine ready," Gareth rumbled.

"Hup... slide," Tim called.

From the platform, Master Kincaid watched it all. He had seen Tim stop.
He had seen him speak to Roland. And he had seen the moment the noble
team transformed from a mob into a unit.

Kincaid crossed his arms, a deep furrow appearing between his brows.

"He aided the enemy," Kincaid muttered to himself. "In a race."

It made no sense tactically. It was a waste of advantage. And yet...
looking at Roland's team, Kincaid saw something he rarely saw in
first-years. He saw cohesion.

Tim hadn't just beaten them. He had fixed them.

Kincaid frowned, tapping his fingers on the railing. The boy wasn't
playing the game to win. He was playing a different game entirely. And
for the first time in years, the Master of the Training Grounds wasn't
sure what the score was.
