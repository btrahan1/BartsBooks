# Chapter 1: The Monastery Mule

The river was a cold, unrelenting mistress, but to Bran, she was simply the rhythm of his life. 

Every morning, before the sun had even dared to peek over the jagged peaks of the Ironwall Mountains, Bran was already at the water’s edge. He carried two wooden buckets, reinforced with iron bands that bit into his knuckles if he didn't hold them just right. At twelve years old, Bran wasn't tall for his age, but he was broad. His shoulders were a yoke of developing muscle, shaped by years of carrying burdens that would have broken a lesser boy.

"Move it, mule!" a voice barked from the path above.

Bran didn't look up. He didn't need to. The voice belonged to Brother Kaelen, a man whose piety was matched only by the sharpness of his impatience. Bran simply adjusted his grip and began the ascent. The path to the Monastery of the Sacred Fountain was a winding, treacherous thing, carved into the very stone of the cliffside. Each step required a focused calculation. One slip meant a spilled bucket; two meant a broken limb.

The Monastery of the Sacred Fountain was a place of healing, or so the bishops proclaimed. To the common folk of the valley, it was a sanctuary where the broken were mended by the divine light of the Fountain. To Bran, it was a sprawling complex of grey stone and cold corridors that demanded an endless cycle of scrubbing and hauling.

He reached the top of the path, his breath coming in steady, controlled bursts. This was a lesson he’d learned in the marrow of his bones—breath was the only bridge between the burden and the finish line. If you fought the weight, it crushed you. If you moved with it, letting the momentum of each step carry the load into the next, you could endure almost anything.

He emptied the buckets into the main cistern outside the infirmary and turned back toward the river. He would repeat this journey another twenty times before the morning bells summoned the brothers for First Prayer. 

As he walked, Bran’s mind wasn't on the labor. It was on the infirmary. Whenever he was tasked with sweeping the halls or scrubbing the floors near the healing chambers, he watched. He observed the way the priests laid their hands upon the wounded, the way they chanted in the Old Tongue to call forth that shimmering, sapphire radiance that the world called a System skill.

*Minor Mend. Cure Ailment. Seal Wound.*

Bran had even managed to sneak into the library once or twice, hidden behind a stack of old linens. He’d seen the Skill Tomes, bound in leather and pulsating with a faint, interior glow. He knew that for the priests, healing was a calculation of stats and mana—a matter of having reached the right level or possessing the correct prerequisites.

For Bran, there were no levels. He had no class, no digital destiny. He was just Bran, the boy who had been left on the monastery steps in a basket of reeds twelve years ago. The monks had taken him in, not out of charity, but because they needed hands to do the work the novices were too proud to touch.

"Still daydreaming, boy?" 

Bran blinked and realized he was standing near the doorway of the lesser infirmary. Father Thomas, one of the elder healers, was looking at him with a tired but not unkind smile. 

"I was just... looking at the tincture bottles, Father," Bran said, his voice quiet.

Thomas sighed, leaning against the doorframe. "You have a sharp eye, Bran. I’ve seen you watching. But you know the way of things. Without the favor of the Fountain, without the world's ledger recognizing your potential, these arts are closed to you. You are built for the heavy earth, child, not for the light."

Bran looked down at his hands. They were calloused and stained with river silt, tools of labor rather than instruments of grace. "I just want to understand how it works, Father. Why the light knits the skin back together. Where the blood goes when it's spilled."

Thomas chuckled. "Questions for a scholar, or a butcher. Neither of which you are. Now, go. The refectory won't sweep itself."

Bran nodded and moved on. But as he swept the long, echoing hall of the refectory, he thought about what Thomas had said. *Where the blood goes.* He’d seen a novice healer fail a Casting once. The boy had been nervous, his internal energy unstable. Instead of closing a deep gash on a woodsman's leg, the light had flared and sputtered. The wound had pulsed, the muscles underneath twitching in a way that had fascinated Bran more than it had horrified him.

He’d seen the way the muscle fibers were layered, like the grain of a fine piece of oak. He’d seen the white flash of bone. It wasn't just magic; there was a structure to it. A logic.

By mid-afternoon, Bran’s duties had shifted to the forge. He wasn't allowed to smith—that was for the Brothers—but he was required to pump the bellows and haul the heavy crates of coal. The heat was stifling, the air thick with the metallic tang of soot.

"Harder, boy! Keep the flame white!" the smith roared.

Bran leaned into the bellows, his entire body working in unison. He felt the pull in his calves, the tension in his thighs, the way his core had to lock to provide the leverage for his arms. He was starting to notice things. The more he worked, the more he felt the individual parts of himself. He wasn't just a single unit of "Strength"—that abstract number the monks talked about. He was a collection of moving pieces, a machine of flesh and bone.

That evening, as the shadows lengthened across the monastery courtyard, Bran sat on a stone bench near the gate, cooling his burning muscles. He was exhausted, but it was a familiar, almost comfortable ache.

"You're Bran, aren't you?"

A young novice, perhaps a year or two older than Bran, stood before him. He wore the clean white robes of a healer-in-training and looked down at Bran with a mixture of pity and curiosity.

"I am," Bran said.

"Father Thomas says you have an interest in healing. He told me to give you this. He says it’s an old text, too simple for the library, but it might settle your curiosity."

The novice handed him a small, worn book. It wasn't a Skill Tome; it didn't glow, and it had no magical resonance. It was just a book of hand-drawn diagrams and notes on basic anatomy and herbalism.

Bran’s heart raced as he took it. "Thank you. Please... tell Father Thomas thank you."

The novice shrugged and walked away, clearly wanting to get back to the more important business of mana cultivation.

Bran opened the book. The pages were yellowed and smelled of dried lavender. He saw a drawing of a human arm, with the skin peeled back to show the muscles and veins. It was crude, but to Bran, it was a revelation.

He stayed there until the bells rang for Silence, his eyes tracing the lines of the drawings, his mind already beginning to map the pictures to the familiar aches in his own body. He wasn't a healer. He wasn't a warrior. He was a mule.

But even a mule, he realized, had a heart. Even a mule had a skeleton.

Three days later, the Abbott called for him.

"Bran," the old man said, his voice like dry parchment. "The Monastery of Saint Jude in the South Reach is low on medicinal salts and consecrated oil. You are our most capable... porter. You will take the hand-cart and deliver these supplies. It is a four-day journey. Do not dawdle."

Bran bowed, his mind already racing. A journey. Outside the monastery walls. He’d never been further than the river.

As he prepared the cart the next morning, he tucked his small anatomy book into his tunic. He felt the weight of the supply crates—hundreds of pounds of salt and oil—and he felt the strength in his own legs. 

He set out as the sun began to rise, the wheels of the cart creaking against the stone path. He was twelve years old, a nameless orphan with a book of drawings and a body built by labor. He didn't know that his life as a "mule" was about to end.

By the afternoon of the second day, the world felt vast and silent. The monastery was a distant memory, hidden behind the folds of the hills. Bran was pulling the cart along a high ridge when he heard it.

It wasn't an animal sound. It wasn't the wind.

It was a sharp, rhythmic *crack*. Like someone was striking a heavy rug with a whip, but with a force that made the very air vibrate.

Bran stopped. He wiped the sweat from his brow and listened. 

There it was again. *CRACK.*

It was coming from a small clearing just off the main road, hidden behind a screen of ancient oaks. Curiosity, a trait the monks had tried to beat out of him for years, flared up. He secured the cart’s brake and crept toward the trees.

Peering through the leaves, Bran saw a man.

The man was older, his hair a shock of iron-grey, his skin tanned to the color of old leather. He wore simple, functional clothes—greaves of boiled leather and a tunic that left his arms bare. Those arms were unlike any Bran had ever seen. They weren't bulky, but they were defined with a precision that made them look like they’d been sculpted from marble.

But it was what the man was doing that held Bran spellbound.

He held a spear. It was a long, elegant thing of dark wood and a head of polished silver that caught the sun. He was moving through a series of forms, his body a blur of controlled motion. He would thrust, the spear head disappearing into the air with a whistle, and then he would roll, the shaft of the weapon spinning like a propeller.

Then, the man stopped. He planted his feet, his body coiling like a spring. 

He lunged.

The spear didn't just move; it *exploded*. The tip struck a solid wooden target—a thick trunk of a fallen tree—and the sound that followed was the *CRACK* Bran had heard. The spearhead buried itself deep into the wood, and the shockwave of the impact ruffled the man's hair.

Bran gasped, his hand flying to his mouth.

The man didn't turn around. He simply pulled the spear from the wood with a single, effortless motion.

"You're late with those supplies, boy," the man said, his voice calm and resonant as a bell. "But I suppose a moment of witnessing is worth a late delivery."

Bran froze. He had been seen.

"I... I’m sorry, sir," Bran stammered, stepping out from the trees. "I was just... I’ve never seen anyone move like that."

The man turned then, and his eyes—piercing, grey, and filled with a terrifying intelligence—locked onto Bran. He looked Bran up and down, his gaze lingering on the boy’s shoulders and the way he stood.

"You’re sturdy," the man observed. "Built like a pack animal. But you have the eyes of a seeker. Why were you watching my spear instead of my feet?"

Bran blinked. "The spear... it was so fast. But... I was actually watching your shoulder, sir. The way it didn't move until the very last second."

The man’s eyebrows shot up. A small, dangerous smile played across his lips. "Is that so? Interesting."

He walked over to a small pack resting near a rock and pulled out a book. It was larger than Bran’s anatomy text, and it hummed with a power that Bran could feel even from ten feet away.

"Common men look at the blade," the man said, walking toward Bran. "Wise men look at the feet. But only a master looks at the source of the lever."

He stopped in front of Bran. The man was tall, imposing, and smelled of ozone and dry earth. 

"I am Gaul," he said. "And since you’re so fond of looking, perhaps you should look at something worth seeing."

He held out the book. 

"The Way of the Spear," he said. "Take it as a payment for the time you’ve wasted watching me. Consider it a warning... or an invitation."

Bran reached out, his fingers trembling, and took the book. As his skin touched the cover, he felt a jolt of energy, a sudden, blinding awareness of every muscle in his own hand.

"Read it," Gaul said, already turning back toward the clearing. "And if you ever manage to make your body match the pictures, come find me in the lands of the Ironwall."

Before Bran could say a word, the man vanished into the trees, leaving only the fading echo of his footsteps and the heavy weight of the book in Bran’s hands.

Bran stood alone on the road, the hand-cart forgotten, as he looked down at the cover of the most important gift he would ever receive.
