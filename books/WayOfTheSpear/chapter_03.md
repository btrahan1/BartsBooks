# Chapter 3: The Torque of the Tide


He felt the torque rising from the earth, through his legs, through his core, and finally exploding in his hands. 

The water didn't just drip out; it sprayed. The fibers of the robe groaned under the tension, and Bran felt the "crimson" bones of his forearms holding steady against the strain. He was a human winch, a machine of flesh and bone.

"What in the name of the Fountain are you doing, boy?"

Bran startled, nearly dropping the robe. Brother Kaelen was standing on the path above, staring down at him with a look of utter bewilderment.

"I’m... doing the laundry, Brother," Bran said, his voice surprisingly steady despite his racing heart.

"You look like you're trying to wrestle a river demon," Kaelen grunted, descending the path. He walked over to the pile of wrung-out linens and picked one up. It was nearly dry, the fibers squeezed tight. "I’ve never seen a boy get so much water out of a wool cloak. And why aren't you sitting? You have a perfectly good rock right there."

Bran looked at the rock. "It was... in the way of my feet, Brother."

Kaelen squinted at him. "Your feet? It’s laundry, not a dance, Bran. You’re making enough noise to wake the Abbott. Keep the slapping down, or I’ll have you scrubbing the latrines instead."

But as the monk turned to leave, he muttered under his breath, "Strange boy. Built like a mule, acts like a madman."

Bran waited until the monk’s footsteps faded before he turned back to the river. He didn't feel like a madman. He felt *connected*. For the first time, the "mule" work and the "healing" arts were starting to blur together. The structure of the muscles he had seen in his anatomy book was coming alive in the heat of his labor.

He looked at his hands. They were red from the cold and the friction, but they weren't shaking. The "blue" fibers of his forearms felt dense, like the wood of Gaul's spear. 

He reached for the next piece of laundry—a heavy, water-logged rug from the refectory. It was the "boss" of the pile, a beast that usually took him twenty minutes of agonizing struggle to clean.

Bran didn't hesitate. He stepped into a wide, deep stance. He visualized the gold line of intent connecting his mind directly to the center of the rug. 

He breathed in, feeling the air fill his belly.

He breathed out.

*WHACK.*

The sound was like a thunderclap. The rug hit the stone with such force that a spray of suds and water flew up, catching the first true rays of the morning sun. For a split second, the droplets looked like diamonds, a shimmering halo around the boy on the riverbank.

Bran didn't stop. He moved into the wring, his body twisting in a perfect, anatomical spiral. He felt a strange, tingling sensation in the base of his spine—a warmth that wasn't from the sun. It was as if a small, glowing ember had been struck deep within his "crimson" core.

It wasn't a "click" yet. Not a full breakthrough. But the gate was creaking.

By the time he finished the pile, the sun was high in the sky. Usually, at this point, Bran would be crawling back to the monastery, his spirit crushed by the weight of the tasks still ahead. 

But as he loaded the damp-but-manageable laundry into the cart, he felt a surge of vitality. He looked at the washing stones. He looked at his own reflection in a quiet pool of the river.

He saw a boy with soot-smudged cheeks and wet hair. But he also saw the "Perfect Image" lurking beneath the surface. He saw the potential for density, for efficiency, for a body that didn't just endure, but thrived.

He pulled the cart back up the cliffside path, his gait rhythmic and strong. He wasn't thinking about the laundry anymore. He was thinking about the next page of the book. He was thinking about the day his "gold" intent would finally be strong enough to make the silk pages turn.

"Not a mule," he whispered to the wind. "A spear."

And as he crested the hill, he didn't even notice that the cart felt like nothing at all.
