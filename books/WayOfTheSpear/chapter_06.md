# Chapter 6: The Living Bellows

The forge of the Monastery of the Sacred Fountain was a place of roar and heat, a cathedral of iron and ash where the garnet architecture of the world was hammered into useful shapes. For the blacksmith, Master Torsten, it was a profession. For the apprentices, it was a path to a trade. 

For Bran, for the next seven days, it was a sanctuary of rhythm.

The bellows were a pair of massive, iron-ribbed lungs made of heavy ox-hide. They hung from the ceiling like the organs of a stone giant, operated by a long wooden lever that required a man's full weight to depress. Usually, the task was delegated to the newest apprentices, who would trade off every thirty minutes, their faces soot-streaked and their bodies trembling from the effort of keeping the "white" heat required for high-grade smithing.

"Too hot! Take it easy, boy!" Torsten roared on the first day, his voice barely audible over the hiss of the quenching tank. 

Bran, standing at the lever, had been pushing with the same ferocity he’d applied to the washing stones. He had been using his *A-Pose* and the coiling rotation of his core, driving the lever down with such force that the flames in the forge had flared a blinding, ozone-blue. The iron bar Torsten had been working was starting to slag.

"Sorry, Master Torsten," Bran said, his voice coming in the deep, rhythmic hiss he’d been practicing.

"You’re a mule, not a hurricane," the smith grumbled, wiping sweat from his brow with a leather apron. "The fire needs a steady heart, not a series of explosions. If you can't keep it at the gold-orange, I’ll send you back to the laundry."

Bran nodded. He closed his eyes. 

He didn't just stand at the lever; he became a part of it. He visualized the map of the *Wind*. He saw his own internal bellows—the deep lobes of his lungs—aligned perfectly with the external bellows of the forge. He realized that this wasn't just another chore; it was a resonance.

He began to breathe in synchronization with the lever. 

As he pulled the lever up, he took a deep, abdominal breath, filling his internal furnace. As he pushed the lever down, he exhaled a sharp, controlled stream of air. He wasn't just pushing wood and leather; he was pumping the Wind into the fire.

He focused on the resistance of the ox-hide. He felt the air rushing into the nozzle, the way the pressure built before the release. He began to adjust his internal rhythm. He didn't use his arms anymore. He used the anchor of his hips and the pressure in his core. He held the pause at the top of the stroke, letting the heat in his base settle, then released it in a measured, relentless tide.

By the second day, Torsten had stopped shouting. 

The fire in the forge was a thing of beauty. It sat at a constant, shimmering gold-orange, the color of a setting sun trapped in a stone hearth. It didn't flicker. It didn't sputter. It roared with a low, musical frequency that seemed to vibrate in Bran’s very bones.

Torsten stood before the anvil, his hammer-strikes matching the rhythm of Bran’s breathing. He looked over at the boy several times throughout the afternoon. Bran’s eyes were closed. His face was a mask of absolute focus, his breath coming in a steady, metallic *hiss-huff, hiss-huff*. 

"Look at him," one of the older apprentices whispered, a boy named Harek who had once been the primary "mule" before Bran had arrived. Harek was supposed to be learning how to mend horseshoes, but he found himself spellbound by the boy at the lever. "He’s been going for four hours. Without a break. And he isn't even sweating."

It was true. While the heat of the forge usually turned a man into a dripping mess within an hour, Bran stayed dry. His breath-engine was running so efficiently that his body wasn't wasting energy on excess cooling. He was a closed system, a furnace within a furnace.

"He looks like he's sleeping," Harek muttered. 

"Shut it and pick up that hammer," Torsten barked, though his own eyes were filled with a strange respect. "If you had half the discipline of that 'mule,' you’d be a journeyman by now."

By the fourth day, the novelty had worn off, replaced by a quiet awe. Bran had become a fixture of the forge. He would arrive at dawn, take his place at the lever, and remain there until the evening bells. 

Torsten had given him a new task. "I have about a hundred horseshoes that need mending. They’re old, rusted through, and require a delicate touch with the heat. Normally, I’d have the apprentices do it, but they can't keep the fire even enough. If you’re up to it, keep that flame at the 'Soft Peach' mark. Not a hair higher, not a hair lower."

Bran just smiled. He didn't open his eyes. 

"On it, Master," he said, the word coming out on a perfectly timed exhale.

What followed was a masterclass in biological engineering. Bran entered a state of flow that he hadn't known was possible. He became so attuned to the forge that he could feel the temperature change just by the way the air brushed against his skin. If the heat dipped even a fraction, his core would tighten, his Wind would intensify, and the lever would move a fraction faster.

He was no longer just hauling weight. He was managing energy. 

The horseshoes were mended with a speed that left the apprentices' heads spinning. Torsten worked two anvils at once, his hammer-strikes creating a rhythmic counterpoint to Bran’s breathing. The forge was no longer a place of chaotic noise; it had become an instrument, a resonant chamber where two masters—one of metal, one of self—were performing a duet.

"You see that, Harek?" Torsten asked as they stopped for a brief meal. Bran, as usual, stayed at the lever, his rhythmic breathing continuing even as he stood still. "That’s not strength. That’s *economy*. Every move he makes is for a reason. Every breath is a fuel injection."

The apprentices, who had once teased Bran for being a "mule," now sat in silence. They watched the way his chest rose and fell, the way his shoulders stayed low and relaxed while his forearms—those dense, corded things—held the heavy lever with a grip that never wavered. 

"He’s something else," Torsten said, his voice low. "He’s a man who’s found the bottom of his own soul and decided to build a house there."

By the seventh day, the "Soft Peach" heat had become so second-nature to Bran that it required almost no mental effort. He had internalised the rhythm of the bellows so deeply that he felt the Pulse of the fire as if it were his own.

And that was when it happened.

He was holding the lever at the top of the stroke, his belly full of Wind, his eyes closed. He was visualizing the air entering his blood, his heart pumping it...

*Thump.*

A sound. Not from the anvil. Not from the bells. 

An internal sound. 

It was a deep, resonant strike that seemed to originate in the very center of his being. It was the *Pulse*—the rhythmic compression of his golden intent into the blue furnace of his muscles.

*Thump.*

He felt a wave of warmth travel from his core, down through his arms, and into the wooden lever. For a split second, Bran felt as if he could "see" the air inside the bellows, the way the molecules were colliding, the way the wind was feeding the flame. 

The heat in the forge didn't just stay at "Soft Peach." It became... vibrant. The light radiating from the coals wasn't just peach-colored; it was a living, shimmering gold that made the very air in the forge feel thick with power.

Torsten stopped mid-swing, his hammer held high. He looked at the forge. He looked at the glowing bar of iron. He looked at Bran.

"What did you just do, boy?" the smith whispered, his voice trembling.

Bran opened his eyes. They were bright, clear, and filled with a terrifyingly calm intelligence. 

"I just... listened to the fire, Master," Bran said.

The Pulse didn't fade. It settled into a constant, low-frequency thrum at the base of his spine. 

He reached into his tunic and felt the book. It was vibrating. It was no longer just a heavy weight; it was a companion, a teacher that was finally starting to speak his language.

He didn't feel like a mule. He didn't even feel like a spear yet. 

He felt like the smith. Only the metal he was shaping wasn't iron. 

It was himself.

"I’m ready for the next page," he whispered to the roaring flames.

And in the silence of his own mind, he heard a sound like a single, silver crystalline strike. 

The next page turned.
