# Chapter 8: The Elastic Click

Brother Kaelen stared at Bran as if the boy had suddenly grown a second head, preferably one with horns. "Laundry? You want *back* on laundry duty? Most boys would pay a king’s ransom in copper to get off the riverbank, and you’re volunteering for another shift?"

Bran bowed, his head low, his face carefully neutral. "The river is quiet, Brother. It gives me time to contemplate the healing arts Father Thomas mentioned."

Kaelen grunted, scratching his balding pate. "Quiet? It’s a swamp of biting flies and soggy wool. But if you’re eager to soak your hands until they’re white again, I won't stop you. Silas and the others were complaining about the load from the visiting pilgrims anyway. Take the cart. Try not to come back after midnight this time."

"Thank you, Brother," Bran said, already turning toward the stables.

He had given his plan a great deal of thought. In the monastery, every moment was watched. If he started practicing the full splits in the cloisters or attempted the backbend during his sweeping duties, he wouldn't just be whispered about; he’d be hauled before the Abbott for suspected demonic possession. He needed solitude. He needed the darkness of the riverbank, where the only witnesses were the frogs and the indifferent moon.

But more than that, he had realized a fundamental flaw in his approach.

The night he had spent in his cell, trying to force his forehead to his knees, had taught him a painful lesson. Static stretching—simply pulling on a muscle until it gave—wasn't enough. It was a cold process. It didn't involve the Wind or the Pulse in a meaningful way. He didn't just need to be able to reach a position; he needed to be able to move *through* it. He needed to inhabit the unbounding.

When he reached the river, the moon was a silver sliver hanging over the Ironwall. The mist was thick, smelling of wet stone and ancient wood. Bran unloaded the first crate of pilgrim linens—heavy, coarse-woven fabrics that had traveled hundreds of miles. 

He didn't find a rock to sit on. He didn't even stand in his usual A-Pose. 

Instead, he looked at the diagram of the deeper stances in the book. He remembered seeing the women from the local village who occasionally came to the lower river to wash. They didn't stand over the stones as the monks did; they adopted a deep, effortless squat, sitting back on their heels with their knees wide, their backs straight as arrows. 

At the time, he’d thought it was just a local custom. Now, looking at the interconnected maps of the hip girdle, he understood it was a mechanical masterpiece. 

He tried to drop into the squat. 

His heels immediately lifted off the ground, his balance teetering. The blue tendons of his Achilles felt like they were about to snap, and his hips were screaming in protest. His dense, powerful frame was resisting the posture with every fiber.

"Intent," he whispered, his breath hissing in the night air. "Wind. Pulse."

He took a deep, abdominal breath, filling his garnet core. On the exhale, he didn't fight the floor. He visualized the blue ribbons of his calves elongating, becoming liquid. He sent the Pulse into his ankles, a rhythmic wave of warmth that softened the rigidity of the bone alignment.

Slowly, his heels touched the mud. 

He stayed there, his weight balanced, his back held straight. He felt a deep, dull ache in his pelvic floor, but for the first time, he felt grounded in a way the standing postures couldn't match. 

He reached for a pilgrim's robe. 

Working from the deep squat changed everything. He was no longer using his back for leverage; he was using the natural spring of his hips and ankles. As he scrubbed the heavy fabric against the stone, he moved his torso in a serpentine, fluid motion. He wasn't just a machine of levers anymore; he was a coiling serpent.

*Elongation,* he thought. *The Spear is long because it extends. I must extend.*

He began to incorporate the forward folds into the scrubbing. Instead of keeping his back rigid, he allowed his torso to drape over his thighs with every stroke, reaching further and further across the stone. He used the rhythm of the Pulse to time the stretch, feeling his hamstrings and lower back muscles giving way, millimeter by millimeter.

He looked at the washing stone, a massive, flat slab of granite. He placed a heavy, wet rug on it and then, instead of just slapping it, he stepped into a half-split. One leg forward, one leg back, his weight low. 

He struck the rug. 

But this time, the rotation didn't just come from his hips. It came from the extreme extension of his rear leg. He felt the force travel from his rear heel, through the elongated blue fibers of his groin, and explode through his shoulder. The reach was incredible. He could strike the far end of the rug with the same power he’d previously used for the center.

"The Unbounding is a multiplier," he realized, his eyes shining in the moonlight. "It’s not just about flexibility. It’s about the length of the lever."

By midnight, the mist had turned into a light drizzle, but Bran didn't notice. He was in a state of total integration. He was wringing out the pilgrim linens while standing on one leg, his other leg stretched high in a vertical hold to balance the torque of his arms. He was moving between squats and folds with a slowness that was more difficult than speed, forcing the Wind into every tight corner of his anatomy.

He felt the tendons in his legs changing. They were no longer just thick cables; they were becoming like the gut-strings of a master’s lute—taut, incredibly strong, but with a range of vibration that was terrifying. 

*The Elastic Click.*

It happened during the wringing of a particularly stubborn wool cloak. He had it gripped at both ends, his body twisted into a spiral. But instead of just holding the pose, he pushed the Pulse into his core and *released* the tension in his hips. 

He felt a sensation like a cord being pulled to its limit and then suddenly offering a reservoir of length. 

A sound—not a thump or a crack, but a low-frequency hum—vibrated through his legs. 

Bran fell into a full split. 

His pelvis hit the soft mud of the riverbank. His legs were stretched in a perfect, horizontal line. 

He didn't feel pain. He felt... *open*.

For a second, his entire golden intent flared white. He could feel the river, the air, the cold drizzle, and the book in his tunic as a single, unified field. He felt the Pulse of the earth itself, a slow, deep rhythm that matched the rotation of his now-aligned hip joints.

He stayed there, the pilgrim's cloak still held in his hands, staring at the moon. 

He had done it. He had unseated the "bound" state of his generic body. He had clicked into the elastic range of the Perfect Image.

Slowly, carefully, he pulled himself out of the split. His legs felt strange—long, light, and filled with a potential energy that made every step back to the cart feel as if the ground itself were giving him a gentle push. 

He began to load the damp laundry. He didn't just bend over; he folded at the waist, his palms flat on the wood of the cart as he secured the crates. He didn't just lift; he used a deep squat to provide a platform of power that felt twice as strong as his previous efforts.

The walk back to the monastery was a revelation. 

Bran experimented with his gait. He found that by allowing his hips to rotate further with every step—using the new flexibility of his unbound groin—his stride length increased by nearly six inches. He was moving faster with less effort. He was a spear that had been elongated, its shaft refined and balanced.

When he reached the gates, the morning mist was already starting to rise. Brother Kaelen was there, huddled in a wool cloak, his face sour.

"About time, boy. I was about to give your cold porridge to the horses. Why are you so late? And why do you look like you’ve been swimming in the mud?"

Bran didn't look down at his mud-streaked trousers. He stood perfectly still, his weight balanced, his spine a graceful, fluid line. 

"I was mastering the weight of the water, Brother," Bran said. 

Kaelen squinted at him. He noticed the way Bran’s legs were placed—a subtle, wide stance that looked both casual and incredibly stable. He also noticed that the boy seemed... taller? No, not taller. Just more *present*. 

"Hmph. Mastered the weight, have you? Well, go master the drying racks. And try not to fall over your own feet. You're walking like a cat in a yarn shop."

Bran bowed—a deep, fluid fold that brought his head nearly to his knees before he rose in a single, unbroken motion. 

Kaelen froze, his mouth hanging open. "What... what was that?"

"A show of respect, Brother," Bran said, a small, knowing smile on his face.

As Bran pulled the cart toward the drying racks, he felt the resonance of the third page turning in his mind. The Elastic Click had settled into his bones. 

He mastered the Structure. 
He mastered the Wind. 
He mastered the Pulse. 
And now, he mastered the Unbounding.

He looked at his hands, the skin red and raw from the laundry water, but the movement underneath as fluid as quicksilver. 

He was twelve years old. He was a nameless orphan. He was a monastery mule.

But as he looked up at the first rays of the sun, Bran knew the truth. 

The mule was dead. 
The spear was almost ready.
And for the first time in his life, the Perfect Image didn't look like a costume or a dream. 

It looked like a reflection.
