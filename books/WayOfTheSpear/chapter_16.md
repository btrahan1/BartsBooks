# Chapter 16: The Art of the Transition

The next night found Bran exactly where he had been for the last three—at the river’s edge, a ghost of silver skin and focused intent beneath the watching moon. The Ironwall peaks were silhouettes of jagged glass against the stars, and the mountain air was a cold, sharp blade that bit into his bare shoulders. 

Bran didn't feel the cold. His breathing was deep and steady, fueling a heart-rate that was currently dedicated to a series of experimental exercises he’d devised.

He was balancing on one foot atop a moss-covered boulder. His other leg was extended in a perfect, horizontal line, his torso tilted to maintain the center of gravity. He looked like a dancer. His lines were clean, his density was absolute, and he moved from point to point with a precision that was, frankly, terrifying.

Stance to stance. Point to point. 

*Clack.* He shifted his weight.
*Clack.* He rotated his hips.
*Clack.* He locked into a deep lunge.

It was efficient. It was powerful. It was the most beautiful thing a machine could do. 

From the shadows of a weeping willow, Elana watched, her breath held. She saw the raw, anatomical perfection of his every move. She saw the golden lines of focus in his mind almost physically manifesting in the way he held the space. 

"What is he doing?" she whispered to herself. 

She had expected him to be practicing the counts. She had expected the mechanical one-two-three of the rehearsal tent. But Bran wasn't counting. He wasn't even looking at the shore. He was staring down into the water.

The stream was clear, shallow, and fast-moving, the silver moonlight refracting off the pebbles on the bottom. In a small, quiet eddy near the boulder, a school of mountain trout were hovering against the current. 

Bran remained perfectly still, his balancing leg as solid as the granite beneath it. He was watching the fish. He had been watching them for an hour.

To any other observer, the fish were just animals. To Bran, they were a lesson in the arts of the body that he hadn't found in any of his books. They didn't seem to mind his presence. He was so still, his rhythm so grounded, that he had effectively merged with the background. He watched a large trout drift a few inches to the left, then dart forward with a sudden burst of speed to catch a water-fly.

Bran’s eyes widened. 

He didn't see the movement as a step. He didn't see it as a shift from point A to point B. 

"They don't... snap," he murmured, his voice barely a breath. "They glide."

In the monastery, the monks had taught him that movement was a series of positions. To throw a wash-bucket, you stood in Stance A, then you moved to Stance B, then you released at Stance C. It was a teleportation of force. *Clack. Clack. Clack.*

The fish didn't teleport. 

When the trout moved, there was no beginning and no end to the action. It wasn't a series of pulses; it was a single, continuous hum of intentional energy. They didn't move *to* a point; they occupied the space between the points.

"Swish, swish, swish," Bran whispered, his mind racing. "Not clack, clack, clack."

He realized that his unbounding had given him the range, and his breath had given him the fuel, but his rhythm was being strangled by his need for points. He was trying to be "correct" at every micro-second, which meant he was stopping and starting his momentum a thousand times a minute. He was a series of perfect snapshots when he needed to be a living, flowing stream.

*Transition,* the word bloomed in his mind like a flower in the Sacred Fountain. *The secret isn't the stance. The secret is the motion BETWEEN the stances.*

He looked at the frigid water. It looked like liquid needles. Bran didn't hesitate. He stepped off the boulder and lowered himself into the stream. 

The cold was astronomical. It hit his garnet frame with a shock that threatened to shatter his breathing cycle. His muscular channels immediately tried to go rigid, to anchor themselves against the invasion of the frost.

"No," Bran commanded his body, his voice a low, vibrating hum that matched the river’s roar. "Don't fight it. Be it."

He ignored the needles. He ignored the numbness. He resumed his dance there in the center of the rushing water, the current pushing against his waist, the trout darting around his ankles. But he didn't focus on the steps this time. He didn't try to "snap" into the lunge. 

He focused on the transition. 

He took a step. Instead of placing his foot and then shifting his weight, he allowed the weight to flow forward like the water around him. He felt the tension in his thighs not as a lock, but as a guide. He was gliding through the resistance. 

He spun. 

Usually, this was where he looked like a spinning top that had been hit with a hammer—all torque and no grace. Now, he didn't think about the start or the finish of the rotation. He thought about the "swish" of the movement. He allowed his golden focus to lead his arms in a wide, fluid arc, his muscles trailing behind in a continuous wave of energy.

Elana's jaw dropped.

The boy in the river was no longer a clockwork doll. He was something else. He was a part of the river. The water hit his body and flowed around him, and he flowed with it. He was moving with a slowness that felt like it had infinite speed, his transitions so seamless that Elana couldn't tell when one movement ended and the next began.

Bran closed his eyes. He didn't need to see. He could feel the hum now. 

It was a resonant frequency that connected the pulse in his core to the pulse of the stream. He felt the elastic range of his tendons not as a limit, but as a medium for the flow.

He was gliding. 

He executed the three-step flow—the one that had left him a tangled mess in the rehearsal tent. 

One... Two... Three...

There was no thud. There was no lurch. It was a swish of silver skin and moonlit water. He moved across the riverbed with the ease of the trout he had been watching, his transition into the final lunge so fluid that it looked like he was being pulled by an invisible tide.

He stopped, standing knee-deep in the center of the stream. He didn't freeze. The movement didn't end. It simply settled, like the water in an eddy. 

Bran breathed out—a long, controlled exhaust of wind that carried the last of his frustration with it. He opened his eyes, and for a second, they were as clear and deep as the mountain stream.

"Gliding," he whispered. 

He reached into his tunic and touched the silk book. He didn't need to turn the page to know what was coming. He had found the rhythm not in a drum, but in the transition. He had learned that to be a Sovereign, you couldn't just be the anchor. You had to be the river.

Elana stepped out from the shadows of the willow, her face filled with an expression that was past awe and into something that looked like prayer.

"I’ve spent twenty years learning that, Bran," she said, her voice a soft, vibrating thread in the night air. "I’ve spent my entire life trying to find the swish in the middle of the snap."

Bran turned toward her. He was shivering now, the cold of the water finally reaching his core, but he didn't care. He was smiling. A real, honest-to-God smile that reached all the way to his radiant center.

"The fish told me," Bran said.

Elana laughed—a bright, warm sound that made the frigid night feel suddenly manageable. She reached out a hand to help him out of the water.

"Then we’ll have to thank the fish, Bran of the Monastery," she said. "Because tomorrow, you're not just going to be the anchor. You're going to be the flow."

Bran stepped out of the river, his legs feeling long, light, and filled with a potential that finally had a language. 

He was thirteen years old. He was a healer. He was a spear. He was the anchor.

And as he walked back toward the camp, his gait was no longer a walk. It was a transition. 

The mule had learned how to glide. And the world, Bran realized, was finally starting to look like it was made of water.
