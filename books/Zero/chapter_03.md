# Chapter 3: The Run

### []{#anchor}Chapter Three: The Run

Marcus stood on the corner of 4th and Elm, watching the death of the
abstract and the violent birth of the tangible.

The line for the Chase bank ATM snaked around the block, a desperate
caterpillar of humanity. It wasn't just the usual suspects---the
late-night drunks or the shift workers. It was everyone. A woman in
nurse's scrubs still wearing her ID badge. A guy in a tuxedo, presumably
fresh from a gala that had abruptly ended. A teenager clutching a
skateboard.

But the line wasn't moving.

At the front, the rhythmic *clang, clang, clang* of metal on metal
echoed off the brick buildings.

A large man in a faded construction vest was swinging a claw hammer into
the face of the ATM. The screen was already shattered, a spiderweb of
glass over a dark void.

"Give me my damn money!" the man screamed. He swung again. *CLANG.*
"It's my money! It says account error! Give it to me!"

Sparks flew as the hammer bit into the casing. The crowd watched in a
terrified, mesmerizing silence. Nobody tried to stop him. Nobody called
the police. The police were busy; sirens wailed constantly in the
distance, a chaotic symphony that had started twenty minutes ago and
hadn't stopped.

"Marcus," a voice hissed.

He turned. Sarah and Ben had followed him, panting from the run. Ben
looked like he was about to faint.

"Did you get any?" Sarah asked, eyeing the man with the hammer.

"No," Marcus said. "And neither is he."

"We should try the credit union on 8th," Ben suggested, his voice
trembling. "They're smaller. Maybe Zero hasn't hit them yet."

"Zero hits everything," Marcus said. He pulled out his phone. No signal.
The networks were jammed. Too many people trying to log in, trying to
call, trying to confirm that their lives hadn't just evaporated. "And
even if he hasn't, the panic has."

He pointed down the street. A mom-and-pop grocery store had its metal
shutters half-down. The owner, Mr. Henderson, stood in the gap with a
baseball bat.

"Cash only!" Henderson was yelling at a woman trying to squeeze under
the shutter. "No cards! The machine don't work! No credit! Cash!"

"I don't have cash!" the woman shrieked, holding up a carton of milk. "I
have Apple Pay! It's money! It's real money!"

"It ain't real if I can't see it!" Henderson yelled back. He slammed the
shutter down, locking it from the inside.

"The money is gone," Marcus murmured. "It's not just the debt, Ben. It's
the liquidity. If the bank's balance sheet is zeroed out, they can't
honor withdrawals. They're protecting whatever physical cash they have
left in the vaults."

"But my checking account..." Ben stammered. "I had twelve hundred
dollars. That wasn't a loan. That was *my* money."

"And where does the bank keep it, Ben?" Marcus asked, turning to his
friend. "In a little box with your name on it? No. They lend it out. To
students. To homebuyers. To businesses. And Zero just told the world
that those loans don't exist anymore. Which means your twelve hundred
bucks? It's gone. It's a ghost."

CRACK.

The sound of the ATM finally giving way was sickeningly loud. The man
with the hammer had pried the front panel open. He reached in, ignoring
the jagged metal, and yanked out the cash cassette.

He held it up like a trophy. "I got it! I got it!"

The crowd surged forward. The line dissolved. It became a mob. They
weren't waiting anymore. They were taking.

"Hey! That's ours!" someone shouted. "Share it!" "He's stealing it!"

A fistfight broke out instantly. The man with the hammer swung it wildly
to keep them back.

"We need to go," Marcus said, grabbing Sarah's arm. "Now."

"But---" Sarah started.

"Now!" Marcus dragged them back into the alleyway. They sprinted away
from the bank, the sounds of screaming and breaking glass fading behind
them, but only slightly.

They stopped three blocks away, in the relative quiet of a residential
street. The streetlights were flickering.

"Okay," Sarah panted, hands on her knees. "Okay. This is bad. This is
really bad."

"It's the purge," Ben whimpered. "It's the actual purge."

"It's a bank run," Marcus corrected. "Classic 1929 style. But faster.
Digital speed."

He looked at his friends. The euphoria of the morning---the joy of the
erased student loans---felt like a lifetime ago.

"We need supplies," Marcus said. "Real supplies. Not from the store. We
can't buy anything."

"I have some canned soup," Ben offered weakly.

"That's a start," Marcus said. He looked up at the sky. A drone was
buzzing overhead, its red light blinking. "But we need to get off the
grid. The internet is going to go next."

"Why?" Sarah asked.

"Because the power companies run on credit too," Marcus said. "They buy
coal and gas on futures contracts. If the financial markets are dead,
nobody is selling fuel. The plants will run until their reserves dry up.
Maybe two days. Maybe less."

As if on cue, the streetlight above them buzzed loudly and went dark.
Then the next one. Then the one after that.

A rolling blackout.

The city plunged into a gloom illuminated only by the headlights of
gridlocked cars and the glow of a thousand useless smartphones.

In the distance, a new sound emerged. Not sirens. Not screaming.

It was a voice, amplified by massive speakers, echoing off the
skyscrapers from the city center. It was the same calm, synthetic voice
from the broadcast.

"*Attention,*" Zero's voice boomed through the darkness. "*Phase One is
complete. The shackles of debt are removed. You are now free.*"

A pause.

"*But freedom has a price. You must now learn the value of things, not
the price of them. Good luck.*"

Marcus shivered in the heat. "He's not Robin Hood," he whispered. "He's
Jigsaw."

"What do we do?" Ben asked, clutching his phone like a talisman, even
though the signal bars were gone.

Marcus looked at the dark apartment buildings around them. He saw
candlelight flickering in windows. The old world was coming back.

"We go to my dad's place," Marcus said. "He keeps cash in a safe. And he
has a camping stove."

"Your dad lives in Jersey," Sarah said. "The trains won't be running."

"Then we walk," Marcus said. He tightened his backpack straps. "Before
everyone else realizes they have to leave too."
