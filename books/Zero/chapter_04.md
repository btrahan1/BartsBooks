# Chapter 4: The Exodus

### []{#anchor}Chapter Four: The Exodus

The George Washington Bridge was a parking lot. A graveyard of idling
engines and dead batteries. The red taillights stretched infinitely into
the haze, a river of blood suspended over the Hudson.

Marcus, Sarah, and Ben walked along the pedestrian path on the south
side. The steel beneath their feet vibrated with the collective tension
of thousands of vehicles that weren't moving.

"This is unbelievable!" Ben shouted, though he didn't need to. The noise
of the city behind them had dulled to a low, ominous hum, but the
silence on the bridge was worse. It was the silence of confusion.

"What's wrong with us?" Ben continued, kicking a crushed soda can. It
skittered near the edge, flirting with the drop to the black water
below. "We cheered. We literally cheered."

"We were desperate, Ben," Sarah said. Her voice was flat. She was
trudging, head down, watching her boots step over discarded trash.
"Desperate people don't read the terms and service."

"We're smart, aren't we?" Ben asked, looking at Marcus. He sounded like
a child asking if Santa was real. "We went to college. We have degrees.
We understand socio-economics."

"I used to think so," Marcus said. He adjusted the strap of his
backpack. It was digging into his shoulder. Inside, he had three bottles
of water, a flashlight, a knife he'd found in his kitchen drawer, and a
first-aid kit. It felt like both too much and pitifully little.

They weren't the only ones walking. The pedestrian path was crowded.

A family pushed a shopping cart filled with diapers and bottled water.
The father wore a tailored suit, tie loosened, sweating profusely. The
mother was on her phone, screaming at a dead signal.

"It won't connect!" she shrieked. "Why won't it connect? I need to
transfer the crypto!"

"Crypto is dead, Karen!" the man snapped. "The exchanges are down! The
servers are down! Just push the cart!"

Marcus kept his head down. "Eye contact is an invitation," his dad used
to say. *Don't invite trouble.*

"Look at that," Sarah whispered, pointing to the gridlocked roadway.

A Tesla sat motionless in the center lane. Its gull-wing doors were
open. The driver, a young guy in a hoodie, was sitting on the hood,
staring at a laptop.

"Hey!" Ben called out before Marcus could stop him. "Any news?"

The guy looked up. His face was illuminated by the blue glow of the
screen. He looked haunted.

"The ledger," the guy said. "I downloaded a copy of the virus before the
net went dark locally. I'm looking at the code."

Marcus stopped. Curiosity won out. He walked over to the barrier
separating the walkway from the road. "And?"

"It's not just deletion," the guy said. He turned the laptop around.
Lines of code cascaded down the screen. "It's randomization. Zero didn't
just erase the debt. He scrambled the ownership keys."

"Meaning?" Marcus asked.

"Meaning... nobody knows who owns what anymore," the guy laughed, a dry,
cracking sound. "See this car? The software just locked me out. It says
I'm not the registered owner. It says the owner is..." He squinted at
the screen. "...a shell company in Panama that doesn't exist."

"So it's a brick," Sarah said.

"It's a sixty-thousand-dollar paperweight," the guy corrected. He
slammed the laptop shut. "I'm walking to my parents' house in
Connecticut. Good luck with the revolution." He hopped off the hood,
grabbed a backpack from the passenger seat, and started walking north,
weaving between the stalled cars.

"Randomization," Marcus muttered. "Chaos."

"We need to keep moving," Sarah said. She sounded scared now. The
bravado of the protest was gone.

They walked for another hour. The heat broke as a thunderstorm rolled
in, soaking them to the bone. The rain was cold, a shock against the
humid air.

By the time they reached the Jersey side, the mood had shifted. The
initial confusion of the exodus was turning into aggression.

They passed a gas station just off the highway. It was a war zone.

Cars were abandoned at the pumps. A group of men were fighting over a
gas can. One of them swung a tire iron.

"Hey! Back off!"

Marcus grabbed Ben's collar and pulled him toward the treeline. "Off the
road. Now."

"But we need water," Ben said, eyeing the convenience store. The windows
were smashed.

"There's nothing left in there, Ben," Marcus said. "Look."

Inside the shattered store, shelves were bare. A man was sitting on the
floor, eating a bag of Doritos, a shotgun across his lap. He locked eyes
with Marcus. He didn't raise the gun, but he didn't smile. He just
chewed.

They moved into the woods that bordered the highway. The ground was
muddy, slick with wet leaves.

"My dad's place is another ten miles," Marcus said. "We stick to the
back roads. No highways. No commercial strips."

"What about the police?" Sarah asked. " The National Guard?"

"If the government can't pay them," Marcus said, pushing a wet branch
out of his face, "do you think they're going to show up? Their mortgages
just got zeroed too. Their families are hungry too."

"So we're on our own," Ben said.

"We always were," Marcus said. "Zero just took away the illusion that we
weren't."

They trudged on in silence. The rain washed away the sweat, but it
couldn't wash away the reality.

They were smart kids. They were the future. And they were walking
through the mud in the dark, hungry and afraid, because a computer
program had decided to press reset.

"Marcus?" Ben asked after a long silence.

"Yeah?"

"If... if we get through this. If the banks come back online. Do I still
owe the money?"

Marcus stopped. He looked back at Ben. Rain dripped from Ben's nose. He
looked pathetic. And hopeful.

"Ben," Marcus said softly. "If we get through this, money isn't going to
mean what you think it means."

He turned back to the dark path.

"Keep walking."
