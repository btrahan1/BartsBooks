# Chapter 5: The Paper Chase

### []{#anchor}Chapter Five: The Paper Chase

The realization hit them like a physical blow, right there in the muddy
shoulder of a New Jersey backroad.

"It's just paper," Ben whispered. The horror in his voice was absolute.

Marcus dropped his backpack into the mud. He ran a hand through his
soaked hair. "My dad's safe. It has... I think maybe ten thousand
dollars in it. He's been stashing it for years. 'Emergency fund,' he
called it."

"And what is that ten thousand dollars?" Sarah asked, her voice rising
in pitch. "It's a promise. A Federal Reserve Note. 'This note is legal
tender for all debts, public and private.' That's what it says on the
bill."

"But there are no debts," Marcus said, staring at the ground. "Zero
erased the debts. And if the government can't track ownership, they
can't collect taxes. If they can't collect taxes..."

"...the government has no revenue," Sarah finished. "Which means the
promise backing that piece of paper is void."

Ben sat down on a wet log. He looked sick. "So we're walking ten miles
to steal... confetti?"

"We're walking ten miles to steal kindling," Marcus corrected grimly.

"Wait," Ben said, a flicker of desperation in his eyes. "People still
trust cash. Henderson at the bodega wanted cash. That guy with the
hammer wanted cash. It has inertia. People will still take it for a
while. A week? Two weeks?"

"Maybe," Marcus allowed. "Old habits die hard. But think about it, Ben.
Why does Henderson want cash? So he can go to his supplier and buy more
milk. But does the supplier want cash? No. The supplier is a massive
corporation. They want electronic transfers. They want credit
verification. They want *numbers*."

"The supply chain is broken at the top," Sarah said, pacing in a small
circle. "The cash works at the bottom layer---me buying a candy bar from
you. But it stops working the second you try to restock the candy bar."

"So the cash has a shelf life," Marcus said. "It's worth something
today. Less tomorrow. And in a week..."

"It's toilet paper," Ben finished.

"We still have to go," Marcus said, picking up his bag. "Even if it buys
us three days of food. That's three days we're not starving."

"But what happens after three days?" Sarah asked. "What's the new
currency?"

Marcus looked at the knife clipped to his belt. He looked at the water
bottles in his bag.

"Utility," he said. "Can you eat it? Can you drink it? Can you use it to
protect yourself? Can you use it to fix something? That's the new gold
standard."

Ben looked down at his own hands. "I have a Master's in Urban Planning.
I know how to design a walkable city center."

"Useless," Marcus said, not unkindly.

"I can code in Python," Sarah said.

"Useless," Marcus repeated. "Unless you can code a tomato plant to grow
faster."

"I... I worked at a bike shop one summer," Ben stammered. "I can fix a
derailleur. I can patch a tire."

Marcus stopped. He looked at Ben. A slow smile spread across his face.

"Ben," Marcus said, gripping his friend's shoulder. "That. That is worth
more than my dad's ten thousand dollars. In a world with no gas,
bicycles are kings."

"Really?" Ben stood up, standing a little taller.

"Really. But we still need to get to the house. My dad has something
else in that safe besides cash."

"What?" Sarah asked.

"Silver coins," Marcus said. "Eagles. Real metal. He bought them off QVC
in the 90s because he was paranoid about Y2K."

"Y2K," Sarah scoffed. "The glitch that didn't happen."

"Well," Marcus gestured to the dark, silent world around them. "It's
happening now. Just twenty years late."

They started walking again. The rain eased up, leaving a dripping, humid
silence.

"Hey," Ben said softly. "You know what the worst part is?"

"What?"

"I was finally going to buy a house. With the debt gone, my credit score
would have been perfect."

Marcus didn't have the heart to tell him that credit scores were just
another list of numbers that Zero had likely turned into gibberish.
Instead, he just kept walking.

They reached the outskirts of his father's neighborhood an hour later.
It was a suburb of cookie-cutter houses, manicured lawns, and two-car
garages.

It was pitch black. No streetlights. No porch lights.

But there was light.

In the center of the cul-de-sac at the end of the street, a bonfire was
burning. People were gathered around it.

"Stay low," Marcus whispered. They crouched behind a parked SUV.

The neighbors were cooking. The smell of grilling meat wafted through
the air---steaks, burgers, sausages.

"They're having a block party?" Ben whispered, confused. "It's the
apocalypse and they're having a barbecue?"

"The freezers died," Sarah realized. "The power has been out for hours.
The meat is thawing. If they don't cook it now, it rots."

"Feast or famine," Marcus murmured. "Tonight, they feast."

"We should go down there," Ben said, his stomach growling audibly.
"They're your dad's neighbors. They'll share."

Marcus watched the scene. He saw Mr. Miller, the neighborhood watch
captain, standing by the fire. He was holding a golf club. He wasn't
using it to putt. He was holding it like a weapon, scanning the darkness
beyond the firelight.

"Look at Miller," Marcus said. "Look at how they're standing. Shoulders
tight. Watching the perimeter."

"They're scared," Sarah said.

"They're tribal," Marcus corrected. "Yesterday, they were neighbors.
Today, they are a clan with a rapidly depleting food supply. And we are
outsiders."

"But it's your dad's house!" Ben hissed.

"Let's check the house first," Marcus said. "Quietly."

They skirted the edge of the light, moving through backyards, climbing
over fences. Marcus knew this terrain. He'd played manhunt here as a
kid.

They reached the back door of his father's house. It was locked. Marcus
felt for the spare key under the fake rock in the garden.

It was gone.

"Dad?" Marcus whispered. He tried the handle. Locked tight.

He peered through the kitchen window. The house was dark, but he saw a
shadow move.

Click-clack.

The sound was unmistakable. A pump-action shotgun being racked.

"Go away!" A voice shouted from inside. It was terrified and aggressive.
"I have a gun! I have nothing! Go away!"

"Dad!" Marcus shouted, pressing his face to the glass. "It's me! It's
Marcus!"

Silence.

Then, a beam of light hit Marcus in the face, blinding him.

"Marcus?" The voice wavered.

"Yeah, Dad. It's me. I brought friends. Open the door."

Locks tumbled. The door swung open.

Marcus's father, Frank, stood there in his bathrobe, a mossberg shotgun
in one hand and a heavy-duty flashlight in the other. He looked ten
years older than the last time Marcus had seen him.

"Jesus, kid," Frank breathed, lowering the gun. "I thought you were
looters. They've been trying the doors for an hour."

"Looters?" Sarah asked, stepping inside. "Already?"

"People are hungry," Frank said, locking the door behind them and
sliding a heavy bolt into place. "And they know I prep. Word gets around
in the suburbs faster than the internet."

He led them into the kitchen. It was lit by a single lantern.

"You guys hungry?" Frank asked. "I'm thawing out the venison from last
season."

"Starving," Ben said.

"Sit. Eat." Frank pointed to the table. "Then we need to talk."

"About what?" Marcus asked, grabbing a piece of jerky from a plate.

Frank looked at the three of them. He placed a heavy metal box on the
table.

"About the safe," Frank said. "I opened it an hour ago."

"And?" Marcus asked. "The cash? The silver?"

Frank opened the lid.

Inside were stacks of hundred-dollar bills. And tubes of silver eagles.

"It's all there," Ben said, relieved.

"Yeah," Frank said. He picked up a stack of bills and tossed it onto the
table. It landed with a dull thud. "And I just tried to trade a stack of
this to Miller down the street for a generator."

"What did he say?" Marcus asked.

Frank picked up a silver coin and flipped it in the air.

"He said he'd take the silver," Frank said. "But he told me to use the
paper to start a fire."

Frank looked at Marcus.

"The paper is dead, son. Miller knows it. I know it. And soon, everyone
out there will know it. We're back to hard assets. Silver, lead, and
blood."

He pushed the box toward Marcus.

"Take the silver. Leave the paper. And grab a shovel. We have potatoes
to plant."

Marcus looked at the useless stack of money. The faces of Franklin
stared up at him, green and mute.

"Zero," Marcus whispered. "He didn't just reset the debt. He reset the
clock."
